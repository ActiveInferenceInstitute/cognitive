# Global Solutions

## Overview

Global problems, such as climate change, loss of biodiversity, resource depletion, pandemics and weak international coordination, share three features: they cross scales from local to planetary, they involve many actors with conflicting incentives, and the underlying systems are only partly understood. Research on global solutions studies how to analyse these systems and how to organise collective action. It combines natural-science assessment, economics, political science and systems analysis. This page is an orientation and not a policy position.

## Main frameworks

- **Planetary boundaries.** Rockström and colleagues (2009) proposed quantitative limits on Earth-system processes, such as climate and nitrogen cycling, within which conditions have remained comparatively stable. The specific thresholds are debated, and the framework has been revised since.
- **Governance of commons.** Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) documented institutional arrangements under which communities manage shared resources without either privatisation or central control, and set out design principles that recur in successful cases.
- **Social-ecological systems and resilience.** Analysis of coupled human and natural systems, adaptive management and thresholds of change.
- **Design-oriented approaches.** Buckminster Fuller's design science and World Game, an early attempt to treat global resources as one planning problem (see [[knowledge_base/research/design_science]]).

## Connection to Active Inference

The relation is an active modelling programme, with a limited track record on global problems. The framework offers a language for nested, multi-scale systems: individuals, communities and institutions can each be modelled as agents whose generative models include the others, and coordination can be described as the alignment of beliefs and preferences. In principle this gives a way to represent how information, trust and incentives affect collective action. Adaptive management of uncertain systems corresponds to an agent that balances pursuing goals with reducing uncertainty about the system, the epistemic term of expected free energy.

These are modelling languages. They do not identify what should be done about emissions or resource allocation, which depends on empirical models of the physical system and on value judgements about distribution, and they have so far produced mostly small-scale or illustrative applications. Political obstacles such as free-riding and unequal power are not removed by a better description of belief updating.
