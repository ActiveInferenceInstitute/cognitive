# Economic Policy

## Overview

Economic policy is the use of taxation, public spending, monetary operations, regulation and institutional design to influence economic outcomes such as employment, inflation, growth and distribution. Policy analysis combines models of how households, firms and markets behave with evidence about how interventions have worked. It is decision-making under deep uncertainty: the system is non-stationary, agents respond to the policy, and experiments are rarely possible.

## Main questions and tools

- **Macroeconomic models.** Dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models, vector autoregressions, and agent-based models that represent heterogeneous agents explicitly.
- **Expectations.** The rational expectations revolution (Muth, 1961) and the Lucas critique (1976) established that behaviour depends on beliefs about policy, so models estimated under one regime may fail under another. This puts beliefs at the centre of policy analysis.
- **Bounded rationality.** Herbert Simon's argument that agents satisfice under limited information and computation, developed in behavioural economics.
- **Institutions and regulation.** Why rules matter, how they are enforced, and how incentives change behaviour.
- **Evidence.** Natural experiments, randomised trials, and structural estimation.

## Connection to Active Inference

The connection is exploratory, with limited application to real policy problems. The framework offers a language that sits between rational expectations and bounded rationality: agents hold beliefs about the economy, update them from noisy signals, and act to bring about preferred outcomes, with approximation limits built in through the choice of generative model. It treats expectations and information-seeking within one objective, and models of institutions as shared generative models align with accounts that regard institutions as shared expectations.

Friston and colleagues' dynamic causal modelling of the COVID-19 pandemic (2020) is an example of the tools from this literature being used in a policy-relevant setting, in epidemiology and not economics, to compare intervention scenarios. Applications to macroeconomic policy proper are mostly conceptual. No published result shows an Active Inference model outperforming standard macroeconomic or agent-based models at forecasting or policy evaluation, and readers should treat claims of that kind as unestablished.
