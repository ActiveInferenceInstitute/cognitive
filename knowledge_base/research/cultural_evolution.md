# Cultural Evolution

## Overview

Cultural evolution is the study of how socially transmitted information, such as skills, beliefs, norms and technologies, changes over time in populations. It treats culture as a second inheritance system alongside genes, with its own mechanisms of variation, transmission and selection. The field was formalised in the 1980s by Cavalli-Sforza and Feldman (*Cultural Transmission and Evolution*, 1981) and by Boyd and Richerson (*Culture and the Evolutionary Process*, 1985). Joseph Henrich's *The Secret of Our Success* (2016) is a widely read synthesis. Richard Dawkins's "meme" (1976) popularised the analogy to genes, though "memetics" as a research programme has been criticised for borrowing the gene concept too literally.

## Mechanisms and questions

- **Transmission biases.** People do not copy at random. Models and experiments examine conformist bias (copy the majority), prestige bias (copy successful individuals) and content bias (copy what is memorable or useful).
- **Cumulative culture.** How techniques improve across generations, and what population size and social structure are needed to maintain complex skills and prevent loss.
- **Gene-culture coevolution.** Cases where cultural practice alters selection on genes, with lactase persistence in dairying populations a standard example.
- **Cultural group selection.** Whether competition between groups shapes norms. This is one of the more contested claims in the field.
- **Methods.** Mathematical population models, laboratory transmission-chain experiments, agent-based simulation, and phylogenetic analyses of languages, artefacts and traditions.

## Connection to Active Inference

The connection is an active research programme, and it is a reinterpretation of an already mature field; cultural evolution theory does not depend on it. The central proposal, developed by Veissière, Constant, Ramstead, Friston and Kirmayer (2020) in "Thinking through other minds", is that individuals acquire shared expectations by inferring what others expect, and that cultural practices are patterns of mutual prediction that make behaviour mutually predictable. Cultural learning then becomes the acquisition of a shared generative model. On this reading, conformity and imitation are ways of reducing uncertainty about what others will do, and norms act as strong shared priors.

A companion idea is niche construction. Constant and colleagues (2018) gave a variational account in which organisms modify their environment so that it becomes a better source of predictions, and the modified environment shapes later learners. This echoes the gene-culture and niche-construction literature but restates it in the free energy framework.

What this adds is open. The mapping reproduces familiar findings such as conformity and norm persistence in qualitative terms. Quantitative predictions that differ from existing transmission-bias models, and tests against historical or experimental data, are still to be provided, and the "collective free energy minimisation" language remains a metaphor unless a specific model is stated.
