# Biological Intelligence

## Overview

Biological intelligence is taken here to mean the adaptive, problem-solving capacities of living systems, from single cells to nervous systems. Nervous systems are the best-studied case. A broader programme, sometimes called basal cognition, asks whether cells, tissues and organisms without neurons also process information and pursue goals, for example in bacterial chemotaxis, in slime mould foraging, and in the coordinated cell behaviour that builds and repairs bodies. Whether "intelligence" is the right word for these capacities is itself a matter of definition and dispute.

## Main questions

- **Neural computation.** How circuits transform sensory signals into action, and what level of description (spikes, populations, networks) is explanatory.
- **Homeostasis and allostasis.** How organisms regulate internal variables such as temperature and glucose, and anticipate demands before they arise.
- **Cellular and tissue intelligence.** How single cells sense gradients and make decisions, and how cell collectives achieve target shapes in development and regeneration (morphogenesis).
- **Evolution and development.** How these capacities arise and are tuned over time.

## Connection to Active Inference

The relation varies in strength with the system. For brains it is a developed research programme with extensive modelling, though contested (see [[knowledge_base/research/brain_theory]]). For homeostasis it is a natural fit: a regulator that keeps variables near preferred set-points can be described as having prior preferences over interoceptive states and acting to realise them, which is consistent with the classical Conant-Ashby result that good regulators contain a model of the system they regulate. Allostasis, regulation by prediction, fits especially well.

At the cell and tissue levels the connection is a theoretical proposal. Friston's "Life as we know it" (2013) argued that the formal structure of a self-maintaining system with a boundary entails inference-like dynamics, and others, including Pezzulo and Levin, have applied the idea to morphogenesis as collective inference about a target form. These suggestions are intriguing, and so far they are mainly demonstrated in simulation. Experimental tests that distinguish them from conventional mechanistic accounts of pattern formation are needed. A fair statement is that the framework supplies a unifying description and a set of hypotheses, and that evidence for it below the level of neural systems is thin.
