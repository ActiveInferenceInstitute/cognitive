# Cost-Benefit Analysis

## Overview
Cost-benefit analysis is a method for evaluating an option by listing the costs and benefits it produces and comparing them, usually in a common unit. In public policy and project appraisal the unit is money, and the analysis asks whether total benefits exceed total costs, or which of several options gives the greatest net benefit (Boardman et al., 2018). In biology and cognitive science the term is used more loosely for any weighing of the fitness or utility consequences of acting.

## Procedure in policy appraisal
The standard steps are to define the alternatives and the population whose welfare counts, identify impacts, quantify them, convert them to monetary values, discount future amounts to present values, and compute a net present value or benefit-cost ratio. Sensitivity analysis tests how conclusions change with uncertain inputs. The valuation of non-market goods, such as health or environmental quality, relies on stated- or revealed-preference methods whose results are debated. The choice of discount rate can decide the outcome for long-lived projects. Critics also note that aggregate net benefit ignores who gains and who loses, and that some values resist monetisation. Arrow et al. (1996) argued that the method is useful for informing decisions in regulation but should not be the only criterion.

## Cooperation
In evolutionary biology, cost-benefit reasoning organises explanations of social behaviour. Hamilton (1964) showed that a costly helping act can be favoured when $rb > c$, where $c$ is the fitness cost to the actor, $b$ the benefit to the recipient and $r$ the relatedness between them. Trivers (1971) argued that reciprocal exchange can be favoured when benefits to recipients exceed costs to donors and the same partners interact repeatedly. These models treat cost and benefit as effects on reproductive success, which may differ from what individuals experience. See [[cooperation]] and [[evolutionary_game_theory]].

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is partial. A cost-benefit comparison resembles the trade-off between preferred outcomes and uncertainty in expected free energy (see [[policy_selection]]), and in both cases an option is chosen by a scalar score over its predicted consequences. Active Inference differs in placing the preferences inside a generative model and including an epistemic term that standard cost-benefit analysis lacks. The two should not be conflated.
