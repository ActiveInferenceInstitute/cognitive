# Preregistration

## Overview
Preregistration is the practice of specifying a study's research questions, hypotheses, design, measures and planned analyses, and depositing them in a time-stamped, read-only record before data are collected or analysed. The record lets readers distinguish confirmatory analyses, which were planned, from exploratory ones, which were decided after seeing data. Nosek et al. (2018) present it as a way of separating prediction from postdiction. It also fixes the operational definitions of the variables in advance, which is the connection with [[operationalism]].

## Rationale
Analysis of a data set involves many choices: which observations to exclude, which outcomes to report, which covariates to include, when to stop collecting data. If these choices are made after seeing results, the nominal error rate of significance tests no longer applies. Simmons et al. (2011) showed by simulation and experiment that flexibility of this kind can make false-positive findings common. Related problems are selective reporting of outcomes and hypothesising after results are known. Preregistration is meant to reduce them by making the plan visible and committing the researcher to it, and so to strengthen the severity of tests in the sense used in discussions of [[falsification]].

## Forms and practice
- **Registries.** Clinical trials have been registered in public registries for many years, and registries now also exist for other fields (Nosek et al., 2018).
- **Registered reports.** A publishing format, adopted by the journal *Cortex* in 2013, in which a journal reviews and provisionally accepts a protocol before results exist, so that publication does not depend on the outcome (Chambers, 2013).
- **Deviations.** Plans often need to change; the usual expectation is to report deviations and their reasons openly.

## Limits and debate
Preregistration does not guarantee that a study is well designed, and a vague plan allows as much flexibility as none. Critics argue that it fits poorly with exploratory and theory-building work, may be a burden, and addresses a symptom, while weak theory and measurement are the root problems. Evidence for its effect on the reliability of published research is still accumulating. Proponents regard it as one tool within [[open_science]], which complements rather than replaces good methods.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no formal link. A loose parallel is that preregistration states a prior over analyses before seeing data, but it is a methodological commitment rather than a Bayesian procedure, and Bayesian analyses can be preregistered as well.
