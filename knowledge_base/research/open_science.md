# Open Science

## Overview
Open science is a set of practices intended to make scientific work more transparent, accessible and verifiable. It includes open access to publications, open data, shared code and materials, open peer review, preregistration of plans and the use of public repositories. The aims are to let others check and build on results, to widen participation, and to reduce waste. In the vault's discussion of operationalism, the relevant element is the sharing of operational details so that procedures can be repeated.

## Practices
- **Open access.** Free availability of articles, by journals or through repositories.
- **Open data and materials.** Depositing datasets, stimuli and protocols, ideally following the FAIR principles of findability, accessibility, interoperability and reusability (Wilkinson et al., 2016).
- **Open code.** Sharing analysis scripts and software with version control, supporting [[reproducible_research]].
- **Preregistration and registered reports.** Specifying hypotheses and analysis plans before data are seen (see [[preregistration]]).
- **Transparency standards.** Nosek et al. (2015) proposed standards for journals covering citation, data, code, materials, design and analysis, preregistration and replication.

## Motivation and critique
Concern about the reliability of published results gave the movement much of its momentum. The Open Science Collaboration (2015) attempted to replicate a sample of published psychological studies and reported that replication effects were generally smaller than the originals, though how to interpret replication is itself debated. Proponents claim that openness improves error detection and trust. Critics point to costs of curation, risks to participant privacy, uneven benefits across fields and career stages, and cases where data cannot be shared. Openness is also not sufficient for validity, since open but poorly designed studies remain uninformative.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no formal link. Open science is a methodological programme. The Active Inference community uses open software and shared models, and the vault itself follows open practices, but the framework neither predicts nor requires them. A loose connection can be made to epistemic communities as shared inference systems (see [[social_epistemology]]), which is an interpretation.
