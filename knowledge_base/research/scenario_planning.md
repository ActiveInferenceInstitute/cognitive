# Scenario Planning

## Overview
Scenario planning is a method for thinking about the future when it cannot be reliably predicted. Rather than forecasting a single most likely outcome, participants construct several internally consistent, plausible accounts of how the environment might develop, and then examine how decisions or strategies would fare in each. Scenarios are not predictions or preferences; they are tools for testing assumptions and widening the range of conditions that decision makers have considered.

## History and practice
Early work on scenarios in the 1950s and 1960s is associated with Herman Kahn and colleagues, who used them in military and policy analysis (Kahn & Wiener, 1967). Pierre Wack developed the approach in corporate planning at Royal Dutch/Shell, where it was used to prepare managers for discontinuities in the energy market (Wack, 1985). Later guides include Schwartz (1991) and van der Heijden (1996), who describes scenarios as a basis for strategic conversation within an organisation.

A common sequence is to define the focal question and time horizon, identify driving forces, rank them by importance and uncertainty, pick the two or three most critical uncertainties as axes, develop a small number of narratives from their combinations, draw out implications, and select indicators to monitor. Variants use quantitative models, cross-impact analysis or participatory workshops.

## Strengths and limits
The method helps reveal hidden assumptions, builds shared understanding, and highlights options that are robust across futures. Limitations include dependence on the imagination and biases of participants, the risk that vivid narratives are read as forecasts, and the lack of agreed ways to test whether scenario exercises lead to better outcomes. It differs from probabilistic forecasting and decision analysis because it does not assign probabilities to scenarios, although the methods can be combined (see [[decision_analysis]], [[uncertainty_quantification]]).

## Relation to Active Inference and complex systems
The vault links the topic to emergence and self-organisation because systems with many interacting parts produce developments that are hard to predict, which motivates scenario thinking over single forecasts (see [[complex_adaptive_systems]], [[critical_transitions]]). The connection is thematic. In Active Inference terms, scenarios resemble the generative exploration of possible futures during planning, but this is an analogy and scenario planning is a human organisational practice with no formal basis in the framework.
