# Robot Control

## Overview

Robot control is the problem of making a physical body follow desired trajectories or achieve goals despite sensor noise, actuator limits, and an uncertain environment. Standard tools include PID and computed-torque control, inverse kinematics, impedance control for contact tasks, and model predictive control. These methods are mature and dominate deployed systems. Learning-based methods, especially reinforcement learning, are used where models are hard to write down.

## Active Inference formulation

In the continuous-state formulation, a robot holds a generative model of how its sensors depend on its body state and the world, together with beliefs about how those states change. Perception is inference about the body state. Action is generated by sending the controllers predictions of the desired proprioceptive input, and reflex-like loops reduce the error between prediction and sensation. Adams, Shipp and Friston (2013) described this "predictions, not commands" view for the motor system. The control signal is derived from a prediction error, not computed from an explicit cost function; goals appear as prior beliefs about future states.

Several specific results support the approach in limited settings:

- Pio-Lopez, Nizard, Friston and Pezzulo (2016) implemented Active Inference for reaching on a simulated robot arm.
- Baltieri and Buckley (2019) showed that a PID controller can be interpreted as an Active Inference process under a linear generative model, which ties the framework to existing control practice.
- Oliver, Lanillos and Cheng (2022) tested the approach on a humanoid robot.

Related themes include body schema learning, where the robot infers its own kinematics from sensory input, and adaptive manipulation, where the model is updated online.

## Status and limits

This is an active research programme with demonstrations in simulation and on laboratory robots. The framework has not displaced conventional controllers in industry. Reasons include the need for an accurate generative model of the dynamics, the computational cost of inferring over continuous states and policies, and sensitivity to the precision settings that determine how strongly sensory errors drive behaviour. Where it reduces to a known controller, as in the PID case, it offers an interpretation more than a performance gain. Claims of broader advantages, such as better exploration or robustness to sensor failure, need direct comparison with strong baselines.
