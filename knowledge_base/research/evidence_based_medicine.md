# Evidence-Based Medicine

## Overview
Evidence-based medicine (EBM) is an approach to clinical practice in which decisions about patient care draw on systematically gathered research evidence rather than on authority, tradition or mechanistic reasoning alone. The Evidence-Based Medicine Working Group (1992) presented it as a new approach to teaching and practising medicine. Sackett et al. (1996) defined it as the conscientious, explicit and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients, and stressed that practising it means integrating individual clinical expertise with the best available external evidence.

## Method
EBM is commonly described as a cycle: formulate an answerable clinical question, search for evidence, appraise its validity and importance, apply it to the patient at hand, and evaluate the outcome. Evidence is ranked by susceptibility to bias, with systematic reviews of randomised trials and well-conducted randomised controlled trials ranked above cohort and case-control studies, case series and expert opinion. Randomisation and blinding aim to remove confounding and observer effects. Tools such as grading systems rate the certainty of a body of evidence, with attention to risk of bias, consistency, precision and applicability.

## Criticisms
Greenhalgh et al. (2014) argued that EBM had been distorted in ways that included an overreliance on hierarchies, the volume of guidelines, commercial influence on trials and neglect of the individual patient, and called for a return to its original emphasis on judgement. Further philosophical objections point out that average effects in trial populations do not translate directly to individuals, that mechanistic evidence is undervalued, and that a rigid hierarchy neglects questions that trials cannot answer. Defenders reply that these issues call for better application rather than abandonment. The relative weight of evidence types is still debated in the philosophy of medicine (see [[philosophy_of_medicine]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is epistemological. Combining new trial results with prior knowledge is naturally expressed as Bayesian updating (see [[bayesian_inference]]), and some statisticians have argued for Bayesian methods in trial design and interpretation. This is an area of methodological debate and not part of the Active Inference framework. A computational psychiatry literature applies Active Inference to clinical conditions, but it is separate from EBM as a practice.
