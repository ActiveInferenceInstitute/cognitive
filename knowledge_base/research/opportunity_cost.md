# Opportunity Cost

## Overview
The opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the best alternative that is given up by making it. The idea is usually traced to the Austrian school of economics and to Friedrich von Wieser in particular, and it is treated in economics as the proper measure of cost: the true cost of using a resource is what it could have produced elsewhere, not only the money spent. Buchanan (1969) developed the view that cost is subjective and attaches to the chooser, being the value of the alternative forgone at the moment of choice. Because resources such as time, attention and money are limited, every allocation carries one.

## Formulation
If a decision maker can choose among options with values $v_1,\dots,v_n$ and selects option $i$, the opportunity cost is $\max_{j\ne i} v_j$, the value of the next best option. Accounting costs may differ from opportunity costs, since a cost that has already been sunk and cannot be recovered does not belong in a forward-looking calculation, whereas an unpaid use of an owned resource may. The concept is built into [[cost_benefit_analysis]] and into comparisons of options in [[decision_theory]].

## Cognitive and behavioural aspects
- **Neglect.** Frederick et al. (2009) found that people often fail to consider what else their money could be used for unless alternatives are made salient, a pattern they termed opportunity cost neglect.
- **Foraging.** In optimal foraging theory, the marginal value theorem says that an animal should leave a resource patch when its current intake rate falls to the average rate for the environment (Charnov, 1976). Here the average rate represents the opportunity cost of staying.
- **Mental effort.** Kurzban et al. (2013) proposed that the felt cost of effort reflects the opportunity cost of engaging in a task, because cognitive resources devoted to one task cannot be used for others. This is a contested hypothesis, and other accounts attribute effort to limited capacity or to metabolic cost.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is conceptual. In expected free energy, committing to one policy forgoes the value of competing policies, and the balance of exploitation and exploration echoes the foraging case (see [[exploration_exploitation]]). Opportunity cost is not a named quantity in the formalism, and any mapping to it is an interpretation.
