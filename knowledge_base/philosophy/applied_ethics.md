# Applied Ethics

## Overview
Applied ethics examines specific, often contested, moral problems that arise in professional and public life, such as the treatment of patients, the use of animals, obligations to future generations and the design of technologies. It draws on [[normative_ethics]] for principles and on [[meta_ethics]] for questions about the status of moral claims, but it also depends on empirical knowledge about the practice in question.

## Main fields
- **Bioethics and medical ethics.** Informed consent, end-of-life decisions, resource allocation and research on human participants. Beauchamp and Childress (2019) presented a widely used framework built on four principles: respect for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice.
- **Research ethics.** Standards for human and animal research, institutional review and data governance, developed in response to documented abuses and codified in documents such as the Belmont Report.
- **Environmental ethics.** The moral standing of nonhuman animals, species and ecosystems, and duties regarding climate and resources (see [[environmental_ethics]]).
- **Business and professional ethics.** Obligations of firms and practitioners to clients, employees and the public.
- **Ethics of technology and AI.** Questions of fairness, privacy, accountability and safety in the design and deployment of technical systems (see [[ai_ethics]], [[technology_ethics]]).

## Methods
Approaches differ on how theory should relate to cases. Top-down methods apply a theory or a set of principles to a case, as in principlism. Bottom-up methods, such as casuistry, reason from paradigm cases by analogy. Many writers use reflective equilibrium, adjusting principles and case judgments against each other. Singer (1993) is a well-known example of a systematic consequentialist approach to practical questions such as animal welfare and global poverty, while others criticise such approaches as overly abstract.

## Relation to Active Inference
Applied ethics is relevant to the framework as a field in which it is used, not as a source of its content: research on agents, simulations of social behaviour and AI systems built on Active Inference raise the same questions of consent, accountability and safety as other computational research. The framework itself does not provide ethical principles.
