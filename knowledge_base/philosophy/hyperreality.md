# Hyperreality

## Overview
Hyperreality is a concept associated with the French theorist Jean Baudrillard (1929-2007), set out in works such as *Symbolic Exchange and Death* (1976) and *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981; English translation 1994). It describes a situation in which the distinction between a reality and its representation collapses, because models, images and codes no longer copy an original but generate the "real" that people experience. According to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Baudrillard held that the realm of the hyperreal, which includes media simulations of reality, Disneyland and amusement parks, malls and consumer fantasylands, television sport, virtual-reality games and social networking sites, is "more real than real", and that its models, images and codes come to control thought and behaviour.

## Context in Baudrillard's account
- **From production to simulation.** Baudrillard argued that modern societies are organised around the production and consumption of commodities, whereas postmodern societies are organised around simulation and the play of images and signs. Political economy ceases to be the foundation for explanation, and the proliferation of signs and codes takes its place.
- **Simulacra.** A simulacrum is a copy that has no original or that replaces its original. In a society of simulation, identities are constructed by appropriating images, and codes and models determine how people see themselves and relate to one another.
- **Implosion.** Distinctions between realms such as economics, politics, culture and sexuality, and between classes and genders, lose their power and collapse into one another.
- **Experience.** People are described as fleeing the "desert of the real" for the more intense experiences offered by entertainment, information and communication technologies, with subjectivity fragmented as a result.

## Criticism
Baudrillard's account has been criticised as a form of technological determinism and as a rejection of political economy as an explanatory principle. His essays on the Gulf War were also met with the objection that the war did take place, even if its media presentation was highly mediated. The concept is better treated as a diagnostic image of media culture than as an empirical hypothesis.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an analogy and should not be overstated. In predictive-processing terms, an agent's experience is always a model-based construction answering to sensory evidence, so what is perceived is never raw reality, and a world heavily shaped by media can supply evidence that mainly confirms a model's own expectations. This differs from Baudrillard's claim, which concerns signs and social order and not neural inference. Discussions of simulated worlds and virtual reality often involve both ideas (see [[simulation_theory]]).
