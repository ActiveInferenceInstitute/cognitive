# Neuroscientific Ethics

## Overview
Neuroscientific ethics, usually called neuroethics, covers two linked areas. Roskies (2002) distinguished the ethics of neuroscience, which studies the moral questions raised by research and technology applied to the brain, from the neuroscience of ethics, which studies how the brain makes moral judgements and what that implies for ethical theory. Farah (2005) described the field as combining practical questions with philosophical ones about the mind.

## The ethics of neuroscience
- **Cognitive enhancement**: the use of drugs or devices to improve memory, attention or mood in healthy people raises questions about fairness, coercion, safety and what counts as treatment rather than enhancement.
- **Mental privacy**: neuroimaging and decoding methods may reveal information about thoughts, preferences or health, prompting debate over protection of mental information.
- **Consent and capacity**: research and treatment involving patients with disorders of consciousness, dementia or psychiatric illness requires care about decision-making capacity (see [[patient_autonomy]]).
- **Incidental findings**: scans performed for research can reveal unexpected abnormalities, raising duties about disclosure.
- **Brain stimulation**: interventions such as deep brain stimulation raise questions about identity, authenticity and responsibility for altered behaviour.

## The neuroscience of ethics
Greene and colleagues (2001) used functional imaging to compare responses to moral dilemmas and proposed that more emotionally engaging personal dilemmas recruit different brain regions from impersonal ones, which they linked to a dual-process account of moral judgement. Berker (2009) criticised inferences from such findings to normative conclusions, arguing that the empirical results are weaker than claimed and that the step from how judgements arise to which are justified requires independent normative argument. The relevance of descriptive findings to ethical theory remains disputed.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference models of decision-making, affect and agency are used in computational neuroscience, and may bear on how moral choices are generated. No established result connects the framework to normative ethics, and a descriptive model of choice does not by itself settle what choices are right.
