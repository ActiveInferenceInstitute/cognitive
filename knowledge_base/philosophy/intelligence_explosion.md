# Intelligence Explosion

## Overview
The intelligence explosion is a hypothesis that once artificial systems reach a level of general intelligence at which they can improve their own design, each improvement could make the next one faster, producing a rapid rise to far-greater-than-human capability. I. J. Good (1965) gave the classic statement, defining an ultraintelligent machine as one that surpasses all the intellectual activities of any human, and noting that since designing machines is such an activity, such a machine could design still better machines. He called the resulting change an intelligence explosion and the first such machine the last invention humanity need make, provided it were docile enough to be controlled.

## The argument
Chalmers (2010) reconstructed the case in a form that can be examined. The premises are that artificial general intelligence (AI) will be created, that an AI can lead to AI+ of greater capability, and that this step can repeat, yielding AI++ if the amplification is proportional. The inference depends on a proportionality thesis: that increases in intelligence produce at least proportional increases in the ability to design further increases. Bostrom (2014) discusses possible dynamics, distinguishing slow, moderate and fast take-offs and asking whether hardware, software or collective effects would dominate.

## Objections
There is no empirical evidence of an intelligence explosion, and the hypothesis is speculative. Critics argue that returns to cognitive improvement may diminish, that progress is limited by data, experiment time and physical resources rather than by raw intelligence alone, and that intelligence is not a single scalar quantity that can be recursively amplified. Others note that human institutions have improved collectively without runaway growth. Chalmers (2010) discusses obstacles of this kind, including diminishing returns, as ways the explosion argument could fail. Defenders reply that these limits may not bind for machines, and that even a moderate chance of rapid take-off justifies attention to safety research.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no derivation connecting the two. Active Inference describes agents that minimise free energy through perception and action, and does not predict self-amplifying growth. Some work uses it to model artificial agents that learn and plan, which bears on how capable agents might be designed and constrained, but claims about take-off speed are outside its scope.
