# Postmodernism

## Overview
Postmodernism is an umbrella term for intellectual currents in philosophy, literary theory, architecture and the social sciences, mostly from the 1960s onward, that question the universal claims of modernity. Common themes are suspicion of totalising theories, scepticism about objective and neutral foundations for knowledge, attention to language, power and context, and the view that identities and meanings are historically constructed. The label is contested, and several thinkers associated with it rejected it.

## Main figures and ideas
- **Lyotard.** In The Postmodern Condition (1979), he characterised the postmodern as incredulity toward metanarratives, the grand stories (progress, emancipation, scientific totality) that legitimated modern knowledge.
- **Foucault.** His analyses of discourse, institutions and power-knowledge treat what counts as knowledge as bound to historically specific regimes of practice.
- **Derrida and Baudrillard.** Derrida's [[deconstruction]] questions stable meaning in texts; Baudrillard argued that signs and images increasingly stand in for reality.
- **Jameson.** He read postmodernism as the cultural logic of late capitalism, offering a Marxist critique rather than an endorsement.

## Criticisms
Critics argue that strong versions slide into [[relativism]] and are self-undermining, since the thesis that no claim is objectively true seems to claim objective truth. Scientists and analytic philosophers have also faulted the style as obscure. Defenders respond that the core point is modest: knowledge claims are situated and subject to critique, a position shared with parts of [[constructivism]] and [[social_epistemology]].

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct technical link. Active Inference is a formal, realist-leaning framework in which generative models are answerable to sensory evidence, which sits uneasily with strong postmodern relativism. A loose parallel exists in the shared emphasis that perception is model-laden and that priors differ between agents, but this is an analogy, not a connection that either literature develops.

## Related concepts
- [[deconstruction]] — Derrida's method of reading, central to the movement
- [[relativism]] — the epistemic position most often attributed to it
- [[constructivism]] — the claim that knowledge and reality are constructed
- [[social_epistemology]] — study of knowledge as a social product
- [[pragmatism]] — a different anti-foundationalist tradition
