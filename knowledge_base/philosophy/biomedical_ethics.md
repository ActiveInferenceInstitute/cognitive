# Biomedical Ethics

## Overview
Biomedical ethics, often called bioethics, is the study of moral questions raised by medicine, clinical practice, health policy and biological research. Topics include informed consent, confidentiality, end-of-life decisions, research on human participants, allocation of scarce resources, reproductive technologies and genetic information. Positions on specific questions differ widely, and this page describes frameworks without taking a side on disputed cases.

## Frameworks
- **Principlism.** Beauchamp and Childress (*Principles of Biomedical Ethics*, first published 1979) propose four prima facie principles: respect for autonomy, nonmaleficence, beneficence and justice. These are specified and balanced in context and are intended to be acceptable from several moral theories. Critics argue that the principles are vague, give no method for resolving conflicts, or neglect other values.
- **Consequentialist and deontological approaches.** Utilitarian reasoning weighs outcomes across patients and populations, while deontological approaches stress duties, rights and constraints such as consent (see [[normative_ethics]]).
- **Virtue and care approaches.** These emphasise the character of clinicians and the relationships involved in caring (see [[care_ethics]]).
- **Casuistry.** Jonsen and Toulmin's casuistry reasons from paradigm cases instead of general principles.

Institutional history shaped the field. The Nuremberg Code (1947), the Declaration of Helsinki (first adopted by the World Medical Association in 1964) and the Belmont Report (1979) set standards for research on human participants, centred on voluntary consent and risk assessment.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct relation. Indirect connections arise where computational models enter clinical practice: computational psychiatry uses generative models to describe conditions and treatment response (see [[computational_psychiatry]]), which raises ethical questions about diagnosis, decision-making capacity and the use of such models in care. Those questions belong to biomedical ethics and are not resolved by the modelling framework.
