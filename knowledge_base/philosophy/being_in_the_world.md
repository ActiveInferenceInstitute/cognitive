# Being-in-the-World

## Overview
Being-in-the-world (*In-der-Welt-sein*) is Martin Heidegger's term in *Being and Time* (1927) for the basic structure of human existence, which he calls [[dasein]]. On this view a person is not primarily a detached subject confronting a world of objects but is always already involved in a web of practices, purposes and equipment that make things matter. The hyphenated expression is meant to show that the self and the world are not two separate items later connected.

## Main ideas
- **Practical engagement.** Heidegger holds that things are first encountered as equipment that is "ready-to-hand" (*zuhanden*), disclosed through use, as when a hammer is used for hammering without being observed. Detached observation, in which an item is "present-at-hand" (*vorhanden*), is a derivative mode, typically arising when something breaks down.
- **Worldhood.** A world is a referential totality of purposes and significance, not a collection of objects.
- **Being-with and care.** Existence is essentially shared with others (*Mitsein*) and structured by care (*Sorge*).
- **Mood and understanding.** Humans are attuned to the world through moods and understanding, prior to explicit judgment.

Dreyfus (1991) gave an influential commentary on the first division of the work and argued that it undermines representational theories of mind, a reading that other scholars dispute. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on Heidegger (Wrathall) gives a general overview.

## Relation to Active Inference
The idea has influenced embodied, enactive and ecological approaches (see [[embodied_cognition]], [[enactive_cognition]], [[ecological_psychology]]). Some authors have sought to reconcile it with Active Inference, noting that an agent in this framework is coupled with its environment through perception and action and acts to maintain itself within expected states, a pattern they compare with skilled engagement. Others argue that the framework's internal generative model still depends on representational vocabulary that Heideggerian thought rejects. The relation is an area of debate and interpretation, not a derivation.
