# Mental Causation

## Overview
Mental causation is the question of whether and how mental events, such as wanting a drink or believing it will rain, cause physical events such as reaching for a glass or taking an umbrella. Common sense says they do, and psychology explains behaviour in these terms. The difficulty is to reconcile this with the widely held view that every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause.

## The problem and responses
- **Interaction.** Descartes' substance dualism faced Princess Elisabeth's objection that an unextended mind could not move an extended body (see [[dualism]]).
- **Causal closure and exclusion.** Given that physical events have sufficient physical causes, Kim (1998) argued that a distinct mental cause is either identical to a physical cause or is redundant, and that nonreductive views therefore cannot give mental properties real causal work.
- **Epiphenomenalism.** Mental states are effects of brain activity without effects of their own. Most philosophers regard this as hard to accept given how reasons appear to explain action.
- **Reductive identity.** If mental properties are identical to physical ones, they inherit causal power (see [[materialism]]), at the cost of facing the multiple realisability objection.
- **Non-reductive physicalism.** Davidson (1980) proposed anomalous monism, holding that each mental event is a physical event but that mental descriptions are not governed by strict laws. Others argue that mental properties are causally relevant via counterfactual or difference-making accounts of causation.
- **Related questions.** Compatibility with [[free_will]], and the status of [[downward_causation]] and [[strong_emergence]].

## Relation to Active Inference
In hierarchical predictive models, higher-level beliefs constrain lower-level processing and drive action by setting predictions that motor systems fulfil, so beliefs seem to play a causal role (see [[circular_causality]]). Whether this resolves the exclusion problem is contested: the account can be read as describing physical processes at several levels of description, which fits the difference-making approach, but it does not show that those levels have independent causal power. The link is interpretive.
