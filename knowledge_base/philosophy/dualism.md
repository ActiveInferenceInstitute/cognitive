# Dualism

## Overview
Dualism in the philosophy of mind is the claim that the mental and the physical are two fundamentally different kinds of thing or property. It is usually contrasted with [[materialism]], which holds that everything is physical, and with monist alternatives such as [[neutral_monism]]. The term is also used in other areas, such as moral or cosmological dualism, which are not covered here.

## Varieties
- **Substance dualism.** Descartes (1641) argued that mind (*res cogitans*) and body (*res extensa*) are distinct substances. Arguments include the conceivability of the mind existing without the body.
- **Property dualism.** There is only one kind of substance, but it has two irreducible kinds of properties, physical and mental. Contemporary versions, such as that of Chalmers (1996), rest on arguments from [[qualia]] and conscious experience.
- **Interaction views.** Interactionism says mind and body causally influence each other. Epiphenomenalism says physical events cause mental events but not the reverse. Parallelism and occasionalism deny direct interaction.

## Objections
The interaction problem, pressed by Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia in her correspondence with Descartes, asks how an unextended mind could move an extended body. A related objection appeals to the causal closure of the physical: if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, mental causation appears redundant. Parsimony and the success of neuroscience in tying mental function to brain activity are also cited against substance dualism. Defenders reply that explanatory gaps for experience are not closed by these successes (see [[explanatory_gap]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The formalism of Active Inference partitions the world into internal, external, sensory and active states separated by a Markov blanket. This is a statistical partition, defined by conditional independence, and is not a division between mind and matter; both sides are ordinary physical dynamics. The literature around the framework generally presents it as non-dualist and embodied, and critics have asked whether the blanket formalism nonetheless reintroduces an inner-outer boundary, a question about interpretation rather than about the mathematics. The relation is conceptual and contested, not a result.
