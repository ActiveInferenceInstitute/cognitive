# Kokugaku Nativism

## Overview
*Kokugaku* has been rendered "National Learning", "Native Japan Studies", "nativism" and "essentialism", and no single translation is accepted. In a broad sense it names the new scholarship on things Japanese that grew during the seventeenth century as peace, prosperity and literacy spread: studies of history, literature, philology, poetics, customs, mythology and the worship of kami, alongside the older studies of China. In a narrower sense, in the eighteenth century some of this work came to concentrate on Japanese distinctiveness, mainly in contrast with China, which was cast as an Other whose influence had been harmful. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry stresses that during the Tokugawa period (1600-1868) kokugaku was not one movement but a succession of private academies with different orientations.

## Main figures
The entry traces a line from Keichu (1640-1701), whose philological work on the eighth-century poetry anthology *Man'yoshu* and whose reverence for *waka* verse later scholars treated as pioneering, through Kada no Azumamaro, Kamo no Mabuchi, Motoori Norinaga (1730-1801) and Hirata Atsutane (1776-1843).
- **Motoori Norinaga.** He wrote a leading literary criticism of *The Tale of Genji*, produced a philological commentary on the *Kojiki* (712 CE), and used that analysis to construct what he held to be the original Way of the kami (*kami no michi*). Applying the concept of *mono no aware* (see [[mono_no_aware]]) to Genji, he argued that the tale should not be judged by Confucian or Buddhist moral standards, but read as a tale of human feeling that invites readers to share in it.
- **Hirata Atsutane.** Less philological than his forebears, he sought support for his views from the widest range of sources and styled what he promoted "True Shinto", presented as the source from which inferior foreign doctrines later derived. His teachings were popular and spread through provincial and grassroots followers, including women.

## Later history
After the Meiji Restoration of 1868, kokugaku became an ideologically charged academic field that was used to create imperial subjects at home and abroad, and it also gave rise to folklore and ethnography. After 1945 it lost its imperial and spiritual colouring. From about the 1980s similar claims about what is uniquely Japanese were grouped under the heading of *Nihonjinron*, "theories of Japaneseness", and the word kokugaku is no longer used for present-day essentialist scholarship.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct relation. A methodological comparison is possible: Norinaga's practice of reading an ancient text from within its own linguistic and emotional world resembles hermeneutic interpretation (see [[hermeneutics]]). The later political history is a useful case for studies of how a narrative about collective identity shapes a population's shared expectations, but that is a sociological reading and not a claim of the framework.
