# Neurophilosophy

## Overview
Neurophilosophy is the project of informing and constraining philosophical theories of mind by results from neuroscience, and of using philosophical analysis to clarify neuroscientific concepts. The term is closely associated with Patricia Churchland, whose 1986 book argued for a co-evolution of theories of mind and brain, so that neither philosophy nor neuroscience proceeds in isolation from the other.

## Central positions
- **Eliminative materialism.** Paul Churchland (1981) proposed that folk psychology, with its beliefs and desires, may be a false theory that a mature neuroscience will replace instead of reduce. The proposal is contested, with critics noting the explanatory success of folk-psychological prediction.
- **Reductive and co-evolutionary strategies.** Intertheoretic reduction is expected to proceed in both directions, with higher-level theories revised in light of lower-level findings.
- **Neuroscientific revisions of philosophical topics.** These include work on free will, moral cognition and the nature of consciousness.

## Criticism
Bennett and Hacker (2003) argued that many neuroscientists and philosophers commit a mereological fallacy by ascribing to brains psychological attributes that apply only to whole persons, such as believing or deciding. Others hold that neuroscience can answer only mechanistic questions and cannot settle conceptual ones, or that the explanatory gap for consciousness resists neural explanation. These disagreements concern the proper scope of the approach, and the page does not adjudicate them.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relationship is active and substantial, though contested. Predictive-processing accounts have been used as a general framework in philosophy of mind, notably by Hohwy (2013) and Clark (2016), who treat perception, action and cognition as aspects of prediction error minimisation. Philosophers debate whether this framework offers a unified theory, a useful metaphor, or a description that does not constrain cognitive mechanisms.
