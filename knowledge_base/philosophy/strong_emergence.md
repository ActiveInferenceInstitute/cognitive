# Strong Emergence

## Overview
Strong emergence is the claim that a system can have high-level properties that are not deducible, even in principle and with unlimited computing power, from complete truths about its low-level constituents and their laws. Chalmers (2006) states the idea as the failure of such deduction, and usually adds that strongly emergent phenomena would require new fundamental laws linking levels. The view contrasts with [[weak_emergence]], where the higher-level pattern is surprising but is still fixed by micro-level dynamics.

## Arguments and objections
- **Candidate cases.** Chalmers argued that consciousness is the one clear candidate, since no description of physical structure and function seems to entail what experience is like. Chemical and biological properties are generally regarded as weakly emergent or reducible.
- **Downward causation.** Strong emergence is often tied to the idea that a whole can causally influence its parts in a way not captured by the parts' own dynamics (see [[downward_causation]]). Kim (1999) pressed the exclusion objection: if every physical event already has a sufficient physical cause, a distinct higher-level cause appears to be either redundant or to violate causal closure of the physical (see [[mental_causation]]).
- **Empirical standing.** No accepted scientific case requires strong emergence, and critics regard it as a philosophical possibility rather than a demonstrated feature of nature. Defenders reply that it cannot be ruled out where the explanatory gap remains unclosed.

## Relation to Active Inference
The free energy principle does not assert strong emergence. Its Markov-blanket partition describes a system's internal and blanket states by their own dynamics at each scale, and the mathematics is consistent with a weakly emergent reading in which higher-level descriptions are coarse-grainings of lower-level ones. Debates over whether hierarchical self-models or the blanket itself play a causal role at their own level touch the same questions, but any claim that Active Inference needs strong emergence would be interpretive rather than a result.
