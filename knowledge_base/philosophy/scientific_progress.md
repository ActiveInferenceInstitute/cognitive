# Scientific Progress

## Overview
Few people doubt that science has made progress, but philosophers disagree about what the progress consists in. The question matters because it ties together methodology, the aims of science and the realism debate: an account of progress implies an account of what scientists are, or should be, trying to achieve.

## Main accounts
- **Semantic (truth-approximation).** Progress is movement toward the truth. Popper's notion of verisimilitude was an attempt to make this precise, and later work on truthlikeness continues the programme, as surveyed by Niiniluoto.
- **Epistemic.** Progress is the accumulation of knowledge. Bird (2007) argued that this, not truth-approximation, is the proper standard, because a false but widely believed theory is not progress.
- **Problem-solving.** Laudan (1977) held that progress is an increase in the problem-solving effectiveness of research traditions, which does not require that theories be true.
- **Noetic.** Progress is a gain in understanding, even where the best available models are idealised and strictly false.
- **Empirical adequacy.** Constructive empiricists such as van Fraassen locate progress in better fit to observable phenomena without commitment to the truth of claims about unobservables.

## Challenges
Kuhn's picture of revolutions and incommensurability questions whether successive paradigms can be ranked on a single scale (see [[kuhn_paradigms]]). The pessimistic meta-induction notes that many past successful theories were later rejected, suggesting that current ones may be as well, and structural realists reply that the mathematical structure is retained across changes (see [[structural_realism]]). Rejecting a theory by falsification is itself only one way for science to improve (see [[falsification]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
Model comparison gives a formal analogue of theory choice. Within Bayesian practice, competing models are scored by evidence, which balances accuracy against complexity, and Active Inference characterises curious inquiry as seeking observations with high expected information gain (see [[epistemic_value]]). Describing a scientific community as a collective inference process is an illustrative analogy, and it does not decide between the philosophical accounts above.
