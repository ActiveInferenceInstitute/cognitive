# Human Rights

## Overview
Human rights are standardly understood as rights that people hold simply by virtue of being human, regardless of nationality, status or conduct, and which protect important interests such as life, liberty, and freedom from torture. The modern international framework is anchored in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 1948, and subsequent treaties. Philosophers dispute what grounds such rights, which rights there are, and how they relate to law and politics.

## Philosophical accounts
- **Natural rights and natural law.** Early modern writers, notably Locke, held that individuals have rights prior to government, grounded in natural law. Such traditions frequently relied on theological or metaphysical premises that others do not share.
- **Personhood or agency.** Griffin (2008) grounded human rights in normative agency, the capacity to form and pursue a conception of a worthwhile life. He argued this fixes both their content and limits.
- **Interest-based and Kantian approaches.** Some accounts ground rights in fundamental human interests, and Kantian ones in human dignity and the demand that persons be treated as ends (see [[deontological_ethics]]).
- **Political or practical conceptions.** Beitz (2009) proposed treating human rights as a practice with a function, namely setting standards whose violation can justify international concern, without requiring a prior moral foundation.
- **Critical positions.** Bentham described natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts". Others argue the framework is culturally parochial, a claim that is challenged by pointing to varied cultural traditions that support related protections (see [[moral_relativism]]), while marxist and communitarian writers have criticised its individualism.

Debates also cover the extension of rights to social and economic goods, their priority when they conflict, and the role of institutions in implementing them. The Stanford Encyclopedia article (Nickel and Etinson) gives a survey.

## Relation to Active Inference
The subject has no connection with Active Inference. It belongs to normative political philosophy and law, and the framework's models of cognition and behaviour cannot settle what rights people have. Empirical work on moral cognition may inform how rights claims are understood but not whether they are justified.
