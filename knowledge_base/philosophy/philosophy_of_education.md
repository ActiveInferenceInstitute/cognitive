# Philosophy of Education

## Overview
The philosophy of education studies what education is for, what should be taught, how knowledge and character are best cultivated, and what authority teachers and institutions have. It draws on epistemology, ethics and political philosophy, and it is distinct from empirical research in [[educational_psychology]], which asks how learning occurs, though the two inform each other.

## Major positions
- **Classical and liberal traditions.** Plato and Aristotle tie education to the development of virtue and rational capacities. Peters (1966) analysed education as initiation into worthwhile forms of knowledge and understanding, and argued that it should not be reduced to training for external ends.
- **Progressivism and pragmatism.** Dewey (1916) treated education as the continuing reconstruction of experience and linked it to democratic life. He held that learners should engage in active inquiry and not passively receive facts (see [[progressive_education]], [[pragmatism]]).
- **Constructivism.** Learners build knowledge through activity, as in [[constructivist_learning_theory]], with implications for the role of the teacher as guide.
- **Critical pedagogy.** Freire (1970) criticised "banking" models in which teachers deposit information in students, and advocated dialogue and the raising of critical consciousness.

## Contested questions
Philosophers disagree about whether education should chiefly serve the individual, the community or the economy, how far it should be neutral on contested values, and how to balance traditional curricula against learner interest. There is also debate about indoctrination, the place of autonomy as an aim, and the meaning of equal educational opportunity. This page records the positions without endorsing them.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy. Active Inference treats learning as updating a generative model, and it ascribes epistemic value to actions that reduce uncertainty, a formulation applied to curiosity and insight (Friston et al., 2017). This can be compared with progressive emphases on active inquiry. It does not decide the normative questions about aims and content, which belong to philosophy of education.
