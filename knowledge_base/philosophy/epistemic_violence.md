# Epistemic Violence

## Overview
Epistemic violence is a term for wrongs that operate through knowledge and speech: the dismissal, silencing or erasure of what some people know or say, and the construction of them as objects of others' knowledge. It has two main sources in the literature. Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak introduced it in the context of colonialism in her essay "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988), and the philosopher Kristie Dotson gave it a conceptual analysis in testimonial exchange in 2011. It is related to, but distinct from, Miranda Fricker's notion of epistemic injustice (see [[epistemic_justice]]).

## Spivak
In "Can the Subaltern Speak?", published in the volume *Marxism and the Interpretation of Culture* edited by Cary Nelson and Lawrence Grossberg (1988), Spivak describes epistemic violence as part of the remotely orchestrated, far-flung and heterogeneous project of constituting the colonial subject as Other. She takes the term "subaltern" from Gramsci and credits the Subaltern Studies group for trying to recover a voice for the subaltern in India, while criticising attempts by outsiders to grant collective speech, which risk assuming cultural solidarity and relying on Western intellectuals to speak for the subaltern. She also questions the view of Foucault and Deleuze that the marginalised can speak for themselves. Her central example is the British colonial treatment of widow sacrifice, in which the debate is conducted between colonial and native male voices, so that the voice of the woman is lost. Her conclusion that the subaltern cannot speak refers to that loss of voice.

## Dotson
Dotson, in "Tracking Epistemic Violence, Tracking Practices of Silencing" (2011), offers a conceptual reading of the epistemic violence present when silencing occurs. She defines epistemic violence as the failure, owing to pernicious ignorance, of hearers to meet the vulnerabilities of speakers in linguistic exchanges. By focusing on how hearers fail to meet speaker dependency in an exchange, she argues, one can distinguish the different types of silencing that members of oppressed groups face when attempting to testify.

## Relation to epistemic justice
Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice* (2007) analyses testimonial and hermeneutical injustice as wrongs done to someone in their capacity as a knower. Dotson's account is offered as a way to distinguish different practices of silencing, which she notes are often hard to identify. For the general area see [[social_epistemology]] and [[feminist_epistemology]]; for the colonial setting see [[colonial_discourse]] and [[coloniality_of_power]].

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is loose. In a model of communication, a speaker's testimony is evidence for the hearer, and how much weight it gets depends on the hearer's priors about the speaker's reliability. Active Inference does not itself say when such weighting is unjust, and no result links it to Spivak's or Dotson's analyses, which are normative and critical.
