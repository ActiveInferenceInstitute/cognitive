# Shinto Philosophy

## Overview
Shinto, "the Way of the kami", is a Japanese religious tradition with ancient roots. In the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's survey of Japanese philosophy, Kasulis and Bouso name Shinto as the first of three major philosophical sources that have fed Japanese thought since ancient times. They distinguish between its archaic form, better termed proto-Shinto or "ancient kami worship", and Shinto as it is now understood. The archaic form had no self-conscious philosophical reflection. Institutional Shinto thought did not begin until the medieval period, and much of today's Shinto philosophy originates in the Native Japan Studies (*kokugaku*) tradition of the eighteenth century (see [[kokugaku_nativism]]).

## Features of the archaic outlook
- **Kami.** The word *kami*, often too narrowly translated "gods", names whatever manifests a power (*tama*) that inspires awe: a celestial deity, a ghost, a possessed human being, a spirit in a natural object, a wondrous natural object such as Mount Fuji, or a legendary sword.
- **No good-evil duality.** Interactions with kami could be beneficial or harmful, but there was no struggle between forces of good and evil. Wrongdoing was usually a violation of a taboo, and the proper response was not guilt or repentance but ritual purification.
- **A continuous field.** The material and spiritual are treated as internally related in a continuous field in which the human and the natural, animate and inanimate, interact and even communicate. Kasulis and Bouso express the point as the world not only being engaged but also engaging us. Reality is therefore not a set of discrete things that happen to be connected, but a field of which we are part, often expressed by the word *kokoro*.
- **Words.** The term *koto* means both word and thing, and *kotodama* ("spirit of words") suggests that words have the power to evoke, and not only to refer to, reality.
- **Creation myths.** Parts of the physical world arise from divine acts, for example the sun and the moon from the eyes of the kami Izanagi when he purified himself in a river. The authors read this as supporting the idea that every part of the world reflects the pattern of spiritual creativity at the cosmic scale.

## Interaction with other traditions
Confucianism and Buddhism entered Japan from the Asian continent and did not take an oppositional stance toward this existing outlook. The entry argues that, as a result, much of its organicism and sensitivity to inter-responsive relations survived within mainstream Japanese thought, and the authors give Japanese esoteric Buddhism as an example of assimilation, in which Shinto kami were relegated to being "surface manifestations" of Buddhist reality.

## Relation to Active Inference
No direct relation exists. The notion of a world that is not simply engaged but also engages us may be compared, as an interpretation, with the circular coupling of perception and action in the framework, in which an agent's model of its environment is shaped by what its actions elicit. Shinto is a religious tradition with ritual and social dimensions, and this comparison does not translate it into a scientific claim.
