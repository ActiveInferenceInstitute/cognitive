# Kuhn Paradigms

## Overview
Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) proposed that science does not advance by steady accumulation or by continual testing of bold conjectures. Instead, it alternates between long periods of normal science, conducted within a shared framework, and episodes of revolutionary change in which one framework replaces another.

## Key concepts
- **Paradigm.** In the first edition the term covers both exemplary problem solutions and a broader shared framework. In the postscript to the second edition (1970) Kuhn separated *exemplars* from the *disciplinary matrix* of shared symbolic generalisations, models and values. Masterman had pointed out that the original usage was ambiguous.
- **Normal science.** Puzzle-solving that applies and extends the paradigm, taking its fundamental commitments for granted.
- **Anomaly and crisis.** Persistent failures to solve puzzles accumulate, and at some point trust in the paradigm erodes.
- **Revolution.** A new paradigm replaces the old, often with changes in standards, problems and meanings.
- **Incommensurability.** Rival paradigms may lack a common measure, so that comparison by neutral observation is difficult. Kuhn later restricted the claim to local semantic incommensurability.

## Criticisms and influence
Lakatos charged that the account reduced theory choice to mob psychology, Popper also criticised it, and Lakatos offered research programmes as a more rational alternative (see [[falsification]]). Others argued that "paradigm" was used in too many senses and that revolutions are rarely as abrupt as described. Kuhn maintained that his view preserved a rational basis for choice through shared values such as accuracy, consistency and fruitfulness. The book made the dependence of observation on background theory a central theme (see [[theory_ladenness]]) and shaped later debates about [[scientific_realism]] and progress.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is a loose analogy with predictive processing, in which perception is shaped by prior expectations that are revised only when prediction errors are persistent enough to change the model. A paradigm can be compared with a high-level prior, and a revolution with large-scale model revision. The comparison is illustrative, not a derivation or established research result. Some researchers have also asked whether the free energy principle itself functions as a framework in Kuhn's sense, rather than as a testable theory.
