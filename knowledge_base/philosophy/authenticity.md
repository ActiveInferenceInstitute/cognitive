# Authenticity

## Overview
Authenticity, in philosophy, is the ideal of living and choosing in a way that is genuinely one's own, as opposed to conforming unreflectively to convention or self-deception. The idea is prominent in existentialism and phenomenology, though the term is used in different senses by different authors, and some argue the concept is either empty or morally neutral.

## Main accounts
- **Heidegger.** In *Being and Time* (1927), authenticity (*Eigentlichkeit*) names a mode of existence in which Dasein owns its possibilities instead of being absorbed in the anonymous "they" (*das Man*). It is tied to confronting one's finitude (see [[being_toward_death]] and [[dasein]]), and Heidegger insists the term is not a moral evaluation.
- **Sartre.** In *Being and Nothingness* (1943), the opposite of authenticity is bad faith, the self-deceiving denial of either one's freedom or one's [[facticity]]. Authenticity requires acknowledging both (see [[being_and_nothingness]]).
- **Taylor.** In *The Ethics of Authenticity* (1991), Taylor traced the modern ideal of being true to oneself to Rousseau and Herder, defended it against some critics of individualism, and argued that it presupposes horizons of significance beyond one's own choice.
- **Critical views.** Adorno criticised the "jargon of authenticity" as ideological. Others question whether a true or inner self exists to be expressed, and psychological work on self-concept has raised similar doubts.

Trilling (1972) and Golomb (1995) discuss the history of the concept and its relation to sincerity.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no established link between authenticity and Active Inference. A loose analogy appears in work on the self as a model: in the free energy framework an agent is described as modelling itself and its environment, so questions about the coherence of a self-model parallel questions about self-knowledge. Whether this bears on authenticity, a normative and existential concept, is speculative.
