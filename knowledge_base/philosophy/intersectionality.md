# Intersectionality

## Overview
Intersectionality is an approach to analysing social disadvantage in which categories such as race, gender and class are treated as interacting and mutually shaping, not as separate and additive. The term was introduced by the legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw in her 1989 article "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" and developed in her 1991 article on violence against women of color. It has since become a broad interdisciplinary field of research and a widely used, and widely discussed, term in political and academic debate.

## Crenshaw's argument
Crenshaw took as a starting point the observation that dominant approaches treat race and gender as mutually exclusive categories of experience and analysis. She argued that a single-axis framework in antidiscrimination law, and also in feminist theory and antiracist politics, erases Black women in the conceptualisation, identification and remediation of discrimination, because it limits attention to the experiences of otherwise-privileged members of each group. Race-discrimination cases tend to be viewed in terms of sex- and class-privileged Black people, and sex-discrimination cases in terms of race- and class-privileged women.

Her central legal illustration was the case of *DeGraffenreid v. General Motors*, in which five Black women sued the company. Crenshaw argued that the court's treatment of such claims showed the limits of a framework that could see only race or only sex. The intersectional experience, she wrote, is greater than the sum of racism and sexism, so analysis that does not take intersectionality into account cannot adequately address how Black women are subordinated. She used the image of traffic through an intersection: discrimination, like traffic, may flow in one direction or another, and a person harmed in the intersection may have been injured by either or by both.

## Developments
Crenshaw distinguished structural, political and representational dimensions of intersectionality in her 1991 article. A 2013 article by Cho, Crenshaw and McCall organises the growing field of intersectionality studies into three kinds of engagement: applying an intersectional framework, debating what intersectionality includes and how it works as theory and method, and political action that uses an intersectional lens. Collins and Bilge present it as a form of critical inquiry and practice. As the concept has moved across disciplines, it has been applied to many categories beyond race and gender and criticised and refined in ways that this page does not attempt to survey. For its place within legal scholarship see [[critical_race_theory]], which lists intersectionality and anti-essentialism among its hallmark themes.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is weak. One could say that social categories shape the priors and the evidence a person encounters, so that combined categories produce experiences that are not predictable from each category alone, but this is a loose analogy and not a result of either field. Active Inference models of social cognition do not take up intersectionality, and no empirical programme links them. The concept's relevance to epistemology is better treated through [[epistemic_justice]].
