# Transcendental Realism

## Overview
Transcendental realism is the position developed by the British philosopher Roy Bhaskar in A Realist Theory of Science (1975), and it is the foundation of critical realism. It proceeds by a transcendental argument, which asks what the world must be like for a given practice to be possible. Bhaskar asked what the world must be like for experimental science to be intelligible. A separate, older use of the term comes from Kant, who applied "transcendental realism" to the view that space, time and objects as we experience them are things in themselves, and rejected it in favour of transcendental idealism. The two senses are unrelated.

## Core claims
- **Intransitive objects**: the objects of scientific enquiry, such as causal mechanisms, exist and act independently of scientists' knowledge of them, even though knowledge itself (the transitive dimension) is socially produced and fallible.
- **Stratified ontology**: reality has three domains. The real consists of structures and mechanisms with causal powers, the actual consists of the events they generate, and the empirical consists of what is experienced. These domains can come apart.
- **Argument from experiment**: experimentalists work to create closed conditions in which a mechanism's effect shows regularly. If laws were merely constant conjunctions of events, as in Humean empiricism, such effort would be pointless and laws would not apply outside the laboratory. Bhaskar concluded that laws describe the tendencies of mechanisms that persist when they are not manifest.
- **Judgemental rationality**: although knowledge is fallible and theory-laden, theories can be rationally compared.

## Development and critique
Bhaskar extended the approach to the social sciences in The Possibility of Naturalism (1979), where social structures are real but depend on the activities they enable. Collier (1994) provides an introduction, and Archer and colleagues (1998) collect essential readings. Critics argue that the transcendental argument establishes less than claimed and that unmanifested powers are metaphysically costly. Debates with [[structural_realism]] and constructive empiricism concern how much of a theory's unobservable content can be believed.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy. Active Inference describes agents inferring hidden causes of their sensations through a generative model, which parallels the distinction between the experienced and the underlying structures that generate it. The framework itself is neutral on metaphysical realism, and its Markov-blanket formulation is read in both realist and instrumentalist ways.
