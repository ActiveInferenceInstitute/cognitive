# Huayan Buddhism

## Overview
Huayan is one of the most philosophically interesting and historically influential schools of Chinese Buddhism. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarises its distinctive contributions as the doctrines that "one is all, and all is one", that ultimate reality is ontologically identical with the illusory world of common sense, and that realising these facts leads to universal compassion. The name means "Flower Garland" and comes from the *Avatamsaka Sutra*, an Indian Sanskrit text. Huayan also shows the influence of Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka, of Yogacara, and of *Awakening of Faith in Mahayana*, but it is a distinctively Chinese form of Buddhism, notably in interpreting emptiness in terms of interdependence. It spread to Korea as Hwaeom and to Japan as Kegon.

## Patriarchs
Like other Chinese Buddhist schools, Huayan identified a series of patriarchs who transmitted its teachings. Dushun (557-640) and Zhiyan (602-668) are named as first and second patriarchs, followed by Fazang (643-712), the third patriarch and the most accomplished and influential of the group, then Chengguan (738-839) and Guifeng Zongmi (780-841). The SEP names Fazang and Zongmi as the two most important. The school's vitality in China lapsed after the persecution of Buddhism under Emperor Wuzong (842-846), although its doctrines, metaphors and vocabulary influenced Chan and Song and Ming Neo-Confucianism.

## Core ideas
- **Interdependence and mutual identity.** Because everything is conditioned, and the identity of any one thing depends on the identities of the others, one is all, and because the whole depends for its identity on its parts, all is one. Fazang's illustration is the rafter and the building: the building is nothing over and above its parts, so each part is essential to its identity, and the rafter is what it is by its role in the building.
- **Indra's net.** A metaphor from the *Avatamsaka Sutra*: the god Indra has a net with a jewel at each intersection, and each jewel is so bright that it reflects every other. It illustrates how each thing depends for its existence and identity on every other thing.
- **Principle and phenomena (li and shi).** On the account of Chengguan, each phenomenon (*shi*) is identical with and includes principle (*li*), and since principle includes all phenomena, each phenomenon includes all others.
- **Samsara and nirvana.** Since the only thing that exists is this one interdependent reality, the world of illusion is not ontologically different from nirvana; samsara is a mistaken perception of the same reality that is experienced in enlightenment. Fazang's golden lion statue illustrates this: the lion is only a form of the gold.
- **One Mind and compassion.** Drawing on *Awakening of Faith in Mahayana*, Huayan posits a transpersonal One Mind of which all the transient aspects of reality are parts. Selfishness is caused by belief in individual selves, and universal compassion by recognition that all are parts of one reality.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an analogy and should be read cautiously. The picture of each part being defined by its relations to every other part resembles relational ontologies (see [[internal_relations]] and [[process_philosophy]]), and nested systems with Markov blankets also describe parts constituted by their couplings to wholes. Huayan is a Buddhist metaphysics and soteriology, not a scientific model, and no derivation links it to the free-energy formalism.
