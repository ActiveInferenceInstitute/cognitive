# Mind Uploading

## Overview
Mind uploading, also called whole brain emulation, is the proposal that the information structure of a brain could be scanned and instantiated in a computer, so that a digital system would reproduce the person's cognition and perhaps their consciousness and identity. It is a speculative idea. No scan or emulation of a human brain exists, and the established state of the art is far smaller: for example, the complete wiring diagram of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, with about 300 neurons, was published by White and colleagues in 1986, and even that has not yielded a fully predictive simulation of the animal's behaviour.

## What the proposal needs
Sandberg and Bostrom (2008) set out a roadmap of requirements: scanning at sufficient resolution, translating the scan into a model of neurons, synapses and perhaps other cellular detail, and running that model with enough computing power. Which level of detail suffices is unknown. The approach assumes that the relevant organisation of a mind is substrate-independent, a position tied to [[functionalism]] and to the [[computational_theory_mind]]. Moravec (1988) presented an early popular case for transferring minds to machines.

## Philosophical questions
- **Consciousness**: if a functional duplicate were built, would it be conscious? Functionalists generally say yes. Searle's biological naturalism and theories such as integrated information theory deny that computation alone suffices.
- **Personal identity**: is an upload the same person, a copy, or a successor? Parfit (1984) argued that what matters in survival is psychological continuity and connectedness, not strict identity, which makes copying less threatening. Others hold that bodily or biological continuity is required, or that destructive uploading amounts to death with a replica left behind.
- **Ethics**: uploaded minds raise questions about moral status, rights, copying and consent.

Chalmers (2010) examines gradual and destructive uploading as test cases for whether consciousness and identity would be preserved, and the arguments on each side remain disputed.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is weak and indirect. The Free Energy Principle describes systems in terms of Markov blankets and is stated independently of material substrate, which some read as consistent with substrate-independence. Enactive readings of active inference stress embodiment and metabolism, which counts against it. Neither reading settles the question.
