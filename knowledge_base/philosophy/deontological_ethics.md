# Deontological Ethics

## Overview
Deontological ethics, from the Greek *deon* (duty), holds that some actions are required or forbidden in virtue of what kind of action they are, not only because of the value of their outcomes. Typical deontological constraints include prohibitions on lying, promise-breaking, and killing or harming innocents, even where doing so would produce better overall results. It is one of the three main families of [[normative_ethics]], alongside [[consequentialism]] and [[virtue_ethics]].

## Kantian ethics
Kant's *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) is the most influential statement. He held that only a good will has unconditional moral worth, and that moral requirements are categorical imperatives, binding regardless of one's desires. He gave several formulations, including:
- **Universal law.** Act only on maxims that one could will to become universal laws.
- **Humanity.** Treat humanity, in oneself and others, always as an end and never merely as a means.

Contemporary Kantian and contractualist work, such as Scanlon's account of principles no one could reasonably reject, continues the focus on respect for persons.

## Other forms
Ross (1930) proposed a pluralist theory of prima facie duties, such as fidelity, non-maleficence and justice, which have to be weighed in judgment without a fixed ranking. Rights-based theories, such as Nozick's, treat rights as side constraints. Distinctions often drawn within the family include agent-relative constraints, which limit what each agent may do, and agent-relative options, which permit agents to give weight to their own projects.

## Objections
Critics point to conflicts of duties with no clear way to resolve them, to the counterintuitive results of absolutist versions, as in cases where telling a lie would save a life, and to the "paradox of deontology", which asks why one should not violate a constraint to minimise violations of the same constraint by others. Defenders answer with weighted or threshold versions and with accounts that ground constraints in the status of persons.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct link. Rule-following can be described in the framework as a strongly weighted prior over policies, but this describes behaviour and does not support any deontological ethical theory.
