# Patient Autonomy

## Overview
Patient autonomy is the principle in medical ethics that patients with decision-making capacity have the right to accept or refuse treatment on the basis of their own values, after receiving adequate information. In the widely used framework of Beauchamp and Childress (2019) respect for autonomy is one of four principles, alongside beneficence, non-maleficence and justice. It developed in the twentieth century in response to paternalistic medical practice and to abuses in research, and it is reflected in law and professional codes in many countries.

## Components
- **Informed consent**: Faden and Beauchamp (1986) analysed informed consent as an autonomous authorisation, with elements such as disclosure, understanding, voluntariness and competence. Consent is understood as an autonomous act, not only a legal formality.
- **Capacity**: a patient must be able to understand relevant information, appreciate its significance for their situation, reason about options and express a choice. Capacity is judged for a specific decision, not as a global status, and varies with illness, age and context.
- **Advance directives and proxies**: instructions or appointed representatives extend autonomy to times when a patient cannot decide.
- **Shared decision-making**: clinicians and patients deliberate together, with clinicians contributing expertise and patients contributing values.
- **Limits**: autonomy does not oblige clinicians to provide treatment they judge medically inappropriate, and it can be weighed against harm to others in cases such as infectious disease.

## Debates
Critics note that the principle can suit an individualistic view of persons. Relational accounts, collected in Mackenzie and Stoljar (2000), hold that autonomy develops and is exercised within social relationships and conditions, so that oppression, dependency or limited options can undermine it. Cross-cultural work finds that family-centred decision-making is preferred in some settings. Defenders of paternalism argue that interference is sometimes justified to prevent serious harm, while others hold that this requires strong evidence of impaired capacity. Dworkin (1988) provides a general philosophical analysis of autonomy.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is little direct connection. Computational psychiatry examines how disorders may alter the sense of agency and decision-making, which can bear on capacity assessment, but this research does not provide a clinical test for capacity.
