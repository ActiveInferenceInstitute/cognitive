# Theory-Ladenness

## Overview

Theory-ladenness is the claim that scientific observation is not a theory-neutral reception of data: what an observer sees, records or counts as an observation is shaped by prior concepts, expectations and theoretical commitments. The idea was developed by N. R. Hanson, Thomas Kuhn and Paul Feyerabend in reaction to the logical-positivist picture of an independent observation language.

## Forms of the thesis

- **Perceptual**: how we see depends on trained expectations. Hanson's example contrasts what a layperson and a physicist see when looking at an X-ray tube. Gestalt switches such as the duck-rabbit figure are used as analogies, though they are weak evidence for the strong claim.
- **Semantic**: observational terms acquire meaning from the theories that use them, so a change of theory changes the meaning of "mass" or "species".
- **Instrumental**: reading an instrument, such as a particle track, requires theory about how the instrument works. Measurement outcomes presuppose a model of the apparatus (see [[measurement_theory]]).
- **Methodological**: choices of what to measure, which anomalies to pursue and which data to discard are guided by theory.

## Consequences and limits

If theory-ladenness is total and circular, then evidence cannot test theory, and relativism threatens. Most philosophers accept a milder view. Observation can be laden with theories other than the one being tested; instruments can be validated using independent theories; and conflicting predictions can still be adjudicated by outcomes that both sides accept. Fodor's argument for the cognitive impenetrability of early perception is an influential counterpoint. The thesis therefore undermines the demand for foundations, not the possibility of empirical constraint. It bears directly on [[operationalism]], whose operations presuppose theory, and on [[logical_positivism]], which relied on a clean observation language. Pragmatists welcome the result since inquiry is anyway treated as a revisable web of belief and practice (see [[fallibilism]]).

## Role in Active Inference

The predictive-processing view of perception gives a mechanistic reading of the thesis. In a hierarchical generative model, sensory input is interpreted through prior beliefs about hidden causes, and what is experienced is the posterior, a product of prior and likelihood. Precision weighting determines how much priors influence an observation, so perception is theory-laden by construction while remaining constrained by prediction error. This supports a moderate rather than a relativist reading, and it is an analogy with scientific practice: how closely brains and scientific communities resemble each other as inferential systems is itself an empirical and contested matter.
