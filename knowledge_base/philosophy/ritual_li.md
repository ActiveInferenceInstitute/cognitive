# Ritual (Li)

## Overview
*Li* (禮) is the Chinese term for ritual, understood broadly to cover sacrifice and ceremony, courtesy, and the conduct proper to one's role. It is a central concept of Confucian thought (see [[confucian_philosophy]]). Mark Csikszentmihalyi's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry presents the Confucius of the *Analects* and the *Records of Ritual* as deeply concerned with the proper performance of ritual and music. It describes the attitudes and affect of the performer as the foundation of a "ritual psychology" in which correct performance reforms desires and begins to develop moral dispositions.

## Confucius
Confucius sought to preserve the Zhou ritual system, and the entry says he theorised about how ritual and music inculcated social roles, limited desires and transformed character. The *Analects* shows him insisting on adherence to the letter of the rites. When a disciple wished to substitute another animal for a sheep in a seasonal sacrifice, he is reported to have said, "though you care about the sheep, I care about the ritual" (3.17). This adherence is why many modern writers call him a traditionalist, but the entry argues that his innovation lay in the rationale. Earlier discussions often explained ritual in terms of making offerings in order to receive benefits, whereas the exchanges between Confucius and his disciples describe benefits that go beyond propitiating spirits or maintaining the social or cosmic order, and stress the value of the performer's interior psychological state. In *Analects* 3.26 Confucius condemns performing ritual without reverence (*jing*).

## Xunzi
For Xunzi, handed-down rituals are the best guide for most ordinary people. Some scholars describe them as efficient social conventions, but Goldin's Stanford Encyclopedia entry explains why that reading is inadequate.
- Xunzi describes the sages as instituting ritual and morality to restrain the competition that unbridled desire would produce.
- He denies that an arbitrary ritual code would work. The sage kings' rituals are legitimate because they accord with what makes humans human, for instance with the distinctions (male and female, old and young) that human beings naturally observe.
- Rituals also foster moral and psychological development. The three-year mourning period, for example, supplies a form in which deep and potentially debilitating grief can be properly expressed.
- Ritual and music together are two aspects of human artifice (*wei*), and the proper implementation of ritual is the key to statecraft.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. Ritual is a structured, shared pattern of action that shapes expectations and emotion, which can be compared with accounts of social practice as a source of shared priors and of the regulation of affect (see [[moral_emotions]]). Nothing in the formalism addresses ritual, and the Confucian claim concerns moral formation, not prediction.
