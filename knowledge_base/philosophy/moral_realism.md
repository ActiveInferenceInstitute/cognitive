# Moral Realism

## Overview
Moral realism is the position in [[meta_ethics]] that moral claims purport to describe features of reality, that some of them are true, and that their truth does not depend on the opinions or attitudes of any individual or culture. On this view, the claim that torturing for fun is wrong can be true in roughly the way that a claim about chemistry can be true. Realists differ over what the moral facts are and how they relate to the rest of the world.

## Forms
- **Non-naturalist realism.** Moral properties are irreducible and sui generis. Moore (1903) argued that "good" is a simple, indefinable property, and later defenders include Shafer-Landau (2003) and Parfit.
- **Naturalist realism.** Moral properties are natural properties that can be studied in the ordinary empirical manner. The Cornell realists argued that moral properties are causally relevant and can be identified with complex natural properties.
- **Robust versus minimal realism.** Robust forms hold that moral facts are mind-independent and substantive, while quasi-realist positions try to earn the language of truth and fact without a heavy ontology.

## Arguments
Supporters cite the apparent objectivity of moral experience, the possibility of moral progress and error, and a parity argument that moral claims are no worse off epistemically than mathematical or modal ones. Opponents offer several objections. Mackie's argument from queerness holds that objective values would be metaphysically and epistemically peculiar. The argument from disagreement points to persistent moral disagreement. Evolutionary debunking arguments hold that natural selection shaped moral beliefs toward fitness, not truth, which undermines their reliability if moral facts are independent (Street, 2006). Realists reply that the debunking argument over-generalises or that evolved faculties could still track moral truth.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no link between the framework and the realism debate. Active Inference models how agents learn and hold preferences and norms, but this is silent on whether any such preference corresponds to a mind-independent moral fact. Evolutionary debunking arguments draw on selection theory, which is a distinct background literature.
