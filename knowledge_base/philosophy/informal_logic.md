# Informal Logic

## Overview
Informal logic is the study of the interpretation, evaluation and construction of arguments in ordinary language. Whereas formal logic evaluates arguments by their form, informal logic is concerned with how arguments function in discourse, including implicit premises, relevance, the support given by evidence and the role of audience and context. It grew from the critical-thinking movement of the 1970s and is closely associated with argumentation theory.

## Main ideas
- **Toulmin's model.** Toulmin (1958) argued that arguments in law, science and everyday life are not assessed by a single formal standard, and proposed a layout of claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier and rebuttal. The model emphasises field-dependent standards of good reasoning.
- **Relevance, sufficiency, acceptability.** Johnson and Blair proposed that a good argument has acceptable premises that are relevant to and together sufficient for the conclusion.
- **Argument schemes and critical questions.** Walton and colleagues catalogue patterns of presumptive reasoning, such as argument from expert opinion, analogy or cause to effect, each paired with questions for testing it. These schemes are defeasible: a conclusion is supported provisionally and may be withdrawn on new information.
- **Fallacy analysis.** The field inherited the study of reasoning errors (see [[fallacies]]) and revised it in the direction of context-dependent, dialogue-based criteria.
- **Relation to formal logic.** Some hold that informal logic is an extension of logic and others that it is a separate normative enquiry, and there is debate about whether deductive validity has a role beyond a limiting case.

Groarke's encyclopedia entry gives an overview.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is limited. Defeasible, evidence-sensitive reasoning resembles probabilistic updating, and some writers use Bayesian models to analyse argument strength, including fallacies such as the argument from ignorance. This is a separate research programme in the psychology of reasoning and is not part of Active Inference itself. The framework describes inference as a process in the brain, while informal logic concerns the norms for argumentation in public discourse (see [[inductive_reasoning]], [[abductive_reasoning]]).
