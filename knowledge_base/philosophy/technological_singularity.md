# Technological Singularity

## Overview
The technological singularity is a hypothesised future point at which technological change, driven by artificial intelligence that surpasses human intellect, becomes so rapid and profound that the world after it cannot be reliably forecast from the world before. The term is borrowed by analogy from mathematics and physics, where a singularity is a point at which a model breaks down, and it should not be confused with a gravitational singularity. It is a speculative idea, not an established prediction.

## Main versions
- **Vinge** (1993) argued that the creation of superhuman intelligence would end the human era as understood, and that it would probably occur within a few decades. He outlined several paths, including AI, computer networks, human-computer interfaces and biological enhancement.
- **Kurzweil** (2005) combined a "law of accelerating returns", the claim that information technologies improve exponentially, with extrapolations of computing power and brain-scanning to predict a merger of human and machine intelligence around the mid-twenty-first century. The forecast dates are his own and have not been validated.
- **Intelligence explosion**: Good's mechanism of recursive self-improvement is often treated as the engine of a singularity (see [[intelligence_explosion]]). Chalmers (2010) separates the argument for an explosion from the further claim of a singularity, discusses obstacles that could block or delay it, and considers how humans might fare. Bostrom (2014) analyses the routes to superintelligence and the control problems it would pose.

## Evidence and criticism
Observed exponential trends, such as growth in computing capability described by Moore's law, are real over limited periods, but extrapolating them indefinitely is contested. Critics argue that trends follow S-curves, that physical and economic limits intervene, that software progress is not guaranteed to follow hardware, and that human-level general intelligence has not been shown to be achievable on a given timetable. Surveys of researchers show a wide spread of estimates. Supporters respond that forecasting errors have often run towards underestimating technological change, and that the possible stakes justify planning.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no formal link. Active Inference is a framework for modelling adaptive agents and is neutral on whether machine intelligence will grow explosively. Discussions of artificial agents built on it are part of ordinary AI research, not forecasts about a singularity.
