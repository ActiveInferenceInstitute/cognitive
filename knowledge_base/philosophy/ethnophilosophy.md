# Ethnophilosophy

## Overview
Ethnophilosophy, in the context of African philosophy, denotes the practice of describing the shared beliefs, myths, language and customs of a people as if they constituted a philosophy. The term is now used mostly as a critical label. According to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, it acquired its notoriety through the work of the Beninese philosopher Paulin Hountondji (1970, 1983), although it had been used before.

## Origins and critique
The paradigm case is Placide Tempels's *Bantu Philosophy* (first French edition 1945; English translation 1959), a work by a Belgian missionary that described a Bantu system of thought. Hountondji criticised Tempels and his followers, including the ethnographer Marcel Griaule, the Rwandan philosopher Alexis Kagame and the theologian John Mbiti, for casting African philosophy as an anonymous system of thought without individual thinkers to claim or account for it.

The Kenyan philosopher H. Odera Oruka drew on the same concerns for his Sage Philosophy project. He aimed to counter the view that traditional Africa was a place of philosophical unanimity, a view that, he noted, could be invoked by political leaders in post-independence one-party states to treat opposition as un-African. He presented evidence of diversity of thought among individual indigenous thinkers, whom he called sages, and sought to separate clearly reasoned ideas from myth, or what he called "anthropological fogs". Hountondji, though critical of ethnophilosophy, was also the first to distance himself from the view that a conceptual leap away from myth is required for philosophy, arguing that local knowledge systems were already separate from myths. Kwasi Wiredu argued that critical disputation and the pursuit of consensus were present in Akan communities.

## Significance and debate
The dispute concerns what counts as philosophy: whether a collective worldview qualifies, or whether philosophy requires identifiable individuals who argue for positions and can be criticised. It also bears on method, since describing communal beliefs without sorting argument from myth risks portraying a culture as uncritical. Accounts of shared concepts, such as those summarised in [[african_communitarianism]], need to be read with this debate in mind. The debate connects to wider questions about whose reasoning is recognised as knowledge (see [[epistemic_justice]] and [[epistemic_violence]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is thematic and weak. A shared worldview can be described as a set of common priors held by a community, and the debate over unanimity versus diversity concerns how much variation in such priors exists among members. This is a loose analogy, and the dispute is about the nature of philosophy and not about inference.
