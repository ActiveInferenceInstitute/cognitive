# Nothingness

## Overview
Nothingness, or non-being, is a topic in metaphysics and existential philosophy concerning what it means to speak of nothing, whether absence has a kind of reality, and why there is something rather than nothing. Sorensen's entry in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy surveys the logical and metaphysical puzzles. Philosophers disagree on whether "nothing" names an object, a quantifier expression with no referent, or a feature of experience.

## Historical treatments
- **Parmenides** argued that what is cannot not be, so that non-being cannot be thought or spoken, which set a long-running problem for later thought.
- **Leibniz** posed the question "why is there something rather than nothing?" as the fundamental question, relating it to the principle of sufficient reason. Holt (2012) gives a modern survey of attempts to answer it, including cosmological, theological and anthropic proposals.
- **Heidegger** (1929), in "What is Metaphysics?", held that anxiety discloses nothing as the ground against which beings are revealed as beings, and used the phrase that the nothing "nothings". He treated it as basic to the question of Being (see [[heideggerian_ontology]]).
- **Sartre** (1943) distinguished being-in-itself, which is full and self-identical, from being-for-itself, consciousness, which introduces negation and lack into the world, so that nothingness enters through human freedom. See [[being_and_nothingness]].
- **Carnap** (1932) criticised Heidegger's treatment as an example of pseudo-statements produced by misusing the logical form of "nothing", treating it as a name. Defenders of Heidegger reply that Carnap misreads the intent of the lecture.

## Distinct senses
Several notions are often run together: the logical empty set, physical vacuum (which in physics is not nothing, since it has structure and energy), absence of a particular thing, and the metaphysical absolute nothing. Perceptual absence, such as noticing that a sound has stopped, is a separate topic in psychology.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct connection. A loose point of contact is that predictive accounts of perception treat expected but missing input as informative, so absences can be represented, but this concerns ordinary absence, not metaphysical nothingness.
