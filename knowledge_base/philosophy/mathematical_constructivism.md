# Mathematical Constructivism

## Overview
Mathematical constructivism is a family of positions in the philosophy and practice of mathematics according to which proving that an object exists requires giving, or in principle providing, a construction of it. A pure existence proof by contradiction does not suffice. (Constructivism in epistemology and learning theory is a different usage; see [[constructivism]].) The view contrasts with [[mathematical_platonism]], on which mathematical objects exist independently of construction.

## Varieties
- **Intuitionism.** Brouwer held that mathematics is a free activity of the human mind and that its objects are mental constructions. He rejected the unrestricted use of the law of excluded middle ($P \lor \neg P$) for infinite domains. Heyting formalised intuitionistic logic, in which a proof of $P \lor Q$ must show which disjunct holds, and a proof of $\neg P$ is a method that transforms any proof of $P$ into a contradiction.
- **Constructive analysis.** Bishop (1967) developed a large part of analysis constructively, with every existence claim carrying computational content, while remaining compatible with classical mathematics.
- **Type-theoretic constructivism.** Martin-Löf's type theory interprets propositions as types and proofs as programs, a correspondence that connects constructive mathematics with computer science.
- **Others.** Russian recursive mathematics and finitist positions are further variants.

Constructivists accept fewer theorems than classical mathematics, for example the intermediate value theorem needs a modified formulation, but they obtain results with explicit algorithms. Troelstra and van Dalen (1988) provide a standard two-volume treatment and the Stanford Encyclopedia article (Bridges, Palmgren and Ishihara) gives an overview. Classical mathematicians, following Hilbert, have objected that restricting reasoning discards useful methods, a debate sometimes called the foundational crisis.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is slight. Intuitionistic logic is relevant to type theory and programming, and some work in categorical or compositional modelling uses constructive foundations. In the mathematics of Active Inference, probability theory is conventionally classical. Brouwer's claim that mathematics derives from mental construction is part of the history of ideas about mathematical cognition (see [[philosophy_of_mathematics]]) and is not an empirical claim of the framework.
