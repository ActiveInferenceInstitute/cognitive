# Philosophy of Technology

## Overview
The philosophy of technology examines what technologies are, how they are designed and used, and how they shape knowledge, action and society. It has descriptive concerns, such as the nature of artefacts and engineering knowledge, and evaluative ones about the benefits, risks and values embodied in technical systems. Ethical analysis of specific technologies is treated under [[technology_ethics]].

## Approaches
- **Instrumental view.** Technology is a neutral means that serves human ends; evaluation concerns how it is used.
- **Substantive view.** Heidegger (1977) argued that modern technology is a way of revealing the world, "enframing", in which nature and people alike appear as resources to be ordered and used. On this view technology shapes human existence beyond the intentions of its users.
- **Politics of artefacts.** Winner (1980) argued that some artefacts embody particular forms of authority or social arrangements, so that design choices have political consequences.
- **Postphenomenology.** Ihde (1990) and Verbeek (2005) analyse how technologies mediate perception and action, for instance by amplifying some aspects of the world while reducing others, and Verbeek extends the analysis to moral agency.
- **Philosophy of engineering and design.** This concerns function, artefact kinds, and the epistemology of design, as distinct from natural science.

## Contested issues
Debates concern technological determinism against social construction of technology, the moral status of artefacts that appear to act autonomously, and responsibility for outcomes of complex sociotechnical systems. These debates have intensified with work on artificial intelligence (see [[philosophy_of_ai]], [[ai_ethics]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The relationship is an analogy with an active literature. Accounts of the extended mind and of tool use treat technologies as incorporated into the cognitive and bodily loop of an agent, and Active Inference has been used to model how a tool changes the expected consequences of action. Whether technology constitutes part of cognition or only scaffolds it is disputed (see [[extended_mind]]), and the framework is silent on the evaluative questions that dominate the field.
