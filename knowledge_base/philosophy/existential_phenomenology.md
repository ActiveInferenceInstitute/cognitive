# Existential Phenomenology

## Overview
Existential phenomenology is a label for philosophers who use Husserl's descriptive, first-person method to study human existence as embodied, historically situated, finite and engaged with others, instead of studying consciousness as a detached source of meaning. Its principal figures are Heidegger, Sartre, Merleau-Ponty and de Beauvoir. The term describes a family resemblance and not a school, and these authors differ on many points.

## Main themes
- **Situatedness.** Heidegger's analysis of [[being_in_the_world]] replaces the subject-object picture with an account of practical involvement in a meaningful world.
- **Freedom and facticity.** Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* (1943) analyses consciousness as free, yet always in a situation (see [[facticity]]). De Beauvoir applied related ideas to ethics and to the situation of women in *The Second Sex* (1949).
- **The body.** Merleau-Ponty (1945) argued that perception is the activity of a lived body that is neither a mere object nor a mind, and that motor skill reveals a form of bodily intentionality that precedes explicit judgment (see [[lived_body]]).
- **Method.** Merleau-Ponty argued that a complete phenomenological reduction is impossible, since one can never fully step outside one's situation. Heidegger also stresses interpretation (see [[hermeneutics]]).

Moran (2000) and Gallagher and Zahavi (2008) provide introductions. Critics, including some analytic philosophers, regard the writing as obscure, and others question whether descriptions of experience can establish ontological claims.

## Relation to Active Inference
The tradition is a stated source for embodied, enactive and ecological approaches in cognitive science (see [[embodied_cognition]], [[enactive_cognition]]), and several authors have explored connections with Active Inference, including in discussions of embodiment, affordances and skilled action. The relation is an ongoing area of interpretation and debate. Some argue that the framework's use of internal generative models is at odds with the tradition, while others see them as reconcilable.
