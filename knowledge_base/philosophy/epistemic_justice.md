# Epistemic Justice

## Overview
Epistemic justice concerns fairness in how people are treated as knowers and contributors to shared understanding. The topic was set out by Fricker (2007), who focused on its opposite, epistemic injustice, a wrong done to someone specifically in their capacity as a knower. The ideal of epistemic justice is then a practice of testimony, interpretation and credibility that avoids such wrongs.

## Two forms of injustice
- **Testimonial injustice.** A speaker receives less credibility than their evidence warrants because of prejudice about their identity, for example gender, race or class. Fricker holds that this harms the speaker both practically and as a rational agent, and distorts the hearer's own knowledge.
- **Hermeneutical injustice.** A gap in shared interpretive resources puts someone at an unfair disadvantage in making sense of their own experience. Fricker's example is the absence, before the term existed, of a concept for sexual harassment.
- **Virtues.** Fricker proposes that hearers cultivate testimonial justice, a reflective correction of prejudice in credibility judgements, and hermeneutical justice, attentiveness to the limits of shared concepts.
- **Extensions.** Medina (2013) examines how structures of ignorance and resistance sustain injustice, and later work covers silencing, exclusion from inquiry and contributory injustice.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is exploratory. In models of social inference, how much weight a listener gives a speaker can be treated as a precision assigned to testimony, and prejudice then appears as systematically mis-set precision tied to social identity (see [[social_cognition]]). Related work on trust studies how agents learn to rely on others (see [[trust_building]]). These are modelling proposals and analogies, with no established empirical programme, and they describe how bias could arise without settling the ethical analysis.
