# Disability Theory

## Overview
Disability theory asks what disability is, how disadvantage associated with bodily or mental difference arises, and what follows for justice, medicine and social policy. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy observes that philosophers have always lived among people with disabilities, but that treating disability as a subject of philosophical interest in its own right is relatively new. It suggests an explanation: the concepts of disability and impairment did not exist until nineteenth-century scientific thinking put variations in human function and form into categories of abnormality, after which it became possible to generalise about "the disabled". Political philosophy of the later twentieth century tended to treat disability as a source of unearned disadvantage to be addressed by medical correction or compensation, and later social philosophers began to see it also as a source of discrimination, oppression and group identity.

## Models of disability
- **Medical model.** Explains the disadvantage in terms of pathological states of the body and mind, so that the limitations faced by people with disabilities result mainly from bodily differences. The SEP says it is rarely defended explicitly, though aspects of it are often adopted unreflectively when the contribution of the social environment is underestimated.
- **Social models.** Explain the characteristic features of disability by the relation between an individual and a social environment that excludes people with certain characteristics, through deliberate segregation and through built environments and organised activity that restrict participation. The "British social model" associated with the Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation (UPIAS, 1976) distinguishes impairment, an individual characteristic, from disability, a disadvantage imposed by society. It is now the dominant paradigm in legislation, social science and the humanities.
- **Objections to the social model.** Critics argue that the stronger versions understate the role of impairment itself as a source of disadvantage, and that both the medical and social models rest on a false dichotomy between biological impairment and social limitation. Some hold that impairment is itself socially shaped, noting shifting classifications and the medicalisation and demedicalisation of conditions. Others reply that the British model does not deny the importance of impairment but restricts the term "disability" to social exclusion and oppression.
- **Affirmative views.** Disability activists and scholars have more recently questioned the assumption shared by the medical and social models that disability is fundamentally a matter of disadvantage, exclusion or functional limitation.

## Definitions
The SEP also surveys philosophical definitions, among them normal-function views, a welfarist view, social constructionisms, inability views and scepticism about defining disability. The choice of definition affects debates in bioethics and justice (see [[biomedical_ethics]] and [[social_justice]]), and the phenomenology of impaired bodies is treated in work on the [[lived_body]].

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is limited. Active Inference has been applied in computational psychiatry and in models of altered perception and action, but those applications use the vocabulary of inference and precision and do not settle the normative question of whether a condition is a disability, a difference or a disorder. Care is needed that the framework's formal language of "dysfunction" is not taken to endorse the medical model.
