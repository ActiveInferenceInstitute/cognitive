# Shingon Esoteric Buddhism

## Overview
Shingon, "the word of truth", is the Japanese pronunciation of the Chinese *zhenyan* and the Sanskrit *mantra*. It is the form of Mantrayana, or Vajrayana, Buddhism that Kukai brought to Japan from Tang China. According to John Krummel's entry in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Kukai was ordained in 804 in order to travel with an official embassy to China, studied in Chang'an, and there received initiation from the master Huiguo into the two lineages of esoteric Buddhism, associated with the Diamond realm and Womb mandalas. He returned to Japan in 806, began building a monastic centre on Mount Koya in 816, and died there in 835, aged 61. He was later given the title Kobo Daishi.

## Main teachings
- **Esoteric and exoteric.** Kukai distinguished the esoteric teachings (*mikkyo*) he introduced from the exoteric teachings (*kengyo*) of the forms of Buddhism already established in Japan, and presented Shingon as the most comprehensive.
- **The cosmic Buddha.** The Buddha Dainichi (Mahavairocana) is identified with the cosmos. Its body is made of six great elements (fire, earth, water, air, space and consciousness), which interpenetrate without obstructing one another. Because they arise in dependence on one another they are also empty, so the cosmic body is at once the ground of beings and their non-substantiality.
- **Three mysteries.** Dainichi's activity appears as body, speech and mind, which are practised as gesture (*mudra*), sound (*mantra*) and visualisation (*mandala*). All sounds are taken to be the Buddha's voice and all forms his body.
- **Enlightenment in this very body.** The doctrine *sokushinjobutsu*, first given systematic explication by Kukai, holds that Buddhahood is attainable in this embodied existence. Krummel describes it as a philosophy of practice that is at the same time a philosophy of the body.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is interpretive. A view in which mind and body are two aspects of one embodied activity and are continuous with their environment has some affinity with embodied and enactive treatments of cognition (see [[lived_body]]), and Shingon's claim that practice shapes experience can be compared with the way action and perception are coupled in the framework. Shingon is a religious doctrine of liberation, and the comparison is suggestive only.
