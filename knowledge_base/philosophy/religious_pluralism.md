# Religious Pluralism

## Overview
Religious pluralism, in the philosophy of religion, is the position that the major religious traditions are different and comparably valid ways of responding to one transcendent reality, and that none has an exclusive claim to truth or salvation. It is the third term in the typology of exclusivism, inclusivism and pluralism (Race, 1983). The word is also used in a descriptive, sociological sense for the coexistence of many religions in a society, and in a civic sense for the principled acceptance of that coexistence. These senses are distinct from the theological claim discussed here.

## Hick's hypothesis
John Hick (1989) gave the best-known philosophical statement. He drew on Kant's distinction between noumenon and phenomenon and proposed that the Real in itself is beyond human concepts, while the traditions experience it through culturally formed lenses, as personal deities or non-personal absolutes. Their varied doctrines are then responses to the Real as it appears in different historical settings. Hick suggested that the traditions can be compared by whether they transform people from self-centredness to a Reality-centred orientation, which he saw as taking comparable moral forms across traditions.

## Criticisms and responses
- **From exclusivists**: Plantinga and others argue that pluralism is itself a substantive view that rejects the self-understanding of most traditions, many of which make incompatible truth claims. Critics also say that pluralism cannot say anything about the Real without contradicting its own claim that the Real is ineffable.
- **From within pluralism**: some defenders reply that the Real is ineffable only in a limited sense and that the hypothesis is a proposal about the best explanation of religious diversity.
- **Relativism**: critics ask whether the view collapses into [[moral_relativism]] or relativism about truth. Hick denied this and kept a realist position on the Real.
- **From some religious adherents**: members of traditions argue that the framework misrepresents them by imposing a standard foreign to their own, a concern raised in the essays collected by Meeker and Quinn (2000).

## Relation to cognitive science
Hick's use of a Kantian distinction, in which experience depends on the categories the subject brings to it, loosely parallels modern views on perception as shaped by priors, as in predictive processing. This is an analogy, and Active Inference does not support or undermine any religious claim.
