# Fallacies

## Overview
A fallacy is an error in reasoning, typically one that is persuasive in appearance. The study of fallacies is one of the oldest topics in logic and goes back to Aristotle's *Sophistical Refutations*. It is usual to separate formal fallacies, which have an invalid logical form, from informal fallacies, whose faults involve content, context or the use of language.

## Types and assessment
- **Formal fallacies.** Examples are affirming the consequent (inferring $P$ from $P \rightarrow Q$ and $Q$) and denying the antecedent. Such arguments are invalid, since the premises can be true while the conclusion is false (see [[deductive_reasoning]]).
- **Informal fallacies.** Common categories include ambiguity (equivocation), irrelevance (appeals to authority, popularity or personal characteristics, and red herrings), presumption (begging the question, false dilemmas) and weak induction (hasty generalisation, post hoc reasoning). Lists and groupings differ between authors, and the classifications are not exhaustive.
- **Context sensitivity.** Hamblin (1970) criticised the "standard treatment" in textbooks as a loose list of cases with no theory. Walton (1995) developed a pragmatic account on which a fallacy is an argumentative move that breaches the norms of a dialogue type, so that the same form, such as an appeal to expert opinion, can be reasonable in one context and fallacious in another.
- **Descriptive versus normative.** Labelling an argument a fallacy is a normative claim about reasoning, and critics warn against using the labels dismissively.

Dowden's encyclopedia entry catalogues many types. See [[informal_logic]] for the broader field of argument analysis.

## Relation to Active Inference
Fallacies are normative categories, while research on heuristics and biases describes how human judgement departs from normative standards (see [[heuristics]], [[kahneman_tversky]]). Some of these departures have been modelled in Bayesian and active inference terms, for instance as consequences of approximate inference or strong priors, but this is a modelling approach and does not turn fallacies into theorems of the framework. The two literatures answer different questions.
