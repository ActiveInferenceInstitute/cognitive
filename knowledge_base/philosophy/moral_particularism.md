# Moral Particularism

## Overview
Moral particularism is the view that moral thought and judgement do not depend on a set of moral principles. Its most developed statement is by Jonathan Dancy, who argues that a feature which counts in favour of an action in one case may be irrelevant or count against it in another. Particularists therefore hold that the moral work is done by the detailed context of each case. The opposing position, generalism, holds that moral reasons are underwritten by principles, as in standard versions of [[consequentialism]] and [[deontological_ethics]].

## Central argument
Dancy's argument rests on the holism of reasons. A feature such as "the act would give pleasure" favours an action in many circumstances, yet in a case of pleasure taken in cruelty it may count against. If the contribution of a feature varies with context, no principle assigning it a fixed moral weight will be exceptionless. Particularists add that competent moral agents do not need such principles, because they perceive what matters in a particular situation, a theme shared with virtue-based accounts that stress practical wisdom ([[virtue_ethics]]).

## Varieties and criticism
Positions differ in strength. Some particularists deny that any true moral principle exists; others permit defeasible generalisations that serve as rules of thumb. Critics argue that without principles moral judgement cannot be explained, taught or made consistent across cases, and that the variability of reasons is compatible with more complex principles that mention the relevant background conditions. Defenders reply that such principles become unmanageably long or vacuous. The debate is carried on within normative theory, and the page takes no side.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct connection. An analogy is available: context-sensitive judgement resembles inference in a generative model where the weight of a cue depends on the inferred situation, in contrast with fixed rules. This is an interpretive parallel, and Active Inference does not itself adjudicate between particularism and generalism.
