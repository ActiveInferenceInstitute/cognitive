# Nyaya Logic

## Overview
Nyaya (literally "rule or method of reasoning") is a leading school of philosophy within the Hindu tradition (see [[hindu_philosophy]]). Matthew Dasti's Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry describes its epistemology as a sophisticated precursor of contemporary reliabilism, centred on the idea of "knowledge-sources" (*pramana*), and its metaphysics as a robust realism that includes universals, selves and substances. The oldest extant text is the *Nyaya-sutra*, attributed to Gautama (c. 200 CE). A "new" phase (*navya-nyaya*) begins with Gangesa Upadhyaya (c. 1325).

## Knowledge and inference
- **Pramanas.** Nyaya recognises four sources of knowledge: perception (*pratyaksa*), inference (*anumana*), analogy (*upamana*) and testimony (*sabda*).
- **Five-step argument.** For debate and instruction, Nyaya uses a formal argument with five members: thesis (*pratijna*), reason (*hetu*), illustration of concomitance (*udaharana*), application (*upanaya*) and conclusion (*nigamana*). The stock example runs: there is fire on the hill, because there is smoke on the hill; wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen hearth and unlike a lake; this hill is likewise smoky; therefore there is fire on the hill.
- **Pervasion.** The inference rests on *vyapti*, the concomitance of smokiness with fieriness, which the entry renders as a universal conditional, and it is supported by corroborative instances (*sapaksa*) and negative examples (*vipaksa*). Nyaya also classifies fallacies and defeaters of inference, and studies suppositional reasoning.
- **Place in the history of logic.** Brendan Gillon's Stanford Encyclopedia entry notes that the *Nyaya-sutra* and the medical *Caraka-samhita* both take the syllogism to have five parts, and that later logicians such as Dignaga (c. 5th-6th century CE) reworked the form into a canonical syllogism without the application and conclusion statements.
- **Default trust.** Phillips and Vaidya observe that Nyaya, which holds that knowledge is not self-evident to the knower as true, still subscribes to the principle "innocent until reasonably challenged".

## Metaphysics
Nyaya defends a pluralist realism of categories (*padartha*), largely adapted from its sister school Vaisheshika: substance, quality, action, universal, individuator, inherence and absence. Substances include atoms, composite objects such as pots and trees, selves (*atman*) and God. The school defended the reality of selves and composite substances against Buddhist critics. One argument is that recollection ("that is the very man I saw a week ago") requires a self that persists between the original experience and the later one. The entry refers to the combined body of doctrine as Nyaya/Vaisheshika metaphysics. Vaisheshika remains a separate school, from which Nyaya adapted the categories with some modifications.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is a loose parallel. Nyaya's analysis of inference from an observed sign to an unobserved property, grounded in a general regularity, can be compared, as an analogy, with inference in the Bayesian sense, though Nyaya's inference is deductive in form and treats the regularity as a universal concomitance and not as a probability. Active Inference makes no use of Nyaya categories, and the comparison is a point of contact in the history of ideas.
