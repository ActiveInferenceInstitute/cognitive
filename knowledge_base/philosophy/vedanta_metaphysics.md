# Vedanta Metaphysics

## Overview
Vedanta is nominally a school of Indian philosophy, but in reality it is a label for any hermeneutics that tries to give a consistent interpretation of the philosophy of the Upanishads or, more formally, of their canonical summary, Badarayana's *Brahma Sutra* (see [[hindu_philosophy]]). Sangeetha Menon's Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Advaita Vedanta notes this, and Andrew Nicholson's entry on Bhedabheda Vedanta adds that its inclusive sense is a philosophy that purports to interpret those texts. The schools differ mainly on how the individual self (*jiva*, *atman*) stands to the ultimate reality, Brahman.

## Advaita Vedanta
"Advaita" is often translated "non-dualism", though it literally means "non-secondness". Shankara (thought by many scholars to have lived in the eighth century) is regarded as its main promoter. Its essential philosophy is an idealist monism.
- **Brahman.** Brahman is the fundamental reality underlying all objects and experiences: pure existence, pure consciousness, pure bliss. Consciousness is Brahman's very nature and not merely a property of it.
- **Maya and ignorance.** The world appears because of Brahman's creative energy (*maya*), and individuality is attributed to ignorance (*avidya*). The entry distinguishes three planes of existence: absolute (*paramarthika*), worldly (*vyavaharika*) and illusory (*pratibhasika*), such as a mirage.
- **Epistemology.** The tradition offers correspondence, coherence and practical efficacy as lesser tests of truth. Its highest test is non-sublatability: a judgment is true if it is not later contradicted, as the snake seen where there is a rope is contradicted on closer inspection. Illusion is explained as superimposition (*adhyasa*) on a real substratum.
- **Liberation.** Knowledge of Brahman, "I am pure consciousness", ends the self-ignorance that "I am limited".

## Bhedabheda Vedanta
Bhedabheda ("difference and non-difference") holds that the individual self is both different and not different from Brahman. It reconciles Advaita, which holds the self to be wholly identical with Brahman, and Dvaita, which teaches complete difference. A notable disagreement concerns the phenomenal world, which Advaita holds ultimately unreal (*mithya*) and Bhedabheda thinkers hold real, in line with most Indian philosophical schools. Bhedabheda ideas strongly influenced the devotional (*bhakti*) schools of the medieval period, among them those founded by Vallabha and Caitanya.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. Advaita's analysis of error as superimposition on a substratum, and its treatment of the world as appearance dependent on ignorance, has been compared with discussions of perception as constructed from a model, but that parallel is imprecise. Active Inference is a theory of inference by physical systems and does not entail an ultimate consciousness. The metaphysical claims of Vedanta are not taken up by the framework (compare [[neutral_monism]] and [[panpsychism]] for Western relatives).
