# Intergenerational Justice

## Overview
Intergenerational justice asks what present people owe to people who lived before them and to those who will live after them. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives three central questions: whether present generations can be duty-bound by considerations of justice to past and future people; whether other moral considerations should guide the living in relating to them; and how to interpret the significance of past injustices in terms of what is owed to the descendants of the direct victims. The topic bears on climate policy, resource use, public debt and the preservation of institutions (see [[environmental_ethics]] and [[existential_risk]]).

## Why intergenerational relations are distinctive
According to the SEP entry, relations between non-contemporaries differ from those among contemporaries in three ways.
- **Lack of reciprocity.** Between generations that do not overlap there is no mutual cooperation or exchange in kind, which seems to undermine justice conceived as reciprocity, although some authors defend indirect or transgenerational forms of reciprocity.
- **Asymmetry of power.** The present generation can affect the desires, circumstances and interests of future people, for instance by a resource policy with long-term negative consequences, whereas remote future people cannot affect the well-being of the living.
- **Contingency of existence.** Present decisions affect whether future people exist, how many exist and who they are.

## The non-identity problem
The third feature gives rise to the non-identity problem, which Derek Parfit developed in *Reasons and Persons* (1984). If a policy changes who is conceived, then the people who live under a worse policy would not have existed under a better one, so it is hard to say that they were harmed by it, even though the policy seems wrong. Parfit draws on a genetic view of personal identity (see [[personal_identity]]). The SEP entry surveys four main responses and discusses Parfit's no-difference view and his disjunctive notion of harm.

## Specifying what is owed
If future people have claims, a further question is the threshold of what they must be left with. The SEP discusses egalitarian, prioritarian and sufficientarian specifications (compare [[distributive_justice]]). John Rawls in *A Theory of Justice* (1971) offered the first systematic account within a theory of justice of obligations to future people, in his just savings principle. The entry also discusses the limits of a rights-based account, and how to treat historical injustice and the claims of descendants of its victims.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is slight. Models of planning in Active Inference involve a finite temporal horizon over which expected outcomes are evaluated, which provides a formal setting in which the weight given to distant consequences can be discussed. Whether present people owe anything to future people is a normative question that the framework does not answer, and no result links the two.
