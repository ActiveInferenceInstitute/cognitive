# Chinese Philosophy

## Overview
Chinese philosophy is the body of philosophical thought produced in the Chinese-speaking world over some two and a half thousand years. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that no comprehensive history of Chinese philosophy was written until the modern period of Western influence, though Chinese thinkers had long engaged with earlier writers. The final chapter of the *Zhuangzi* gives an early survey, and the historian Sima Tan (c. 165-110 BCE) made the first real attempt at classification, into six schools: Yin-Yang, Confucianism, Mohism, the School of Names, Legalism and Daoism. Hu Shi's *An Outline of the History of Chinese Philosophy* (1919) was the first comprehensive history by a Chinese scholar. A common characterisation is that the trajectory of the whole can be seen in terms of "the three teachings" (*sanjiao*): Confucianism, Daoism and Buddhism, understood as streams of influence that flow together and affect one another.

## Periods and themes
- **Pre-Qin period (before 221 BCE).** The classical period includes the *Classic of Changes* (*Yijing*), which the IEP treats as the repository from which reflection on the nature of reality begins; Confucius (551-479 BCE) of the *Analects*, concerned with human self-development and social ethics and with concepts such as humaneness (*ren*) and the exemplary person (*junzi*); Mozi and the Mohists, who were masters of argument; the School of Names, which explored perspectivism and the relativity of distinctions; the *Daodejing* and the *Zhuangzi* of classical Daoism; and Mencius and Xunzi, the two major later Confucian thinkers (see [[confucian_philosophy]]).
- **Qin to Tang (221 BCE to 618 CE).** The period includes syncretic philosophies of Qin and Han times, the Legalism of Han Fei, the critical philosophy of Wang Chong, Profound Learning (*Xuanxue*), and the arrival and sinification of Buddhism, with schools such as Tiantai, Consciousness-only, Huayan and Chan (see [[chinese_buddhism]]).
- **Song and Neo-Confucianism (960-1279).** Thinkers including Zhou Dunyi, the Cheng brothers and Zhu Xi reworked Confucianism around concepts such as *qi*, *yin* and *yang*, and principle (*li*). Zhu Xi compiled the Four Books, which became the foundation of the imperial examinations. Wang Yangming later developed a rival school.
- **Modern period.** The encounter with Western philosophy shaped thinkers such as Kang Youwei, Hu Shi and Mao Zedong, and later movements such as New Confucianism.

## Using the term with care
"Chinese philosophy" is an organising label of modern origin, and the tradition is internally diverse. Readers should treat the period labels and school names as conveniences, since, as the IEP notes, the categories were added to and blended over time.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is limited and interpretive. Readers sometimes set Daoist ideas of spontaneity, Neo-Confucian *qi* and principle, or the Buddhist analysis of the self beside accounts of adaptive self-regulation, but such parallels are not derivations, and the Chinese traditions have their own aims and concepts. See [[process_philosophy]] for a Western tradition often compared with Daoist views of change.
