# Religious Epistemology

## Overview
Religious epistemology asks whether and how religious beliefs, such as belief in God, can be justified, rational or count as knowledge. It applies general epistemological notions, including evidence, warrant and justification, to religious claims, without presupposing that they are true or false. The page describes positions and their proponents and takes no view on the truth of any religion.

## Main positions
- **Evidentialism.** Clifford (1877) held that it is wrong to believe on insufficient evidence, and later evidentialists apply this to theistic belief, which they take to require supporting arguments or evidence. Some take the evidence to suffice, others not.
- **Reformed epistemology.** Plantinga, Wolterstorff and Alston argued that belief in God can be properly basic, rational without being inferred from other beliefs, in the way perceptual and memory beliefs are (Plantinga and Wolterstorff, 1983). Plantinga (2000) develops an account on which theistic belief has warrant if produced by properly functioning faculties aimed at truth.
- **Religious experience.** Alston (1991) argued that perceptual-like experience of God can give prima facie justification in a way parallel to sense perception, provided that the doxastic practice in which it figures is socially established and reliable.
- **Fideism.** Faith is held to be independent of, or to take priority over, rational assessment. Traditions of fideism cite thinkers such as Kierkegaard.
- **Sceptical and naturalistic critiques.** Hume's discussion of miracles and critics such as Mackie (1982) argue that the evidence does not favour theism, while naturalistic accounts of religious belief seek its causes in cognition and culture.

## Central disputes
Disagreements concern whether the debate over properly basic belief generalises to arbitrary beliefs, the significance of religious diversity for justification, and whether explaining the origin of a belief undermines its warrant (see [[skepticism]], [[justification]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct link in the philosophical literature. Some cognitive scientists have applied predictive-processing ideas to religious experience and belief, treating them as inferences under strong priors, but this work is early and does not bear on questions of justification or truth.
