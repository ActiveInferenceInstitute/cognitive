# Exchange Value

## Overview
Exchange value is a central concept of Karl Marx's analysis of capitalism, set out at the start of *Capital* (Volume 1, 1867), and it has older roots in classical political economy. It names the ratio in which a quantity of one commodity exchanges for a quantity of another, and so, initially, is to be understood as a commodity's price. Marx contrasts it with use-value, the usefulness of a thing, and treats the relation between the two as the starting point of his theory of value.

## Marx's account
According to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, the argument runs as follows.
- **Commodities.** A commodity is a useful external object produced for exchange on a market. Commodity production requires a market and a social division of labour in which different people produce different things.
- **Use-value and exchange value.** Commodities have both. Marx says use-value is easily understood, but that exchange value is a puzzling phenomenon that needs explanation: why does a given quantity of one commodity exchange for a given quantity of another?
- **The common third thing.** If two commodities can be put on either side of an equals sign, there must be a "third thing of identical magnitude" in both to which they are reducible. Marx argues that the only plausible common element is labour. The SEP remarks that both steps of this argument are highly contestable.
- **Value and labour time.** The labour theory of value asserts that the value of a commodity is determined by the quantity of socially necessary labour time required to produce it, meaning labour exerted at the average level of intensity and productivity for that branch of the economy. Exchange value is the form in which this value appears in exchange.
- **Surplus value.** Marx applies the theory to explain profit through exploitation: the value of a day's labour power is the value of the commodities needed to keep the worker alive, and labour beyond that necessary labour is surplus labour, which produces surplus value for the capitalist.

## Problems and debate
Marx himself recognised that prices vary systematically from values in real economic life, and the mathematical problem of relating the two is known as the transformation problem. The SEP reports that his own attempt has technical difficulties, and that if the theory is read as a theory of price, the indirect connection between price and value drains away its intuitive motivation. Others hold that this reads Marx superficially and that he aimed to see through the appearances of capitalism to its underlying basis. A further objection is that the claim that only labour can create surplus value is unsupported, since any commodity could be given the same formal role. How Marx's theory should be read remains an active area of scholarly debate.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is slight. Active Inference does not have a theory of economic value, though economic and market interactions have been modelled in its terms, and its notions of preference and expected utility are formal and not labour-based. The concept belongs to political economy and critical social theory, and no derivation links it to the free-energy framework.
