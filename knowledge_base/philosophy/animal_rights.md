# Animal Rights

## Overview
Animal rights is the family of positions in moral and political philosophy holding that at least some non-human animals possess morally significant interests or entitlements that should not be overridden merely for human benefit. It is narrower than [[animal_ethics]], which also includes welfarist views permitting some animal use. The issues concern how animals are used in food production, research, entertainment and clothing, and whether they should have legal standing.

## Main positions
- **Rights-based view**: Tom Regan (1983) argued that animals who are "subjects-of-a-life", with beliefs, desires, memory and a sense of the future, have inherent value and a basic right to respectful treatment. On this deontological account they may not be treated merely as means to others' ends.
- **Utilitarian view**: Peter Singer (1975) argued from the equal consideration of interests that the capacity to suffer, not species membership, is the relevant criterion. Singer himself does not rest his case on rights, and he is often contrasted with Regan on that point.
- **Other approaches**: Christine Korsgaard (2018) proposed a Kantian-influenced account in which animals are ends in themselves, and capability approaches extend questions of justice to animals. Welfarist positions accept animal use while requiring that suffering be limited.

## Objections and debates
Critics raise several challenges. Some argue that moral rights depend on capacities for reciprocity or rational agency that animals lack. Others contend that species membership is morally relevant, or that rights language is a poor fit for animals. Questions remain about where to draw the line among species and how to weigh conflicts between animal and human interests. Defenders respond that the argument from marginal cases, which compares animals with humans of limited cognitive capacity, shows that such criteria cannot be applied consistently.

## Relation to cognitive science
Empirical findings on animal sentience and cognition inform the debate, since claims about consciousness and suffering bear on moral status (see [[animal_cognition]]). The Free Energy Principle and Active Inference make no particular claims about moral status, and there is no substantive link beyond the shared interest in how organisms represent their needs.
