# Recognition Theory

## Overview
Recognition presupposes a subject who recognises and an object who is recognised. Mattias Iser's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry distinguishes recognition from identification, since we may identify something as an X without affirming it as X, whereas recognition requires a positive evaluation. Mutuality is the explanatory and normative core of the concept, and most theories draw on Hegel's idea that self-consciousness is gained only through a process of mutual recognition (see [[dialectical_method]]). Paul Ricoeur distinguished as many as 23 usages of "to recognise", grouped as recognition as identification, recognising oneself and mutual recognition.

## Forms of recognition
The entry reviews how the modern order of recognition has differentiated.
- **Respect and esteem.** The premodern notion of honour, assigned to people as members of a group in a hierarchy, split into the modern notion of equal respect awarded to all agents capable of autonomy, and esteem due to one's achievements.
- **Taylor's three forms.** In "The Politics of Recognition", Charles Taylor distinguishes a politics of universalism, which aims at equal recognition of all persons in their common humanity; a politics of difference, which emphasises the uniqueness of specific, especially cultural, features; and the recognition of concrete individuality in loving care.
- **Honneth's spheres.** Axel Honneth reads the three dimensions as stages in which individuals gain self-confidence within loving relationships, self-respect in the legal realm and self-esteem through social recognition of their accomplishments (*The Struggle for Recognition*).
- **Elementary recognition.** Some authors hold that a more basic form of recognition underlies all of these.

## Recognition and redistribution
In the mid-1990s Nancy Fraser argued that identity politics threatened to displace the issue of redistribution, and insisted that recognition and redistribution together are needed to secure "participatory parity", the ideal of equal participation in public life. Redistribution secures its objective condition and recognition its intersubjective condition. Fraser illustrated the independence of the two claims by contrasting the cultural humiliation of homosexuals with the economic exploitation of workers. Honneth replied that recognition can be applied to questions of distributive justice if respect and esteem are properly differentiated, so that redistribution is itself a problem of recognition. Fraser answered that many problems of global injustice stem from systemic features of capitalism and not primarily from misrecognition. The entry also considers whether recognition can function as ideology.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is an analogy. Accounts of the self as formed through interaction (see [[dialectical_method]]) resemble work in which an agent's model of itself is inferred partly from how others respond to it. This is a conceptual parallel and recognition theory carries normative commitments about justice that the framework does not address.
