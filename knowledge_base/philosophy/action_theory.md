# Action Theory

## Overview
Action theory, or the philosophy of action, asks what distinguishes things people do from things that merely happen to them. When an arm rises because its owner raises it, and when it rises because someone else lifts it, the bodily movement is the same, yet only the first is an action. The field studies the nature of intention, the individuation of actions, and the way reasons explain what agents do.

## Main positions
- **Causal theory of action.** Davidson (1963) argued that a reason, a belief paired with a pro-attitude such as a desire, can cause an action, and that explaining an action by its reasons is a form of causal explanation. Later causal theorists refined the view, partly to handle "deviant causal chains" in which the right mental states cause the right movement in the wrong way.
- **Anscombe's account.** In *Intention* (1957), Anscombe characterised intentional actions as those to which a particular sense of the question "Why?" applies, and described practical knowledge as knowledge of what one is doing that is not based on observation.
- **Planning theory.** Bratman (1987) treated intention as a distinctive plan-embodying state that settles future conduct and coordinates action over time, not simply a belief-desire pair.
- **Volitionism.** Older views posited an inner act of willing preceding the movement. Critics such as Ryle (1949) objected that this invites a regress.

A further dispute concerns individuation. Davidson held that one action may fall under many descriptions, whereas finer-grained views, such as those of Goldman and Kim, treat each description as picking out a distinct action.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active inference gives a computational account of how behaviour is produced: movement results from fulfilling predictions, and policies are selected by expected free energy, with preferences encoded as prior beliefs about outcomes (see [[action_selection]]). The structure plays a role comparable to belief-desire explanation, because prior preferences and beliefs jointly determine what is done. The link is an interpretive one. The framework is a model of control and does not by itself settle philosophical questions such as whether reasons are causes, how deviant chains are excluded, or what makes an action intentional. Whether the formalism supports a causal, an Anscombean, or some other reading is contested.
