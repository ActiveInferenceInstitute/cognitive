# Neurolaw

## Overview
Neurolaw is the field that asks what findings from neuroscience, especially brain imaging, imply for law and legal practice. Its topics include criminal responsibility, mental capacity, evidence of pain or deception, the sentencing of young offenders, and the protection of mental privacy. Garland (2004) collected early scholarly discussion, and Pardo and Patterson (2013) offered a conceptual analysis of the relation between law and neuroscience.

## Main issues
- **Brain-based evidence**: courts have heard structural and functional scans offered to show impairment, injury or reduced capacity. Critics note that group-level imaging findings do not straightforwardly describe one individual, that images can be persuasive beyond their evidential weight, and that the link between a brain state and a legal standard such as intent is not a purely empirical matter.
- **Responsibility**: legal responsibility is usually tied to capacities such as rationality and control, and to mental states such as intent and knowledge. Neuroscience might inform whether a defendant had those capacities, for example in cases of brain injury or developmental immaturity.
- **Lie detection and memory**: proposals to read deception or recognition from brain activity are contested on reliability and admissibility grounds.
- **Mental privacy**: methods for decoding mental states raise questions about compulsion to submit to scanning.

## Central debate
Greene and Cohen (2004) argued that neuroscience may gradually reshape intuitions about retributive punishment by revealing mechanistic causes of behaviour, even if it does not formally change legal doctrine. Others, associated with Stephen Morse, argue that law presupposes a folk-psychological view of persons as reason-responsive agents, which is compatible with causal explanation of behaviour, so that neuroscientific claims often overreach. Pardo and Patterson argue that some claims commit conceptual errors by attributing mental properties to brains rather than to persons. The positions differ over [[free_will]], [[determinism]] and [[agency]].

## Relation to Active Inference
There is little direct connection. Computational psychiatry uses Active Inference to model altered agency and control in some conditions, and such models could in principle inform discussion of capacity. This work is exploratory and has not been established as legal evidence.
