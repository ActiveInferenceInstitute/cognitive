# Care Ethics

## Overview
Care ethics is a moral approach that treats caring relationships as basic to morality. Instead of starting from impartial principles applied by independent individuals, it starts from the fact that people are dependent on one another, especially in infancy, illness and old age, and that moral life is largely about meeting needs in particular relationships. It is usually classed with [[normative_ethics]] and shares features with [[virtue_ethics]], but it is distinguished by its focus on relations instead of individual character.

## Origins and development
Gilligan (1982) challenged models of moral development that ranked reasoning from abstract justice above reasoning from responsibility and connection, and described a distinct "care" orientation. Noddings (1984) developed an ethics grounded in the experience of caring and being cared for. Held (2006) and Tronto (1993) extended it into a political and global ethic. Tronto and Fisher distinguished phases of care: caring about, taking care of, care-giving and care-receiving. Care ethics is closely allied with feminist philosophy and with pragmatist traditions, including the work of Jane Addams (see [[pragmatism]]).

## Central claims
- Moral attention should be directed to particular, concrete others, and not only to general principles.
- Dependence and vulnerability are normal conditions of human life, not exceptions.
- Care work has been undervalued and unevenly distributed, which makes the ethics also a critique of social arrangements.
- Emotion, particularly empathy and attentiveness, plays a legitimate role in moral judgment.

## Objections
Critics argue that care ethics risks parochialism, favouring those close to us, and does not by itself resolve conflicts between competing claims or give guidance for justice at a distance. Others note that associating care with women risks reinforcing stereotypes, and that caring relations can involve exploitation of the carer. Many care theorists respond that care and justice are complementary, and that care ethics has a political dimension.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is thematic. Work in the framework on empathy, shared generative models and interpersonal inference concerns how agents model one another's states (see [[social_cognition]]), which is one empirical background to responsiveness to others. This is not a derivation of care ethics, which is a normative position.
