# Faith and Reason

## Overview
Faith and reason is a long-running topic in the philosophy of religion concerning whether religious belief can or must be supported by rational argument and evidence, and what trust or commitment adds beyond reasoning. The question arises in Jewish, Christian, Islamic and other traditions, as well as in secular critiques of religion. The positions below are described as their proponents hold them, and no stance is endorsed here.

## Main positions
- **Harmony.** Thomas Aquinas held that truths of faith and truths of reason cannot ultimately conflict because both come from God, though some doctrines are known only through revelation. Medieval thinkers including Maimonides and Averroes also sought accommodation between scripture and philosophy.
- **Evidentialism.** Clifford (1877) argued that it is wrong to believe anything on insufficient evidence. Applied to religion, this demands that theistic belief meet the same evidential standards as any other belief (see [[justification]]).
- **Fideism.** Fideists, often associated with Kierkegaard and sometimes with Pascal, hold that faith does not rest on, and may even transcend, rational justification.
- **Reformed epistemology.** Plantinga (2000) argues that belief in God can be properly basic, warranted without argument, if produced by properly functioning faculties aimed at truth.
- **Pragmatic approaches.** James held that in some live, forced and momentous options, evidence cannot decide and the will may legitimately enter, a view in the spirit of [[pragmatism]]. Critics of this view side with Clifford.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct link. Active Inference is a scientific framework that is silent on theological questions. A general connection exists only through the shared epistemological theme of how prior commitments and evidence jointly shape belief, which is a topic for [[epistemology]], and treating the framework as bearing on religious truth would go beyond what it supports.
