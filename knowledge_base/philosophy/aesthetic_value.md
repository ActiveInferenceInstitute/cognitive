# Aesthetic Value

## Overview
Aesthetic value is the kind of value attributed to objects, performances and experiences in virtue of how they appear, sound or are experienced, as in judgments that a landscape is beautiful, a poem moving or a design elegant. Philosophical aesthetics asks what such judgments express, whether they can be correct or incorrect, and how aesthetic value differs from moral, cognitive or instrumental value.

## Central questions
- **Objectivity and taste.** Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757) acknowledged variation in taste but argued that the joint verdicts of qualified critics, with delicate perception, practice and freedom from prejudice, provide a standard. Others hold that aesthetic judgments are expressions of feeling or response-dependent.
- **Kant's analysis.** In the *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790), Kant held that a judgment of beauty is based on disinterested pleasure, makes a claim to universal assent, and arises from the free play of imagination and understanding without a determinate concept.
- **Aesthetic properties.** Sibley (1959) argued that properties such as gracefulness or garishness depend on non-aesthetic features but are not governed by rules, and are grasped through taste or perceptual sensitivity.
- **Value and the artwork.** Positions include the view that aesthetic value lies in the intrinsic character of the experience an object affords (Beardsley's aesthetic-experience account) and contextual views that stress the artwork's history, category and relation to a tradition.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no established derivation of aesthetic value from the free energy principle. Some predictive-processing authors have speculated that aesthetic pleasure relates to the rate at which prediction error is reduced, or to a balance of predictability and surprise, and similar ideas appear in curiosity research. These proposals are hypotheses under empirical investigation and do not resolve the normative question of whether one judgment of taste is better than another. A descriptive account of why people respond to certain stimuli leaves the philosophical question of aesthetic correctness open.
