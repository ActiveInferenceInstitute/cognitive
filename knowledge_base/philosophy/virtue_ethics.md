# Virtue Ethics

## Overview
Virtue ethics takes the central question of morality to be what kind of person one should be, rather than which act or rule is correct. It evaluates agents by their stable dispositions, the virtues, such as courage, temperance, justice and honesty. Its main alternatives are [[consequentialism]], which judges acts by outcomes, and deontology, which judges them by duties.

## Aristotelian core
The classical source is Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*. Its key elements are:
- **Eudaimonia.** The human good is a flourishing life, understood as activity in accordance with excellence over a complete life, and not a feeling of pleasure.
- **Virtue as a mean.** Character traits are settled states between excess and deficiency, as courage lies between recklessness and cowardice. The mean is relative to circumstances and not a fixed arithmetical midpoint.
- **Habituation.** Virtues are acquired by practice, so that acting well repeatedly shapes a disposition to act well readily and with fitting feeling.
- **Practical wisdom (phronesis).** The virtues are unified by the capacity to perceive what a situation requires and to deliberate well about it. This capacity cannot be reduced to a rulebook.

## Modern revival and objections
Anscombe's 1958 essay criticised rule-centred moral philosophy and helped prompt the revival, followed by work of Foot, MacIntyre and Hursthouse. Objections include the charge that the theory is not action-guiding, since "do what the virtuous person would do" appears circular, and that the virtues depend on a contested account of human nature. Empirical critics, citing situationist research in social psychology, argue that behaviour depends more on circumstances than the notion of robust character traits allows. Virtue ethicists respond that the evidence addresses only some conceptions of character.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy, and a loose one. The Aristotelian emphasis on habituation resembles the way Active Inference models habit formation, in which repeated action strengthens priors over policies (see [[automaticity]]). Character can then be read as a set of entrenched preferences and policy priors. This is an interpretation offered for illustration. The framework is not a moral theory, and it does not decide between virtue, duty and outcome-based views.
