# Hermeneutic Circle

## Overview
The hermeneutic circle is the thesis, central to interpretation theory, that understanding proceeds in a circular or spiral way: the meaning of a part of a text, utterance or practice is understood by reference to the whole, while the whole is understood from its parts. Interpretation therefore never starts from nothing. The circle is usually presented as a feature of understanding to be worked through, not a logical fallacy to be avoided.

## Development
- **Early formulations.** Schleiermacher treated interpretation as a movement between the whole and its parts, and Dilthey extended hermeneutics to the human sciences as a method for understanding lived expressions of experience.
- **Heidegger.** In *Being and Time* (1927), Heidegger argued that interpretation always rests on a fore-structure of prior possession, sight and conception, so that the circle is not vicious but the way understanding is possible. The task is to enter it correctly, grounding fore-structures in the things themselves instead of fancies. This ties interpretation to [[dasein]]'s own existence.
- **Gadamer.** In *Truth and Method* (1960), Gadamer described understanding as shaped by the interpreter's prejudices or pre-judgments, which belong to a tradition and may be tested and revised in dialogue with the text, a process he described as a fusion of horizons.
- **Criticism.** Hirsch and others defended objective validity in interpretation against what they saw as relativism in Gadamer's view, and critical theorists such as Habermas questioned whether tradition can be trusted without critique. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on hermeneutics (George) surveys the history of the field.

## Relation to Active Inference
Some authors note a structural similarity between the circle and predictive processing, in which perception combines prior expectations (top-down) with incoming evidence (bottom-up) and revises the former in light of the latter (see [[top_down]], [[bottom_up]], [[prior_beliefs]]). Understanding in both cases presupposes prior structure that is changed by what is understood. This is an analogy drawn by commentators, not a formal equivalence. Hermeneutic understanding concerns meaning within a tradition, while predictive processing describes statistical inference, and the extent to which the second can capture the first is debated.
