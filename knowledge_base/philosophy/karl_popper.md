# Karl Popper

## Overview
Karl Popper (1902-1994) was an Austrian-born philosopher who spent most of his career in Britain, at the London School of Economics. He was close to, but never a member of, the Vienna Circle, and became one of its most influential critics. His principal works include *Logik der Forschung* (1934, English *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, 1959), *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) and *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963).

## Main ideas
- **Falsifiability.** Scientific theories are distinguished by being open to refutation (see [[falsification]]).
- **Critical rationalism.** Knowledge grows through conjecture and criticism. Popper rejected the view that induction justifies belief, holding that theories are never established but only survive tests, an attitude close to [[fallibilism]].
- **Corroboration.** A theory that withstands severe tests is corroborated, a degree of testing history and not a probability of truth.
- **Verisimilitude.** Theories can be closer to the truth than their rivals, although his formal definition was later shown to be defective.
- **The open society.** In political philosophy he defended liberal democracy and criticised historicist and totalitarian doctrines.
- **Three worlds.** Later he distinguished physical objects, mental states, and the objective contents of thought as separate domains.

His conception of science has been criticised by Kuhn, Lakatos and Feyerabend, and by Bayesian philosophers who defend inductive confirmation (see [[kuhn_paradigms]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
Several authors describe perception and cognition as hypothesis testing, in a line running from Helmholtz through Gregory (1980), who compared perceptions to hypotheses, to descriptions of the brain as a scientist testing models. Active Inference extends this by having the agent select actions that resolve uncertainty. The Popperian echo is real but partial. The framework is Bayesian, treating belief updating as accumulation of evidence, which stands in tension with Popper's rejection of inductive support, and the analogy concerns the shape of the process, not a shared theory of justification.
