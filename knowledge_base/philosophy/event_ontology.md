# Event Ontology

## Overview
Event ontology is the part of metaphysics that asks whether events, the things that happen, such as a wedding, an explosion, or a stabbing, are genuine entities, and if so what kind. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that human perception, action, language and thought show a prima facie commitment to such entities, and that even among philosophers who are realists about events there is significant disagreement about their nature. Events matter for the analysis of causation, action, and the semantics of verbs and tenses, and they are central to process-oriented metaphysics (see [[process_philosophy]]).

## Events and other categories
- **Events and objects.** Objects are said to exist, whereas events occur or happen. Objects have relatively crisp spatial boundaries, whereas events tend to have crisp temporal boundaries and tolerate co-location. A common view is that objects endure, being wholly present at each time, while events perdure, having different parts at different times. Philosophers who treat objects as four-dimensional, including Whitehead, Russell, Quine and Lewis, would draw no deep distinction, and Goodman put the point by calling an object a monotonous event and an event an unstable object.
- **Events and facts.** If events are fine-grained like facts, then the death of Caesar and his violent death are two events. If they are particulars that can be redescribed, they are one event under two descriptions.
- **Events and properties.** On Kim's property-exemplification account, an event is the exemplification of a property by an object at a time. Because the structure is part of the event, a difference in the constitutive property yields a different event, making events nearly as fine-grained as facts.

## Identity conditions
Quine's slogan "no entity without identity" makes the criterion for event identity a central issue. Thalberg's terminology divides positions into radical unifiers, who treat events as coarse-grained as ordinary objects, and radical multipliers, who treat them as fine-grained as facts, with moderate positions in between. Davidson (1969) proposed a causal criterion: events are identical just when they have the same causes and the same effects. This allows the stabbing and the killing of Caesar to be one event. As critics noted, the criterion is circular if causes and effects are themselves events, and Davidson later rejected it. Other writers hold that identity questions are largely semantic, depending on the context in which event-referring expressions are used.

## Events and process
For process philosophers, the move from enduring substances to events is basic. Whitehead's [[actual_occasions]] are momentary events with an experiential character, and Whitehead describes an event in the general sense as a nexus of occasions. Contemporary analytic event ontology does not usually adopt this experiential claim, although both traditions share the idea that change is not secondary to things. See also [[internal_relations]].

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is thematic. Generative models in Active Inference are often framed over trajectories, sequences of states, and transitions, which fits an event-structured view of the world. Active Inference does not itself settle the metaphysical question of whether events are coarse- or fine-grained, and the modelling choice of a state space is separate from claims about identity conditions.
