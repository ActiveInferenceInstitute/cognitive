# Explanatory Gap

## Overview
The explanatory gap is the apparent gulf between a physical description of a conscious state and an explanation of why that state has the subjective character it does. Levine (1983) introduced the term. He noted that an identity statement such as "heat is molecular motion" is intelligible: knowing the microphysics lets one see why heat has the macroscopic properties it does. By contrast, "pain is the firing of C-fibres" leaves it open why such firing should feel as pain does, or feel like anything.

## The argument
- A good reductive explanation lets one deduce, or at least see the necessity of, the higher-level facts from the lower-level facts.
- For conscious experience, no such deduction seems available even given complete neuroscience.
- Levine's conclusion is epistemic: the gap is a limit on explanation. Whether it reflects a metaphysical gap, in which experience is not physical, is a further question that he left open.

This differs from the stronger anti-physicalist claims built on related intuitions, such as the zombie argument, which move from conceivability to metaphysical possibility. The [[hard_problem]] of Chalmers (1995) is closely related and is sometimes treated as the same issue; it is more usually framed as a problem about why physical functions are accompanied by experience at all.

## Responses
Type-B physicalists accept the explanatory gap but deny a metaphysical one, usually by appealing to special features of phenomenal concepts. Type-A physicalists, such as Dennett, deny that there is a further fact to explain once the functions are accounted for. McGinn (1989) argued that the gap reflects a cognitive limit of human minds, a view known as mysterianism. Dualists and panpsychists take the gap to indicate a real ontological difference.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference and predictive processing supply mechanistic accounts of perception, interoception and affect, and some authors use them to map properties of conscious experience onto computational features, an approach sometimes called the real problem of consciousness. Such work narrows what is left unexplained but does not claim to close the gap, and most proponents of the framework do not say it does. The relationship is that of an empirical programme working alongside an open philosophical question.
