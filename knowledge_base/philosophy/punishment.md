# Punishment

## Overview
In the psychology of learning, punishment is any consequence that follows a behaviour and decreases the probability that the behaviour recurs. The definition is functional: an event counts as punishment only if the behaviour weakens. This operant sense, set out by Skinner, is distinct from the legal and moral sense of punishment as the imposition of a penalty on a wrongdoer, discussed under [[retributive_justice]].

## Forms
- **Positive punishment** adds an aversive stimulus after a response, such as a mild shock after a lever press.
- **Negative punishment** removes a desirable stimulus after a response, such as loss of a privilege or of a reward opportunity.

Punishment should be distinguished from negative reinforcement, in which removing an aversive stimulus increases a response. Punishment decreases behaviour and reinforcement increases it, regardless of whether the stimulus is added or removed.

## Determinants and effects
Experimental work by Estes (1944) and later by Azrin and Holz (1966) examined variables influencing the effectiveness of punishment. Reviews from this literature identify the immediacy of the consequence, its intensity, its consistency, and the availability of alternative rewarded responses as important. Suppression can be temporary, and punishment may produce side effects, including escape or avoidance of the punishing agent, emotional responses, and generalisation to other situations. For these reasons many applied behaviour analysts favour reinforcing alternative behaviour over punishment, although views on its appropriate use differ across settings.

## Relation to Active Inference
In reinforcement learning, punishment corresponds to negative reward and to negative prediction errors that reduce the value of an action (see [[reinforcement_learning]], [[reward_prediction_error]]). In Active Inference there is no separate reward signal. Aversive outcomes are outcomes with low prior preference, and policies expected to lead to them have high expected free energy and are chosen less often. Friston, Daunizeau and Kiebel (2009) argued that behaviour usually ascribed to reinforcement learning can be recast in this way. This reformulation is a modelling choice, and empirical comparison of the two accounts continues.
