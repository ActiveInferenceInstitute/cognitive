# Coherence Theory

## Overview
Coherence theories hold that what makes a belief true, or justified, is its fitting together with a wider system of beliefs, instead of its matching an independent fact. Two theses should be distinguished. The coherence theory of truth, associated with British idealists such as Bradley and later with Blanshard (1939), identifies truth with membership in a maximally coherent whole. Coherentism about justification, defended by BonJour (1985) among others, makes the epistemic status of a belief depend on its coherence with the rest of a person's beliefs.

## Core ideas and objections
- **Holism.** Beliefs are justified together, as in Neurath's image of sailors rebuilding a ship at sea, not by resting on a privileged base. This is the main alternative to [[foundationalism]].
- **Coherence.** Usually understood as more than consistency: it includes mutual explanation and probabilistic support among beliefs, and is sometimes given a formal Bayesian measure.
- **Isolation objection.** A fictional but internally tight story is coherent without being true. Coherentists reply that the system must also fit observation and be the best available explanation of experience.
- **Plurality objection.** Russell noted that several incompatible systems might each be coherent, so coherence alone cannot select among them.
- **Contrast.** Unlike [[correspondence_theory]], coherence accounts are internalist about what makes a belief acceptable, though some pair a coherence test with a correspondence definition of truth.

## Relation to Active Inference
In a hierarchical generative model every belief is constrained by neighbours above and below, so evidence is evaluated holistically, which resembles coherentist justification. The free energy objective, however, is not pure coherence: it includes an accuracy term tying the model to sensory data and a complexity term penalising needless departure from priors, so the model is anchored to observations. The parallel is an analogy for how belief systems are structured, not an endorsement of coherentism as a theory of truth.
