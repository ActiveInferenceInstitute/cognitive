# Hard Problem

## Overview
The hard problem of consciousness is the question of why and how physical processing gives rise to subjective experience. Chalmers (1995) distinguished it from the "easy" problems, which concern the explanation of cognitive functions such as discrimination of stimuli, integration of information, verbal report, attention and the control of behaviour. These problems are called easy not because they are simple but because they are the kind of problem that standard methods of cognitive science and neuroscience can in principle solve by specifying a mechanism. The hard problem remains even after all the functions are explained, because it asks why performing them is accompanied by experience at all. Nagel (1974) had earlier expressed the same worry in terms of there being "something it is like" to be a conscious organism.

## Responses
- **Reductive and deflationary.** Dennett and others argue that once the easy problems are solved, nothing further needs explaining, or that the intuition that something remains is itself mistaken. Illusionism holds that phenomenal consciousness, as normally conceived, does not exist.
- **Non-reductive.** Chalmers proposes that experience is a fundamental feature of the world, along with space, time and mass, linked to physical processes by psychophysical laws. Panpsychism and Russellian monism (see [[panpsychism]]) offer related views.
- **Mysterian.** McGinn proposes that human minds are cognitively closed to the solution (see [[explanatory_gap]]).
- **Meta-problem.** Chalmers (2018) suggests explaining why we find the problem seem hard, which can be addressed with the methods of cognitive science.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference can address versions of the easy problems, such as how brains infer causes of sensation and regulate bodily states, and some researchers use it to account for features of experience, such as the sense of presence or emotion as inference about interoceptive states. These contributions are better described as work on the "real problem", which seeks to explain properties of consciousness instead of dissolving the hard problem. Active Inference does not claim to solve the hard problem, and the framework's compatibility with several metaphysical positions, from physicalist to dual-aspect, reflects this.
