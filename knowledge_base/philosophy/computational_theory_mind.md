# Computational Theory of Mind

## Overview
The computational theory of mind (CTM) holds that the mind is a computational system, and that mental processes such as reasoning, perception and decision are computations. Rescorla (2015) surveys it as a central framework in cognitive science. The claim is usually combined with [[functionalism]]: mental states are individuated by their causal-computational roles instead of by the stuff that realises them.

## Varieties
- **Classical CTM**: mental processes are rule-governed manipulations of symbols in a language of thought, with syntax that mirrors semantic relations. Fodor (1975) is the leading proponent. The computing system is modelled on a Turing machine.
- **Connectionist CTM**: cognition is computation in networks of simple interconnected units, with distributed representations and learned weights rather than explicit symbolic rules.
- **Bayesian and probabilistic CTM**: cognition is approximate probabilistic inference over structured models.
- **Mechanistic accounts**: Piccinini (2015) argues that physical systems compute in virtue of their functional organisation, so that computational explanation is a kind of mechanistic explanation.

## Objections
Searle (1980) argued with the Chinese room scenario that manipulating symbols according to rules is not sufficient for understanding, since syntax alone does not yield semantics. Others argue that computation is too cheap, as nearly any physical system can be mapped onto some computation, unless a constraint on implementation is added. Critics from [[embodied_cognition]] and [[enactivism]] reject the idea that cognition consists in internal representation manipulation. Proponents reply that these objections target particular formulations, and the debate over which notion of computation is relevant remains open.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference is formulated as a computational account, in which brains implement approximate Bayesian inference using generative models, so it sits within a broad computationalism and in the Bayesian family. The Free Energy Principle itself is a mathematical claim about self-organising systems and does not require that the system manipulate symbols. Whether active inference models commit to internal representations is disputed, and some authors give them a non-representational reading.
