# Copenhagen Interpretation

## Overview
The Copenhagen interpretation was the first general attempt to say what quantum mechanics tells us about the world of atoms. Its main architect was Niels Bohr, with important contributions from Werner Heisenberg and Max Born. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that neither Bohr nor Heisenberg used the phrase as a joint name for their ideas, and that the two never fully agreed on how to read the formalism. Today the label is mostly associated with indeterminism, Bohr's correspondence principle, Born's statistical interpretation of the wave function and Bohr's complementarity.

## Main ingredients
- **Statistical reading of the wave function.** Born (1926) proposed that the squared modulus $|\psi(x)|^2$ gives the probability density for the outcome of a position measurement, so the theory predicts frequencies of results and not individual events.
- **Indeterminacy.** Heisenberg's 1927 analysis showed that position and momentum cannot both be sharply defined; in its modern form the spreads obey $\sigma_x\,\sigma_p \ge \hbar/2$. After the 1935 paper of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen, Bohr spoke of an "indeterminacy" relation with ontological consequences, where he had earlier often said "uncertainty", which suggests a limit on knowledge.
- **Complementarity.** Bohr introduced it in his 1927 Como lecture, published in 1928. Descriptions that depend on mutually exclusive experimental arrangements, such as a precise position or a precise momentum, cannot be combined in one description but are jointly needed for a full account.
- **Classical concepts.** Experimental results must be reported in ordinary, classical terms, so the measuring apparatus is described classically even though the atomic object is not. For Bohr a phenomenon includes the whole experimental arrangement.
- **Measurement.** Textbook versions add von Neumann's projection postulate, in which a measurement collapses the state. Wigner (1967) later proposed that the mind of the observer causes the collapse.

## Was there one interpretation?
Howard (2004) argued that the term was coined by Heisenberg in 1955 and that a unitary "Copenhagen interpretation" did not exist before then; his exposition, with its prominent role for the observer, became the target of critics such as Bohm. Later scholarship stresses that Bohr and Heisenberg diverged, and that Bohr has been read as an instrumentalist, an anti-realist and a realist of several kinds, so any claim of the form "the Copenhagen interpretation says" needs a named author.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. Active Inference is not a theory of quantum measurement. Its quantum-flavoured extensions, discussed under [[measurement_problem]], treat an observer as a bounded system separated from its environment by a boundary, which resembles the Copenhagen insistence that a measurement needs a specified apparatus, but this resemblance does not make Active Inference a version of Copenhagen. Operationalist themes, in which concepts are tied to procedures (see [[operationalism]]), are the more direct link.
