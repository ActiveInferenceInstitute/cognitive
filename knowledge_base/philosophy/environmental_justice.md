# Environmental Justice

## Overview
Environmental justice is both a social movement and a field of normative and empirical research. It concerns how environmental risks, such as pollution and hazardous waste, and environmental goods, such as clean air, water and green space, are distributed among social groups, and who takes part in the decisions that shape that distribution. The movement is usually traced to the United States, where local resistance to the siting of a hazardous waste facility in Warren County, North Carolina, in 1982 drew national attention. A U.S. General Accounting Office study of 1983, requested after those protests, and a 1987 report by the United Church of Christ Commission for Racial Justice, *Toxic Wastes and Race in the United States*, reported that hazardous waste sites were disproportionately located in or near communities of colour. Robert Bullard's *Dumping in Dixie* (1990) was an early book-length treatment. The sources for this history are partly advocacy accounts, and the details of the events are reported with some variation.

## Theoretical content
David Schlosberg, in *Defining Environmental Justice* (2007), argues that a theory of environmental justice must be broader than distribution alone. It includes:
- **Distribution** of environmental harms and benefits.
- **Recognition** of the standing of individuals and communities in the moral and political community, whose absence can underlie unjust distribution.
- **Capabilities**, the conditions needed for people's functioning and flourishing.
- **Participation**, inclusion in political decision-making.

Schlosberg holds that these elements are interrelated and interdependent and that a theory of environmental justice includes, and goes beyond, distributive conceptions of justice. The concept links to [[distributive_justice]], which concerns the allocation of benefits and burdens, and to [[intergenerational_justice]], which extends the question to future people.

## Connections and debates
Environmental justice overlaps with [[environmental_ethics]] but differs in emphasis: much of environmental ethics asks about the moral standing of nature, whereas environmental justice asks about fairness among human groups in their relation to the environment. It draws on the analysis of overlapping disadvantage in [[intersectionality]] and on work on racism in law and society in [[critical_race_theory]]. The scope of the concept, for example whether to extend it to nonhuman animals or to global and climate contexts, is a matter of continuing discussion.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is indirect. Active Inference treats an organism as coupled to an environment it both senses and shapes, and ecological applications model such coupling in formal terms, but environmental justice is a normative question about fairness that the framework does not address. The two meet only in the shared subject of how environments affect those who live in them.
