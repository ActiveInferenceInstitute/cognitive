# Anthropic Principles

## Overview
Anthropic principles concern how the existence of observers constrains what can be observed. Brandon Carter (1974) introduced the idea to counter the assumption that humans occupy a typical position in the universe, noting that our situation is necessarily privileged to the extent compatible with our existence as observers. Barrow and Tipler (1986) gave the main systematic treatment and the standard terminology. The principles are controversial, and their status as science or philosophy is disputed.

## Forms
- **Weak anthropic principle**: the observed values of physical and cosmological quantities are not equally probable, but take values restricted by the requirement that carbon-based life can exist and that the universe be old enough to have produced it. This is an observation-selection effect and is widely regarded as a logical truism.
- **Strong anthropic principle**: the universe must have properties that allow life to develop at some stage. This is stronger and much more disputed, because it can be read as teleological or as a claim about why the constants are as they are.
- **Participatory and final versions** proposed by Wheeler and by Barrow and Tipler are speculative and are not part of mainstream physics.

## Uses and criticism
A frequently cited use is Weinberg's (1987) argument that, if the cosmological constant varies across a multiverse, observers should typically find a value of the same order as the largest one compatible with galaxy formation, since a much larger value would prevent galaxies from forming. This is an example of anthropic reasoning as a prediction, though its force depends on assumptions about a measure over universes. Critics argue that appeals to a multiverse are not independently testable, that anthropic explanation can excuse a lack of deeper theory, and that fine-tuning claims depend on contested probability measures. Bostrom (2002) analysed the underlying problem of reasoning under observation selection effects, including the choice of reference class, and showed that different assumptions yield different conclusions.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is analogical. The Free Energy Principle observes that systems that persist must occupy a limited set of characteristic states, so existing systems are in some sense selected by their persistence. This resembles anthropic selection but concerns individual organisms, not universes, and no derivation links the two. The shared theme is that conditioning on existence changes what one expects to find.
