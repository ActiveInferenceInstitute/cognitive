# Charvaka Materialism

## Overview
Carvaka, also called Lokayata or Indian materialism, is the name for the heterodox current in Indian philosophy that rejected supernaturalism. According to the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, it rejects an immaterial soul, gods and the afterlife and takes a naturalistic approach, and it has long been regarded as the most radical of the Indian philosophical systems. "Lokayata" literally means "philosophy of the people", and the school is also associated with the figure Brhaspati and with the name Carvaka. Its place in the tradition has been marginal: other schools called it *nastika*, a term of rebuke for a school that has breached acceptable religious and ethical beliefs.

## Sources
There are no surviving doctrinal texts of the school. Only fragments of the Brhaspati Sutra remain, so what is known comes largely from the criticisms of opponents, such as the summary of Carvaka views in Madhava's *Sarva-darsana-samgraha*, as cited in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. Scholars have therefore disagreed about its origins, its coherence as a school, and which of its reported positions its own adherents held. What follows describes the views as reported.

## Main positions
- **Epistemology.** Carvaka recognised perception as a source of knowledge and rejected inference and testimony as sources. The reason given in the Stanford Encyclopedia is that inference depends on generalisations of the form "all F are G" that go beyond perceptual evidence, so that no one can know them, and that testimony presupposes that the speaker tells the truth and is open to the same objection. Other Indian schools responded that the sceptic himself relies on such generalisations, and Buddhist and Nyaya philosophers turned the Carvaka point about induction into an argument for fallibilism about inference. Some accounts describe a more moderate position that allowed inference from premises grounded in experience, but this is contested.
- **Ontology.** Reality consists of the four elements, earth, water, fire and air. Consciousness arises from their combination in the body and does not survive its dissolution. The school denied god, a disembodied soul and the afterlife.
- **Cosmology.** There is no creator and no teleology, and the principles of karma and fate are rejected. The fundamental notion is *svabhava*, the nature of things.
- **Ethics.** Opponents characterised the school as hedonistic or egoistic, treating pleasure as the good. How far this reflects the school's own position is uncertain, since the evidence comes from critics.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is thematic and slight. The emphasis on perception as the only warranted source resembles an empiricist stance in which beliefs are answerable to sensory evidence (see [[empiricism]]), and the objection that inference outstrips evidence echoes the problem of induction, which Bayesian approaches address by treating generalisations as probabilistic. Carvaka does not use a probabilistic framework, and the comparison is an analogy only.
