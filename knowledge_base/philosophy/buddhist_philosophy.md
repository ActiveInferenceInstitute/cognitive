# Buddhist Philosophy

## Overview
Buddhist philosophy is the body of analysis developed within Buddhist traditions from the teachings attributed to the Buddha in the first millennium BCE. It addresses the causes of suffering, the nature of persons and perception, and the path to liberation. It is diverse, with Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana lineages and many scholastic schools, and it combines metaphysics, epistemology, ethics and meditative practice.

## Core teachings
- **Four noble truths.** Life involves suffering (dukkha), suffering has causes in craving and ignorance, it can cease, and there is a path leading to that cessation.
- **Impermanence and non-self.** All conditioned phenomena are impermanent, and what is called a self is analysed as a changing collection of processes (the five aggregates) without a permanent core (anatta).
- **Dependent origination.** Phenomena arise in dependence on causes and conditions and not as self-existent things.
- **Emptiness.** In the Madhyamaka school, associated with Nagarjuna, things lack intrinsic nature (svabhava), which Garfield (1995) renders as a middle way between substantialism and nihilism.
- **Yogacara.** This school analyses experience as a process of mind and its constructive activity.
- **Epistemology.** Dignaga and Dharmakirti developed influential theories of perception and inference.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an active research and dialogue area but is not a derivation. Varela, Thompson and Rosch (1991) opened the connection to [[enactivism]], and later work applies predictive-processing and Active Inference models to meditation, proposing that practices alter precision and the self-model, which would bear on the Buddhist analysis of non-self (see [[meditation]], [[self_awareness]]). The correspondences between technical terms are proposed by individual authors, remain debated, and should not be read as showing that the traditions say the same thing.
