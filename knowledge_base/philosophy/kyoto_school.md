# Kyoto School

## Overview
The Kyoto School (Kyoto-gakuha) is a group of twentieth-century Japanese philosophers who drew on the intellectual and spiritual traditions of East Asia, Mahayana Buddhism in particular, together with the methods and content of Western philosophy. Its founder was Nishida Kitaro (1870-1945), whose first major book, *An Inquiry into the Good* (1911), led to a post in the philosophy department of Kyoto University. The two other most prominent members are Tanabe Hajime (1885-1962) and Nishitani Keiji (1900-1990). The name was applied partly from outside, and scholars define membership differently: one working definition describes an intellectual network centred on Nishida and Tanabe and formed by those directly influenced by them, a looseness that leaves room for serious disagreement among members.

## Absolute nothingness
The shared central concept is "absolute nothingness". It is not simple privation of being, which the school calls relative nothingness, but the formless ground in which both being and subjectivity arise. The members developed it in different ways.
- **Nishida.** From the mid-1920s he placed reality in the "place" (*basho*) of absolute nothingness, which envelops both the subjective, noetic side and the objective, noematic side of experience. Reality is described as a dynamic "self-identity of absolute contradiction", so that nothingness and being are not separate spheres.
- **Tanabe.** After criticising Nishida, he developed a "logic of species" and a view of absolute nothingness as absolute mediation that is experienced as other-power. After the Second World War he wrote a philosophy of repentance, called metanoetics.
- **Nishitani.** In *Religion and Nothingness* he describes three fields, being, nihility and emptiness, and treats the nihility met in the experience of nihilism as a threshold to the emptiness of Buddhist thought.
- **Ueda Shizuteru**, of the third generation, developed the idea through the linguistic horizons of a world opened within an "empty expanse".

## Politics and criticism
Kyoto School members wrote and spoke during the 1930s and 1940s, and their wartime essays and symposia have been the subject of sustained controversy. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry treats this at length, including the strategy of "cooperative resistance", the way some members' remarks served nationalist ends, and the postwar reflections of Tanabe and Nishitani. A fair account of the school keeps both its philosophical contributions and this history in view.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. Discussions of the self in Active Inference, where a self-model is part of an agent's generative model, are sometimes compared with the Buddhist theme of a self that is not a substantial self, and the Kyoto School's discussion of a "self that is not a self" is one place such a comparison could start. Neither framework derives from the other, and absolute nothingness is a metaphysical and religious notion with no counterpart among the quantities of Active Inference.
