# Samkhya Dualism

## Overview
Samkhya (also spelled Sankhya or Samkhya) is one of the major "orthodox" Indian philosophies (see [[hindu_philosophy]]). In the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry by Ferenc Ruzsa it is described as having been, two millennia ago, the representative Hindu philosophy. Its classical formulation is Ishvarakrishna's *Samkhya-Karika* (c. 350 CE), a condensed account in seventy-two verses. It is a strong Indian example of metaphysical dualism (compare [[dualism]]) but, unlike many Western counterparts, it is atheistic.

## The two principles
- **Prakriti.** Nature is single, eternal, unconscious, productive and purposeful, and it works for the purposes of persons. It is made of three *gunas*, which are not ordinary qualities but complex aspects of anything material: *sattva* (light, intelligence), *rajas* (change, energy) and *tamas* (inertia, heaviness). In every phenomenon all three are present and they interact. The entry suggests a modern paraphrase as coherence or information, energy, and inertia.
- **Purusha.** Persons are numerous, eternal, conscious, and essentially inactive. A *purusha* is a witness that enjoys and observes nature's activity without causing it, and it has no *gunas*. Arguments for its existence include that complex structures such as the body must serve something other than themselves, and that conscious features cannot be explained by the *gunas* alone.
- **Causation.** Samkhya holds *sat-karya-vada*, the "existent effect" theory: an effect is already present in its material cause, as a pot is in clay. This opposes the Nyaya view (see [[nyaya_logic]]).

## Evolution and liberation
Creation is a conjunction of prakriti and persons. The text compares them to a blind man carrying a lame man through the wilderness. From root-nature arise intellect (*buddhi*), ego (*ahamkara*), the eleven powers including mind (*manas*), the five subtle sensibilia, and the gross elements. The intellect, ego and mind together form the material psyche, which transmigrates as a subtle body, while the *purusha* itself only watches. Suffering ends through knowledge of the fundamental difference between nature and person, reached by practising "this is not me, this is not mine, I am not this" with respect to every element of nature. The result is isolation (*kaivalya*) of the person.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. Samkhya's separation of a passive witness from an active material process that includes the intellect and ego has been compared with discussions of the observer and self-model in philosophy of mind (see [[philosophy_of_mind]]). The comparison should not be pressed. Active Inference does not posit a non-material witness, and Samkhya's persons are not part of any model.
