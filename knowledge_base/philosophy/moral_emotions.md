# Moral Emotions

## Overview
Moral emotions are affective states elicited by events judged to be morally relevant, whether the agent's own conduct or that of others. Haidt (2003) distinguishes self-conscious emotions such as guilt, shame and embarrassment, other-condemning emotions such as anger, contempt and disgust, other-praising emotions such as gratitude and elevation, and other-suffering emotions such as compassion. In the emotional-processing literature they are often described as value-based, since they depend on appraisals of what matters to oneself and to others.

## Functions
Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek (2007) review evidence that these emotions guide behaviour in everyday life. Guilt is typically associated with a focus on a specific act and motivation to repair harm, whereas shame is associated with a global negative evaluation of the self and a tendency to withdraw. The relative moral benefit of the two has been debated. Empathic concern is linked to prosocial behaviour, while moral anger is linked to punishing or confronting violations. Evidence is mainly correlational and based on self-report, and effects vary with measurement and context.

## Philosophical positions
Sentimentalists in the tradition of Hume hold that moral judgement is grounded in feeling. Rationalists in the tradition of Kant hold that it rests on reason, with emotion as a consequence or an aid. Contemporary views take intermediate positions, and empirical work on the order of judgement and emotion remains contested. Strawson (1962) argued that reactive attitudes such as resentment and gratitude are central to how people hold one another responsible, a theme taken up in discussions of [[retributive_justice]].

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is a developing research direction, not an established derivation. Emotions are modelled in this literature as inferences about the causes of bodily and social states, with valence linked to expected progress in minimising free energy. Extending that account to moral emotions would treat them as inferences about social norms and others' expectations. Specific moral applications remain largely conceptual.
