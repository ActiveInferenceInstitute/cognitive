# Temporal Parts

## Overview
Temporal parts are the proposed time-slices of an object: the part of a thing that exists at a single moment or during a restricted interval. Whether ordinary objects have such parts is central to the metaphysics of persistence, which asks how a thing can exist at different times and remain the same while changing. The debate is summarised by Hawley in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and in her book How Things Persist (2001).

## Competing views
- **Perdurantism.** An object persists by having different temporal parts at different times, as a road extends through space by having different spatial parts. The object is a four-dimensional "worm" extended in time. This view is defended by Lewis (1986) and Sider (2001) and is associated with a block or eternalist conception of time.
- **Endurantism.** An object persists by being wholly present at each moment of its existence and has no temporal parts. Endurantists must say how a thing can have incompatible properties, such as being bent and straight, at different times, and answers include indexing properties to times or treating properties as relations to times.
- **Stage theory.** Ordinary objects are instantaneous stages, and statements about persistence are made true by temporal counterpart relations among stages. Sider defends a version of this view.

## Arguments
Perdurantists point to the problem of temporary intrinsics and to arguments from vagueness and from puzzles of coincidence, such as a statue and its clay. Endurantists object that temporal parts conflict with ordinary beliefs about change and with the thought that objects are fully present in time, and some argue that such parts are metaphysically extravagant. Van Inwagen (1990) is among those who have criticised the doctrine. See Haslanger and Kurtz (2006) for a collection of readings. The debate also interacts with the metaphysics of persons ([[personal_identity]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an analogy, not a derivation. The free energy principle characterises persisting systems as those that maintain themselves within characteristic states, a dynamical account of persistence that does not take a position on endurantism or perdurantism. Philosophers of biology and process philosophers may find the account closer to process views ([[process_philosophy]]). The metaphysical question is not settled by the framework.
