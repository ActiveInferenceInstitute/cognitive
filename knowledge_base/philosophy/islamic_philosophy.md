# Islamic Philosophy

## Overview
"Islamic philosophy" covers the philosophical thought produced in the Islamic world from the ninth century onward, largely but not exclusively in Arabic. Several distinct traditions are involved. *Falsafa* is the tradition rooted in the Greek-into-Arabic translation movement and is indebted to Greek Neoplatonism and Aristotelianism. *Kalam*, a rational theology, developed largely independently of Greek philosophy, with its own conceptual framework and problems. Post-classical metaphysics was shaped by the competing methods of Fakhr al-Din al-Razi and Suhrawardi and by a current of ontological monism. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats these through a series of entries on Arabic and Islamic metaphysics, mind, religion, language and logic, and other topics.

## Falsafa and the Greek sources
Cristina D'Ancona traces how, before Islam, Syriac-speaking Christian communities had already assimilated Greek philosophy, especially Aristotle's logic and Porphyry's *Isagoge*. Under the early 'Abbasid caliphate a large translation movement, in which Syriac Christians took part, made much of the Greek philosophical and scientific corpus available in Arabic. Al-Kindi (d. after 870) and al-Farabi (d. 950-1) are the main protagonists of early falsafa in the entry by Benevich. In metaphysics, Aristotle's *Metaphysics* was the centrepiece for centuries, Amos Bertolacci reports, and the Neoplatonic *Theology of Aristotle* and *Book of Causes* extended the Aristotelian corpus. Avicenna's (Ibn Sina, d. 1037) encyclopedia *The Cure* (*al-Shifa*) incorporated the text and doctrine of Aristotle's *Metaphysics* so successfully that Avicenna's own metaphysical works gradually superseded it as the text to be copied and commented upon, according to Bertolacci.

## Essence, existence and later metaphysics
Fedor Benevich describes five episodes in the debate on essence and existence: early falsafa, classical kalam (where Basran Mu'tazilites held that "the non-existent is a thing"), Avicenna's own analysis, which set the framework for later discussion, the reactions of Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (d. 1210) and Suhrawardi (d. 1191), and a trend of ontological monism. The monist trend began with Ibn 'Arabi (d. 1240) and Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi (d. 1274) and culminated in Mulla Sadra (d. 1640).

## Faith and reason
McGinnis and Acar distinguish three positions on whether unaided reason can discover religious truths: that it is sufficient, that it discovers the basics but needs a prophet for the rest, and that it cannot reach religious knowledge at all. They give Abu Bakr al-Razi (865-925) as a purported representative of the first and report further positions on interpreting scripture and on reconciling religion with philosophy (see [[faith_and_reason]]). The entry also covers arguments for the existence of God, creation as emanation or as origination, and prophecy.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct relation. A historical link exists through the transmission of Aristotelian and Neoplatonic psychology, with discussions of the soul and intellect, to later European philosophy of mind (see [[philosophy_of_mind]]). Any connection to the free energy principle would be a modern interpretive reading and not a claim made by the tradition.
