# Facticity

## Overview
Facticity names the given, unchosen character of a person's situation: one's birth, body, historical period, past and the fact of existing at all. The term has a technical role in the existential phenomenology of Heidegger and Sartre, where it marks the way human existence is not a free-floating subject but is always already situated, and yet is not reducible to the facts about it.

## Heidegger and Sartre
- **Heidegger.** In *Being and Time* (1927), facticity (*Faktizität*) is the character of Dasein as thrown (*geworfen*) into a world it did not choose, finding itself disposed in a mood and bound up with entities in the world, while still having to take over its being. A distinction is drawn between the factuality of objects and the facticity of Dasein, whose "that it is" is an issue for it. See [[dasein]].
- **Sartre.** In *Being and Nothingness* (1943), facticity is the aspect of the for-itself that is given: its body, past, social position and place. Sartre argues that consciousness is free in how it takes up its facticity, and that bad faith involves denying either freedom or facticity (see [[being_and_nothingness]]).
- **Beauvoir and others.** De Beauvoir and later feminist and critical phenomenologists used the idea to analyse how embodiment and social position condition what choices are available.

## Significance
Facticity is used to mark a middle position between two extremes: that human conduct is wholly determined by circumstances and that it is unconstrained by them. It thereby bears on debates about [[free_will]] and [[determinism]], though the existential authors do not frame it as a thesis about causal determinism. It is also linked to [[authenticity]], which is said to require acknowledging one's facticity without being defined by it. Inwood (1999) gives the Heideggerian usage.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no formal link. A loose analogy has been suggested between thrownness and the fact that an agent begins with prior beliefs fixed by evolution and development, to which its inference is then applied, but this is a metaphor and not an established mapping, and the existential accounts concern lived experience, not mechanisms.
