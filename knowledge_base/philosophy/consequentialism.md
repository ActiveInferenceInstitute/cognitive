# Consequentialism

## Overview
Consequentialism is the view that the moral status of an action, rule or institution depends solely on the value of its consequences. An act is right if it produces outcomes at least as good as any available alternative. The theory needs two ingredients: an account of what is good (welfare, preference satisfaction, or a plural list of goods) and a principle that ranks options by that good. It is usually contrasted with deontological views, which hold that some acts are constrained regardless of outcome, and with [[virtue_ethics]], which centres on character.

## Main forms
- **Classical utilitarianism.** Associated with Bentham, Mill and Sidgwick, it identifies the good with pleasure or happiness and requires maximising the aggregate across all affected beings, each counted equally.
- **Act and rule versions.** Act consequentialism evaluates each individual act. Rule consequentialism evaluates acts by whether they conform to rules whose general acceptance would produce the best outcomes.
- **Satisficing and prioritarian variants.** These relax strict maximisation or weight benefits to the worse off more heavily.
- **Agent-neutrality.** Standard versions weigh everyone's interests equally, which critics say conflicts with special obligations to family and friends.

## Standard objections
Critics argue that the theory is excessively demanding, since it permits no ordinary partiality. They also claim it can justify sacrificing an innocent person to benefit many, and that it ignores the separateness of persons, a charge pressed by Rawls. Williams argued that it undermines personal integrity by treating an agent's commitments as just another source of outcomes. Defenders reply with rule-based versions, indirect strategies, or revisions to the theory of the good. A practical difficulty is epistemic: consequences are uncertain and extend far into the future, so applications rely on expected value.

## Relation to Active Inference and decision theory
The connection is structural rather than doctrinal. Consequentialism is a normative theory, and formal decision theory supplies its machinery: options are scored by expected utility (see [[utility_theory]] and [[decision_theory]]). In Active Inference, policies are scored by expected free energy, which also depends on predicted outcomes and on preferences over them, so the form of the evaluation is similar. However, expected free energy concerns an agent's own adaptive behaviour, not impartial moral aggregation across persons, and it adds an information-seeking term with no counterpart in classical utilitarianism. Any claim beyond this analogy is speculative.
