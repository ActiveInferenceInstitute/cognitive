# Pure Land Buddhism

## Overview
Pure Land teachings centre on the possibility of liberation from the cycle of birth and death by being born in the buddha-field, or Pure Land, of Amida Buddha. They are often regarded as popularised, devotional extensions of a more demanding contemplative tradition based on emptiness, but the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry by Dennis Hirota notes that the concept of multitudes of buddha-fields is present already in the earliest Mahayana texts. Sutras on buddha-fields were among the earliest Buddhist scriptures translated into Chinese, and the sutras describing Amida's land (Sukhavativyuha texts) soon followed. A continuous commentarial tradition runs through Indian thinkers such as Nagarjuna and Vasubandhu and Chinese masters such as Tanluan (476-542) and Shandao, before reaching Japan.

## Japanese developments
- **Honen (1133-1212).** After training in the Tendai monastery on Mount Hiei, Honen taught that simply uttering the Name of Amida, "Namu-amida-butsu", entrusting oneself to Amida's vow to save all beings, results in birth in the buddha-field. He made the vocal *nembutsu* an independent and self-sufficient path, with no need for intellectual command of the teachings, accumulation of merit or other practice.
- **Shinran (1173-1263), Shoku and Ippen** developed Honen's teaching in different ways. In Shinran's writings the decisive element is *shinjin*, a deeply entrusting mind that includes a clear recognition of oneself as a foolish being bound by blind passions, together with faith in Amida's vows.
- **Metaphysics.** The entry argues that the dominant traditional treatment of the reality of Amida and the Pure Land does not rest on substance, identity or permanence. Instead it moves across provisional dichotomies, such as formless reality and form, wisdom and ignorance, buddha and sentient being. Tanluan's discussion of how beings can be "born" when all beings are in the end unborn is a central source for this.
- **Modern encounters.** The entry also covers the comparisons with the Christianity of the Reformation that Western observers drew, the modernisation of Buddhist thought, and existential readings of Pure Land thought (compare [[kyoto_school]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct relation. The tradition concerns salvation and the nature of Amida, and the notion of "other-power" is a theological claim. A distant comparison with the Active Inference description of an agent's dependence on a prior it does not itself choose would be an interpretive analogy and should not be pressed.
