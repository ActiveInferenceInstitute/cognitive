# Environmental Ethics

## Overview
Environmental ethics asks what we owe to the non-human natural world and why. The field emerged in the 1970s in response to ecological crisis and divides mainly on whether nature has value only for humans or in itself.

## Positions
**Anthropocentrism.** Only humans have intrinsic value; nature matters instrumentally, for resources, health and recreation. Weak versions allow long-term and aesthetic human interests and so support conservation.

**Extending moral standing.** Singer argued for sentient animals, based on capacity for suffering; Regan for animals as "subjects of a life"; biocentric views extend to all living things.

**Holism.** Leopold's land ethic (*A Sand County Almanac*, 1949) holds that a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability and beauty of the biotic community. Callicott developed it philosophically. Critics note that a holistic ethic can appear to subordinate individuals, including humans, to the whole.

**Deep ecology.** Naess (1973) distinguished shallow from deep ecology and proposed value in the flourishing of all life, with human identification extending to the ecological Self.

**Environmental pragmatism.** Light and Katz (1996) argued that debates over intrinsic value are less productive than inquiry into concrete problems, with pluralism about values and revision in light of consequences. This is the point of contact with [[pragmatism]], which treats ethical concepts as tools for resolving problematic situations.

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference does not supply an ethics, and nothing in it decides between the positions above: the framework describes how agents with given preferences act, and the content of those preferences is an input. Its relevance is modest and applied. Ecosystem-scale applications treat ecological systems as nested agents that keep themselves within viable bounds, which yields a descriptive vocabulary for resilience, regime shifts and stewardship ([[ecosystem_management]], [[resilience_thinking]]). An agent-centred view of "viability" can inform what counts as the integrity of a system in the land-ethic sense, but it does not justify the moral claim that this integrity matters. Moving from a model of self-preserving systems to an obligation to preserve them would be an is-ought step, and framing the preferences as the system's own does not supply that premise.
