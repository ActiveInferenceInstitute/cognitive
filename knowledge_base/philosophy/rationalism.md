# Rationalism

## Overview
Rationalism is the view that reason has a central and independent role in the acquisition of knowledge. Rationalists hold that at least some significant truths can be known a priori, that is, by intellectual insight or deduction without depending on sensory evidence. The term most often refers to the seventeenth-century continental tradition of Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz, and is classically set against [[empiricism]], which grounds knowledge in experience.

## Central theses
- **Intuition and deduction.** Knowledge of fundamental truths can be reached by clear rational intuition and derivation from it, as in mathematics.
- **Innate knowledge.** Some ideas or principles are not derived from experience. Leibniz argued that the mind contributes structure of its own, in his reply to Locke, summarised by the phrase that nothing is in the intellect that was not first in the senses, "except the intellect itself".
- **Substantive metaphysics through reason.** Descartes argued from the clarity of the cogito to the nature of mind and body, while Spinoza built a deductive system in the manner of geometry. The ambition to reach metaphysical conclusions by argument alone is a mark of the tradition (see [[metaphysics]]).
- **Contrast with empiricism.** The dispute is one of degree. Few thinkers deny both reason and experience a role. Kant's critical philosophy is often described as a synthesis, holding that experience requires a priori forms contributed by the mind.

## Later developments
The debate persists in cognitive science as the question of how much structure is built in. Chomsky's nativist account of language acquisition is the best-known modern position with a rationalist character, and it is contested by statistical learning approaches that stress what can be learned from input.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is conceptual. An Active Inference agent has a generative model with prior beliefs, which play the role the rationalist assigns to the mind's contribution, but these priors are themselves shaped over evolutionary and developmental time by experience and can be revised by evidence. The framework therefore sits between the two camps, and the question of how rich the initial priors are remains an empirical one rather than something settled by the formalism (see [[bayesian_brain]]).
