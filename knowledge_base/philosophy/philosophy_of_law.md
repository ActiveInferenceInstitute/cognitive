# Philosophy of Law

## Overview
The philosophy of law, or jurisprudence, asks what law is, how it differs from other social norms, whether it has any necessary connection with morality, how legal reasoning proceeds, and whether and why citizens are obliged to obey it. The field is usually divided into analytic questions about the concept of law and normative questions about its justification and limits.

## Main theories
- **Natural law.** Associated with Aquinas and, in the twentieth century, Finnis (1980), it holds that law is connected to moral reasons, so that unjust enactments may fail fully to be law or lack the authority that law claims.
- **Legal positivism.** Associated with Bentham, Austin, Kelsen and Hart, it holds that the existence of law depends on social facts, such as recognition by officials, and not on its merits. Hart (1961) described law as a union of primary rules of conduct and secondary rules of recognition, change and adjudication.
- **Interpretivism.** Dworkin (1986) argued that legal reasoning is constructive interpretation, which seeks the reading of past legal practice that puts it in its best moral light, so that morality enters the determination of what the law requires.
- **Legal realism.** Realists in the early twentieth century argued that judicial decisions are better predicted by what courts do than by formal rules.

Fuller (1964) proposed that law has an "inner morality", a set of procedural requirements such as generality and publicity, a claim disputed by positivists.

## Related questions
Further topics include the justification of punishment ([[punishment]], [[retributive_justice]]), the grounds of political obligation, the interpretation of constitutions, and responsibility and excuse in criminal law. Positions on these are matters of continuing dispute.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct connection. Some work applies cognitive science to legal questions, such as the reliability of eyewitness memory or the nature of intent, and that work could draw on predictive models of perception and agency, but such uses are exploratory and not part of the theory of law.
