# Structural Realism

## Overview
Structural realism is a position on what successful scientific theories tell us about the world. It holds that they reveal the structure of reality, the relations among things as described mathematically, but not the intrinsic nature of the unobservable entities that stand in those relations. Worrall (1989) presented it as a way of keeping the strengths of [[scientific_realism]] and of anti-realism together.

## Motivation
Realists argue that the success of science would be a miracle if theories were not approximately true. Anti-realists reply with the pessimistic meta-induction, pointing to past successful theories, such as the nineteenth-century ether theory of light, whose central entities were later abandoned. Worrall observed that Fresnel's equations for the behaviour of light were retained, as a limiting case, in Maxwell's theory even though Fresnel's elastic ether was not. What is preserved across theory change is structure, so realism should be about structure.

## Two versions
- **Epistemic structural realism.** There are unobservable things with natures, but we can know only their structural properties. A traditional source is Russell's view that we know the world only by its formal structure. Newman's objection to Russell, that any set of the right size can be given any structure, suggests that pure structure is too cheap unless supplemented by non-structural constraints.
- **Ontic structural realism.** Ladyman (1998) and Ladyman and Ross (2007) went further, claiming that structure is all there is and that objects are derivative. They appeal in part to quantum mechanics, in which particles of the same kind lack individuality in a way that challenges the notion of an object as a bearer of properties.

Critics question whether structure can be coherently separated from the objects that instantiate it and whether the account fits sciences other than physics.

## Relation to Active Inference
The free energy principle treats an organism as embodying a generative model of the causes of its sensory input, and models that guide successful action are often described as capturing the causal structure of the environment, without being copies of it. This invites a comparison with epistemic structural realism: what the model shares with the world is relational structure, not intrinsic nature. The comparison is an analogy suggested in the philosophical literature on predictive processing, not a theorem, and the precise sense in which a model "mirrors" structure is debated.
