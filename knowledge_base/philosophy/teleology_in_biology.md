# Teleology in Biology

## Overview
Biologists routinely say that the heart is for pumping blood, that a bird migrates in order to find food, or that an immune response aims to eliminate a pathogen. Teleology is the philosophical study of such purposive explanation. The central question is whether talk of function and goals reflects something real about living systems, and if so how it can be reconciled with explanation in terms of ordinary physical causes.

## Historical background
Aristotle gave final causes, the "that for the sake of which" something occurs, a central role in explaining organisms. Kant described organisms as natural purposes in which parts exist for the sake of the whole and each other. Darwin's theory of natural selection offered a way to explain apparent design without foresight. Pittendrigh coined the term teleonomy for goal-directedness explained by evolved programs, and Mayr (1961) distinguished it from the discredited notion of a cosmic purpose guiding evolution.

## Accounts of function
- **Etiological.** The function of a trait is what it was selected for in the past, as in the approaches of Wright (1976) and Millikan. This explains why the trait exists and permits malfunction.
- **Systemic (causal-role).** Cummins (1975) held that a function is the contribution of a part to a capacity of the containing system, whatever the history.
- **Organisational.** Functions are contributions to the self-maintenance of a system that produces and maintains its own components (see [[autopoiesis]], [[organizational_closure]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The free energy principle proposes a formal description of why living systems appear goal-directed: to persist, they must keep their states within a limited set, which can be described as minimising surprise relative to a model whose prior preferences encode characteristic states. Preferences and goals then appear as priors rather than extra causes (see [[homeostasis]]). This is an active research programme and a subject of debate. Critics note that the formalism may restate persistence in different terms, and that it does not by itself distinguish selected function from mere stable dynamics. It engages most directly with the organisational accounts, with the etiological accounts requiring evolutionary extensions.
