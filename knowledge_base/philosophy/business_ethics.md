# Business Ethics

## Overview
Business ethics studies the moral norms that bear on commercial activity: the obligations of firms to owners, employees, customers, suppliers and society, and the conduct of individuals within organisations. Topics include corporate governance, honesty in marketing, labour conditions, environmental impact, conflicts of interest and the ethics of markets themselves. The field draws on normative theory and on economics, law and organisational research, and it contains real disagreement on its central questions.

## Positions on corporate purpose
- **Shareholder view.** Friedman (1970) argued that the social responsibility of business is to use its resources to increase profits while staying within the rules of the game, open and free competition without deception or fraud, on the ground that managers act as agents of the owners and that social goals are for governments and individuals.
- **Stakeholder view.** Freeman (1984) proposed that managers should consider the interests of all groups affected by or affecting the firm. Donaldson and Preston (1995) distinguished descriptive, instrumental and normative versions of stakeholder theory. Critics argue that it gives managers unclear guidance.
- **Social contract and integrative approaches.** Donaldson and Dunfee proposed that firms are bound by hypothetical and actual agreements within communities, an approach called integrative social contracts theory.
- **Virtue and rights perspectives.** Applications of [[normative_ethics]] consider the character of business leaders, duties of fair dealing and the rights of workers and consumers.

Empirical work on trust, reputation and repeated interaction, drawing on [[game_theory]], bears on how far self-interested conduct and ethical conduct coincide, which is itself disputed.

The link is indirect.
