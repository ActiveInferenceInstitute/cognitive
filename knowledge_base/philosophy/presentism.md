# Presentism

## Overview
Presentism is the thesis that only present objects and events exist. The past is no longer, and the future is not yet, so there are no dinosaurs or distant future events, only the present. It is the most common view among non-specialists and is a leading version of the A-theory of time, which holds that the distinction between past, present and future is objective (see [[philosophy_of_time]]).

## Motivations
- **Passage.** It accords with the sense that time flows and that the present is special.
- **Openness.** If the future does not yet exist, it can be genuinely open, which fits ordinary ideas about choice.
- **Parsimony.** The ontology contains less than that of rival views.

## Problems
- **Truths about the past.** If the past does not exist, what makes it true that Caesar crossed the Rubicon? Presentists offer responses such as treating past-tensed truths as grounded in present traces or in primitive tensed facts, and critics find these unattractive.
- **Cross-time relations.** Statements like "Socrates influenced Plato" or "this event is earlier than that one" appear to relate things that do not both exist.
- **Special relativity.** Simultaneity is relative to a reference frame, so there is no frame-independent present. Putnam (1967) argued that this leads to the conclusion that all events are equally real. Presentists reply that relativity should be supplemented with a preferred foliation of spacetime, which is not detectable, a response that critics regard as ad hoc.

Presentism is contrasted with [[eternalism]] and with intermediate views such as the growing block, in which the past and present exist but the future does not (Crisp (2003) and the Stanford Encyclopedia entry on time set out the options).

## Relation to Active Inference
The framework is neutral on the issue. Its generative models contain beliefs about past, present and future states, but these beliefs are present states of the agent, and such models can be read in a presentist way, as encoding the present traces of the past and expectations of the future, without ontological commitment. The topic is of interest here for the treatment of time in models, not because the framework supports either ontology.
