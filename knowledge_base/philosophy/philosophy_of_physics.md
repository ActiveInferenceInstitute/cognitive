# Philosophy of Physics

## Overview
The philosophy of physics studies the conceptual foundations and metaphysical implications of physical theories. It asks what a theory says about the world, how its formalism should be interpreted when the mathematics leaves this open, and whether successful theories can be read as literally true. It is a branch of philosophy of science with close ties to physics itself.

## Quantum theory
The formalism of quantum mechanics predicts measurement statistics with great accuracy, but it does not fix a unique account of what happens in a measurement. Principal interpretations include the Copenhagen tradition, which gives measurement a special role, Everett's many-worlds interpretation, in which the wave function evolves unitarily and branches, and Bohmian mechanics, which adds definite particle positions guided by the wave function. Objective-collapse theories modify the dynamics. These interpretations are, to varying degrees, empirically equivalent in current experiments, and their evaluation depends on philosophical criteria as well as physical ones (see [[quantum_mechanics]]).

## Space, time and statistical mechanics
In the debate between substantivalism and relationism, substantivalists hold that spacetime is an entity in its own right, while relationists hold that it consists in relations among material events. Relativity raises questions about simultaneity and whether time passes (see [[philosophy_of_time]]). The directionality of time is traced to boundary conditions of low entropy and to the statistical foundations of thermodynamics, where the status of probabilities and of the second law is a continuing topic (see [[statistical_physics]]). Maudlin's volumes survey these topics.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is real but contested. The free energy principle borrows from statistical physics, using Markov blankets, nonequilibrium steady states and path-integral formulations (see [[free_energy_principle]]). Philosophers debate whether its claims about physical systems are literal descriptions, whether the Markov blanket is a feature of nature or a modelling device, and whether it should be understood instrumentally (see [[scientific_realism]]). The framework supports no particular interpretation of quantum theory.
