# Philosophy of AI

## Overview
The philosophy of artificial intelligence examines what AI systems are and could be, and what their existence implies for theories of mind, knowledge and value. Its questions overlap with [[philosophy_of_mind]] and cognitive science, since attempts to build intelligent machines function as tests of theories of cognition, and with [[ai_ethics]], which addresses what should be done with such systems.

## Central questions
- **Can machines think?** Turing (1950) proposed replacing this question with a behavioural test, the imitation game, and argued that objections to machine thought, such as those from consciousness or from the supposed limits of rule-following, were not decisive.
- **Understanding.** Searle (1980) argued with the Chinese Room that running a program, however capable, would not suffice for understanding, because syntax does not yield semantics. Replies include the systems reply, the robot reply and appeals to functional organisation (see [[functionalism]]).
- **Symbol grounding.** Harnad (1990) asked how symbols in a computational system acquire meaning beyond their relation to other symbols, motivating embodied and sensorimotor approaches.
- **The frame problem.** How can a reasoning system update relevant beliefs after an action without checking everything that might have changed? It was identified by McCarthy and Hayes (1969) and became a broader problem of relevance.
- **Critiques of symbolic AI.** Dreyfus argued that human expertise depends on embodied, situated skill that cannot be captured by explicit rules.
- **Consciousness and moral status.** Whether artificial systems could be conscious or have interests, and how this could be tested (see [[artificial_consciousness]]).

Recent debates concern whether large statistical language models display understanding or only imitate it, and are unresolved.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference is offered as a framework for building and analysing agents that perceive, act and plan under a single objective, and its proponents emphasise embodiment, uncertainty-driven exploration and explicit generative models. This links it to the embodied and enactive responses to the symbol-grounding and frame problems. Claims that such agents could be sentient or understand are programmatic and contested, and the framework does not settle the questions above.
