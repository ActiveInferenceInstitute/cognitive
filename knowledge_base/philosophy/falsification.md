# Falsification

## Overview
Falsification is the idea that what distinguishes scientific theories is that they can in principle be refuted by observation. Popper (1959, original 1934) proposed it as a criterion of demarcation between science and non-science, and as an alternative to the verifiability criterion of the [[logical_positivism]] of the Vienna Circle. A theory that forbids certain possible observations is empirically informative; a theory compatible with every conceivable observation says nothing about the world.

## Logic and method
The argument rests on an asymmetry. No finite set of observations can verify a universal claim such as "all swans are white", but a single contrary instance refutes it by *modus tollens*: if the theory implies an observation and the observation fails, the theory is false. Popper therefore recommended bold, highly falsifiable conjectures and sincere attempts to refute them, with theories that survive severe tests said to be corroborated, which does not mean confirmed as true.

## Criticisms
- **Duhem-Quine holism.** Theories face observation only together with auxiliary assumptions, so a failed prediction does not identify which part to reject.
- **Research programmes.** Lakatos (1970) argued that scientists reasonably protect a theory's core while modifying auxiliary hypotheses, and that programmes should be judged by whether they progress.
- **Probabilistic claims.** Statistical hypotheses forbid no outcome strictly, so refuting them needs further conventions.
- **Historical practice.** Kuhn and others observed that anomalies are often tolerated for long periods (see [[kuhn_paradigms]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The question is live in discussions of the free energy principle. The principle is mathematical and generic, and many commentators hold that it functions more like a framework or a variational principle than like a theory that makes a direct empirical prediction. Specific process theories and generative models built within it, such as particular message-passing schemes or models of a task, do make testable predictions, and competing models can be compared by model evidence (see [[model_comparison]]). Whether this makes the overall programme falsifiable in Popper's sense, or progressive in Lakatos's, is debated and not settled.
