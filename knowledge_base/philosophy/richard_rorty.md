# Richard Rorty

## Overview
Richard Rorty (1931-2007) trained in the analytic tradition and became its best-known internal critic. His book *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979) argued that modern philosophy had been organised around a picture of knowledge as an accurate mental mirroring of a mind-independent world. He drew on Sellars, Quine and Davidson to attack that picture, and later turned toward Dewey, Hegel and Darwin for an alternative. Rorty is the most prominent figure of the pragmatist revival described in [[pragmatism]], although philosophers such as Susan Haack and Cheryl Misak dispute whether his view is faithful to the classical pragmatists.

## Main positions
- **Anti-representationalism.** Rorty calls his view of knowledge "epistemological behaviorism": to know something is to be able to justify a claim to one's peers, not to mirror reality. Truth, on his view, has no deep substantive nature beyond what is warranted.
- **Anti-foundationalism.** No vocabulary is inescapable or ideal, so the search for an ultimate foundation (see [[foundationalism]]) is a mistake. Progress comes from inventing new vocabularies, not from solving permanent problems.
- **Ethnocentrism and solidarity.** We cannot step outside our acculturation to a God's-eye view. Moral progress consists in widening the "we" by training sympathy, often through novels and redescription, as developed in *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* (1989).
- **Liberal irony.** The "liberal ironist" holds her deepest commitments as contingent while still working to reduce cruelty, and separates private self-creation from public justice.
- **Philosophy as cultural politics.** Later essays recast philosophy as a contribution to cultural and political conversation, not as a discipline with privileged access to truth.

Critics, including those who treat the position as a form of [[relativism]], reply that dropping correspondence as a goal of inquiry sacrifices the idea of being wrong about the world. Rorty rejects that description of his view.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is interpretive. Rorty's attack on mirroring has a partial echo in readings of Active Inference in which a generative model is judged by how well it supports action and prediction rather than by resemblance to the world (see [[generative_model]] and [[correspondence_theory]]). Other readings keep a representational vocabulary, and the free energy principle does not commit to Rorty's claims about truth or about cultural politics. His emphasis on justification within a community has loose affinity with work on shared generative models (see [[social_epistemology]]), but Rorty did not engage with this literature.
