# Experimental Philosophy

## Overview
Experimental philosophy, often abbreviated x-phi, applies the methods of psychology and cognitive science to questions that philosophers have traditionally addressed from the armchair. Its typical studies present ordinary people with short vignettes, such as thought experiments about knowledge, intentional action or moral blame, and record their judgements. The movement took shape in the early 2000s and has since grown into a recognised subfield with its own journals and conferences.

## Aims and methods
Philosophical arguments frequently appeal to intuitions: that a certain case is not knowledge, that an agent did not act freely, that a particular outcome is permissible. Experimental philosophers ask empirical questions about these judgements. Do they vary across cultures, education levels, personality types or the wording and order of the cases? What cognitive processes produce them?

Two broad programmes are commonly distinguished. A negative programme argues that if intuitions are unstable or sensitive to irrelevant factors, philosophers should be cautious about relying on them as evidence. A positive programme uses experiments to map the structure of folk concepts, which is itself a source of insight into how people think.

## Illustrative findings
- Judgements about whether a side effect was brought about intentionally depend on whether the side effect is morally good or bad, a pattern now widely called the Knobe effect.
- Early reports of cultural variation in intuitions about Gettier-style cases and reference attracted considerable attention, although later attempts to replicate some of these results have been mixed.
- Studies of moral dilemmas, including trolley cases, show that judgements shift with framing and with how directly harm is caused.

## Criticisms
Critics note that trained philosophers may respond differently from lay participants (the expertise defence), that vignette comprehension is hard to control, and that survey responses may differ from the intuitions used in philosophical argument. Replication problems that affected psychology more broadly have also affected parts of the field.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct theoretical link to Active Inference. The connection is methodological: experimental philosophy belongs to a naturalistic stance in which philosophical questions about mind and knowledge are informed by cognitive science. Treating intuitions as outputs of a learned predictive model is a possible interpretation, but it is a framing rather than an established result.
