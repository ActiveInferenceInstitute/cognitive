# Feminist Phenomenology

## Overview
Feminist phenomenology applies the phenomenological method, the careful description of lived experience, to questions about gender and other social positions, and in particular to the experience of the body. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on feminist perspectives on the body reports that a return to interest in feminist phenomenology, in the footsteps of Simone de Beauvoir, started with the work of Sandra Bartky and Iris Marion Young in the late 1970s but became widespread only in the 1990s. At its centre is the lived experience of the body, with embodiment understood as our mode of being-in-the-world (see [[being_in_the_world]] and [[lived_body]]).

## Sources and method
Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) provides an influential phenomenology of female embodiment. The SEP notes her accounts of how the young girl's body is experienced differently from the young boy's, and of women living their bodies as objects, and describes these as anticipating Bartky and Young. A second major influence is Merleau-Ponty, whose analysis of the pre-reflexive, bodily engagement with the world is the base for many feminist accounts (see [[existential_phenomenology]]). The experiences to which these writers draw attention are of bodies in situations, in which natural and social elements cannot be disentangled.

## Young: throwing like a girl
Young's essay "Throwing Like a Girl" (1980) is the best-known example. According to the SEP, she argued that the social norms governing female bodily comportment produce an inhibited intentionality, an interruption in the pre-reflexive engagement with the environment described by Merleau-Ponty, and she followed Beauvoir in holding that this derives not from anatomy but from the situation of women in society. Women often experience their bodies as things "looked at and acted upon" as well as sources of capacities. Her later essays on pregnancy, breasted experience and menstruation add that distinctive features of female embodiment can open positive possibilities as well as negative ones.

## Developments
Writers such as Bartky, Alcoff, Heinämaa and Weiss carry the project forward, drawing on Beauvoir and Merleau-Ponty to make visible the experiences of gendered, raced, classed, differently abled and differently aged bodies. Alcoff points out that phenomenological accounts require cross-indexing by cultural and ethnic specificity and uses the approach to analyse "visible identities". Toril Moi has suggested replacing the categories of sex and gender with that of the lived body. This work connects with the concerns of [[intersectionality]], and it differs from poststructuralist approaches that are suspicious of appeal to experience.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is real but modest. Active Inference treats perception as bound up with action and the body, a theme it shares with embodied approaches (see [[embodied_cognition]]), though how closely it aligns with phenomenological or enactive views is debated. Feminist phenomenology adds the claim that social norms shape bodily comportment, which can be read in predictive terms as norms acting as priors on movement, but this is an interpretive suggestion, not an established result in either field.
