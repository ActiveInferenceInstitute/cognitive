# Social Justice

## Overview
Social justice concerns what a fair arrangement of a society's institutions looks like, particularly in the distribution of rights, opportunities, resources, and social standing among its members. The term is used in political philosophy, law, economics and public debate, and its meaning and scope are contested. This page describes some influential positions as their authors state them, without endorsing any.

## Influential accounts
- **Justice as fairness.** Rawls (1971) argued that principles of justice are those that rational persons would choose behind a veil of ignorance about their own position. He proposed equal basic liberties and an arrangement of inequalities that works to the benefit of the least advantaged.
- **Libertarian entitlement.** Nozick and others hold that justice consists in respecting rights of acquisition and voluntary transfer, and criticise patterned redistribution.
- **Capabilities.** Sen (2009) and Nussbaum emphasise what people are actually able to do and be, and compare real arrangements instead of constructing an ideal institutional blueprint.
- **Recognition and difference.** Young (1990) and Fraser emphasise domination, oppression and misrecognition as injustices that distributive models can miss.
- **Utilitarian and communitarian views.** These assess institutions by aggregate welfare or by shared traditions and goods, and disagree with contractualist approaches about the role of individual rights.

Debates also cover global justice, intergenerational duties and the status of nonideal conditions.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct link. Active Inference is a descriptive framework and does not itself supply normative principles. Indirect contact occurs in work on collective dynamics, such as [[socioeconomic_active_inference]], and in analyses of how shared expectations and norms sustain or entrench social arrangements (see [[social_norms]]). Claims that the framework favours a particular conception of justice would go beyond the formalism.
