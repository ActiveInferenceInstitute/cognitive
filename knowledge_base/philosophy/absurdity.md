# Absurdity

## Overview
In philosophy, absurdity names the sense that human life involves a search for meaning, purpose or justification that the world does not answer. The term is used in this sense by existentialist writers and is distinct from the logical sense of an absurdity as a contradiction, as in *reductio ad absurdum*. Accounts differ on where the absurd lies and how to respond to it.

## Camus
Albert Camus developed the idea in *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942). On his account the absurd is not a feature of the world alone or of the human mind alone but arises from their confrontation: human beings seek clarity and unity, and the world offers none that reason can confirm. Camus asks whether this recognition makes life not worth living, and he discusses three responses. Suicide removes one term of the confrontation. What he calls philosophical suicide, which he attributes to thinkers such as Kierkegaard, resolves the tension by a leap to faith or to a transcendent meaning. The response he defends is revolt, a lucid persistence in living without appeal to such resolution. He uses the figure of Sisyphus to illustrate this attitude. Camus himself rejected the label of existentialist, although the essay is commonly placed in that tradition.

## Nagel
Thomas Nagel (1971) examined the arguments commonly given for the absurdity of life, for instance that we are insignificant on cosmic scales or that our lives will not matter in the long run, and argued that they do not succeed. He proposed instead that absurdity arises from a collision within the human perspective between the seriousness with which people take their lives and the ever-present possibility of stepping back and viewing that seriousness as arbitrary. Because the capacity for this detached viewpoint cannot be suppressed, he suggests that irony is a more fitting response than either heroic defiance or despair.

## Other treatments
Kierkegaard used the term for the paradoxical content of faith, as in *Fear and Trembling*, and Sartre's account of contingency and freedom is often grouped with Camus's despite their disagreements ([[being_and_nothingness]], [[facticity]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no substantive link. Active Inference models perception and action and does not bear on claims about the meaning of life. A parallel between the absurd as a gap between expectation and world and prediction error is metaphorical, since Camus's gap is conceptual and normative, not a quantity to be minimised.
