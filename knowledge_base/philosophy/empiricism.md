# Empiricism

## Overview
Empiricism holds that knowledge, or at least knowledge of the world, is grounded in sensory experience. Its traditional opponent is rationalism (Descartes, Leibniz), which allows substantive knowledge from reason alone. The debate concerns the sources of concepts and the justification of beliefs.

## Core claims
**British empiricists.** Locke (1689) denied innate ideas: the mind begins as a blank slate, and ideas arise from sensation and reflection. Berkeley took the view to idealism: to be is to be perceived. Hume (1739, 1748) divided knowledge into relations of ideas (a priori, necessary) and matters of fact (a posteriori, contingent), and argued that induction and causation cannot be justified by reason alone, posing the problem of induction.

**Kant's response.** Kant held that experience supplies content but the mind supplies structure (space, time, causal categories) that makes experience possible, a synthesis of empiricist and rationalist insights.

**Logical empiricism.** The Vienna Circle added a criterion of meaning: statements are meaningful only if analytic or empirically verifiable ([[logical_positivism]], [[verification_theory]]). Quine's attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction (1951) and Popper's falsificationism undermined the strict versions.

**Contemporary forms.** Constructive empiricism (van Fraassen) holds that science aims at empirical adequacy, not truth about unobservables ([[instrumentalism]] is the older relative). Pragmatism ([[pragmatism]]) is empiricist in tone but treats experience as active inquiry.

## Role in Active Inference
Perception as inference descends from Helmholtz's unconscious inference, an empiricist tradition: percepts are conclusions drawn from sensory data. Hierarchical Bayesian models learn priors from experience, matching the empiricist claim that structure is acquired. But the framework also requires a generative model whose form (hierarchy, causal structure, the existence of a Markov blanket) is fixed before learning, so it is closer to Kant than to the blank slate: experience fits parameters, while the model class is given. Whether that class is itself learnable, through structure learning and evolutionary selection of priors, is an open question ([[structure_learning]]). Active Inference's emphasis on action as evidence-gathering also echoes the experimental, interventionist strand of empiricism.
