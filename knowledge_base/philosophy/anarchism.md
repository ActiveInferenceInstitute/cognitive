# Anarchism

## Overview
Anarchism is a cluster of political theories and movements that hold that coercive, hierarchical authority, in particular that of the state, is unjustified or unnecessary, and that social order can arise from voluntary association. It is a contested subject, and critics and proponents disagree about whether its claims are coherent and feasible. This page describes positions and attributes them to their advocates without endorsing any.

## Positions
- **Philosophical anarchism.** Wolff (1970) argued that the autonomy of the individual is incompatible with a general duty to obey the commands of any state, so that legitimate authority is never established. Simmons and others distinguished this from political anarchism, which urges abolishing the state.
- **Mutualism and collectivism.** Proudhon proposed an order based on reciprocal exchange and federation, and Bakunin stressed collective ownership and opposition to both state and church.
- **Anarcho-communism.** Kropotkin (1902) drew on observations of cooperation among animals and humans to argue that mutual aid is a factor in evolution, supporting voluntary cooperative society.
- **Individualist and market anarchism.** Stirner, Tucker and later market anarchists emphasised individual sovereignty, and some of the latter defend private provision of protection.

Responses include the minimal-state view of Nozick (1974), who argued that a limited state can arise from a state of nature by legitimate steps, and critics who claim that order without authority is unstable. Kinna (2019) offers an accessible survey. The tradition also overlaps with debates over [[justice]] and [[social_justice]].

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no derivation connecting anarchism with Active Inference. The link is an analogy at most: research on self-organisation in biological and social systems, such as stigmergy in insect colonies, studies coordination without a central controller, a theme that anarchist writers like Kropotkin drew on informally. Such work is descriptive and does not bear on the normative question of whether states are legitimate.
