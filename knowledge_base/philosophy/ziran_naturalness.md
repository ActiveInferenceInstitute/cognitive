# Ziran (Naturalness)

## Overview
*Ziran* is built from *zi* ("self") and *ran* ("so") and means literally what is "so of itself". It is usually translated "naturalness" or "spontaneity". In the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on the *Laozi*, Alan Chan explains that the text uses the concept to describe the workings of the Dao. As an abstract concept, *ziran* gives no specific information beyond saying that the Dao is not derived from or modelled after anything (chapter 25). Since heaven and earth, interpreted in most modern studies as nature, are said to be born of the Dao and to come to be through their *de* (virtue or power), the text is in effect saying that the ways of nature reflect the function of the Dao.

## Interpretations
- **Qi and yin-yang.** The Heshang Gong commentary reads nature as governed by the operation of vital *qi* energies in an ideal yin-yang system marked by harmony and fecundity (see [[yin_yang_theory]]).
- **Principles in nature.** For Wang Bi, the *Laozi* means more generally that there are inherent principles (*li*) in nature.
- **Human beings.** Humans are born of heaven and earth and so are modelled after them, either through their *qi* constitution or through governance by the same basic principles. *Ziran* therefore points both to the power of the Dao expressed in nature and, practically, to a way of acting in accordance with it, which is the role of *wuwei* (see [[wuwei]]).
- **Nature includes the spiritual and the social.** In the Daoist sense nature does not exclude spirits, which were thought of as made of *qi*, nor sociopolitical institutions: the king and the family are treated as "natural" institutions. As an ethical concept, *ziran* thus extends from the personal to the political level.

## Naturalness and desire
The *Laozi* traces political decline to excessive desire, which is a violation of *ziran*. Naturalness is held to cover basic human needs, but to be distinct from the desire that fuels self-gratification without limit. This is also the basis of its criticism of Confucian effort to cultivate virtue, which in the Daoist view only accentuates the loss of natural goodness (see [[confucian_philosophy]]). The entry notes that *ziran* remains influential in Chinese ideas of love, beauty and attitudes to life and death.

## Zhuangzi
Chad Hansen's entry on the *Zhuangzi* reads the term in a naturalistic key. It speaks of things "self-realising" their possible paths, and of the Dao as following *ziran*, so that normativity and choice emerge from the unfolding of the cosmos in which many agents realise options. This is one interpretation among several in the literature.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is interpretive. Treating order as arising from the local, self-organising activity of many things, without a central controller, resembles the way Active Inference describes ordered behaviour as emerging from systems that maintain themselves in characteristic states (see [[process_philosophy]] and [[naturalism]]). *Ziran* is a normative and metaphysical concept of the Daoist tradition, and the resemblance is conceptual only.
