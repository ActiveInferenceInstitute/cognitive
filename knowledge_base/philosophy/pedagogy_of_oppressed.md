# Pedagogy of the Oppressed

## Overview
*Pedagogy of the Oppressed* is the best-known work of the Brazilian educator and philosopher Paulo Freire (1921-1997). According to Kim Diaz's entry in the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, he wrote it during the first years of his exile from Brazil and published it in 1968, and it was translated into English in 1970. It has been banned by several governments, and the entry mentions the apartheid government of South Africa and, in the twenty-first century, a public-schools ban in Arizona. The book grew out of Freire's literacy work with peasants and workers in Brazil and Chile, many of them descendants of enslaved people.

## Banking and problem-posing education
Freire criticises teachers who see themselves as the only possessors of knowledge and their students as empty receptacles into which knowledge must be deposited, which he calls the "banking" method. He likens it to colonisation, since the colonising culture presents itself as correct and valuable and the colonised culture as inferior. The relationship is vertical, from teacher to student, and is marked by insecurity, suspicion and the teacher's need for control. He proposes instead critical or problem-posing pedagogy, a horizontal and reciprocal relationship in which teacher and student learn from one another through dialogue. Teachers problematise a subject, so that ready-made answers are not available and students gain confidence in raising their own questions.

## Key concepts
- **Conscientizacao.** The process of becoming aware of social and political contradictions and then acting against the oppressive elements of one's conditions. It starts with recognising oneself as a subject with agency and not as an object.
- **Internalisation.** The oppressed may carry the oppressor "inside" them, so that they behave as the oppressor would have them behave even when absent. Freire illustrates it with peasants who occupied a plantation but were unable to guard the landowner.
- **Freedom and liberation.** Freire holds that freedom has to be claimed and cannot be given by another, that the oppressed must not become oppressors in turn, and that liberation is a mutual process in which the oppressed free both themselves and their oppressors. He distinguishes false charity from social justice.
- **Dialogue.** Dialogue rests on love, respect and tolerance, and he contrasts it with imposition. In the third chapter he distinguishes humanism from paternalistic humanitarianism, and in the fourth he criticises revolutionary leaders who deny the people participation in their own liberation.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. Freire's claim that people's self-conception is shaped by the roles that social structures assign them can be compared with models in which an agent's beliefs about itself are inferred partly from others' responses. Active Inference does not address pedagogy or oppression, and that comparison is conceptual only.
