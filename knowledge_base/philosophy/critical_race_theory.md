# Critical Race Theory

## Overview
Critical race theory (CRT) is a body of scholarship that began in the mid-1970s among lawyers, activists and legal scholars who concluded that the advances of the civil rights era of the 1960s had stalled and in many respects were being rolled back, and that new theories and strategies were needed to address subtler forms of racism. In the account given by Delgado and Stefancic, early figures included Derrick Bell, Alan Freeman and Richard Delgado, the group held its first conference in the summer of 1989, and it later produced spin-off movements such as LatCrit and Asian American jurisprudence. CRT builds on critical legal studies and radical feminism, and draws on thinkers such as Gramsci and Derrida and on the American radical tradition. It is a movement in legal and social theory, not a single doctrine, and not every participant accepts every tenet.

## Hallmark themes
Delgado and Stefancic identify propositions with which many, though not all, critical race theorists would agree.
- **Racism is ordinary.** It is the usual way society does business, not an aberration. For that reason colour-blind or formal conceptions of equality can remedy only the most blatant discrimination.
- **Interest convergence.** Because racism advances the interests of white elites materially and working-class whites psychically, large segments of society have little incentive to eradicate it. Bell's 1980 article argued that *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) may have owed more to the converging interests of elite whites, including Cold War concerns about the image of the United States abroad, than to moral concern for black people. The legal historian Mary Dudziak later reported archival evidence supporting that hypothesis.
- **Social construction.** Race and races are products of social thought and relations, not objective biological categories. Societies invent, manipulate or retire them when convenient.
- **Differential racialization.** Dominant society racialises different minority groups at different times in response to shifting needs such as the labour market.
- **Intersectionality and anti-essentialism.** No person has a single, unitary identity. See [[intersectionality]].
- **Voice of colour.** Because of their different histories, writers of colour may be able to communicate matters that white readers are unlikely to know. Delgado and Stefancic note that this thesis coexists in some tension with anti-essentialism.

## Counterstorytelling
The legal storytelling movement urges writers of colour to recount their experiences of racism and the legal system and to use their own perspectives to assess law's master narratives. Delgado's 1989 article, "Storytelling for Oppositionists and Others: A Plea for Narrative", is a founding text. A counterstory is a narrative that challenges the account told by the dominant group.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is weak and should not be overstated. Like [[critical_theory]], CRT concerns how beliefs and institutions serve interests, a topic Active Inference can model only indirectly, for instance as shared priors shaped by social context. The question of whose narratives count as evidence relates to [[epistemic_justice]]. These are discussion prompts, not results in either field.
