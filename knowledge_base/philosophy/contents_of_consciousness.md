# Contents of Consciousness

## Overview
The contents of consciousness are the items of experience of which a subject is aware at a given time, such as perceptions, bodily sensations, emotions, thoughts and mental images. The notion is usually contrasted with the level or state of consciousness, which concerns whether and how wakefully a subject is conscious, as in coma, sleep or wakefulness. Bayne, Hohwy and Owen (2016) argued that this contrast is useful but that the two dimensions are not fully independent, since clinical states differ in both.

## Main issues
- **Access and phenomenal consciousness.** Block (1995) distinguished phenomenal consciousness, what an experience is like, from access consciousness, availability of content for reasoning, report and action. Whether the two can come apart is disputed, and the answer affects what counts as a conscious content.
- **Neural correlates.** Chalmers (2000) defined a neural correlate of a specific content as a system whose state suffices for that conscious content, and distinguished such content-specific correlates from correlates of the conscious state generally. Experimental paradigms such as binocular rivalry, blindsight and masking contrast conscious with nonconscious processing of the same stimulus (see [[binocular_rivalry]], [[blindsight]]).
- **Nature of content.** Representationalists hold that the phenomenal character of an experience is fixed by what it represents, whereas others hold that [[qualia]] have intrinsic properties that go beyond representational content.
- **Theories of access.** Global workspace theories associate conscious content with information broadcast across the brain (see [[global_workspace]]), while integrated information theory ties content to the structure of a system's cause-effect repertoire.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive-processing accounts treat perceptual content as the hypothesis that currently best explains sensory input, so that what is experienced corresponds to the winning posterior beliefs (Hohwy, 2013). Binocular rivalry has been modelled this way as alternation between competing hypotheses. This is an active research programme, but it explains the selection of perceptual content more readily than why any content is accompanied by experience, a question that remains open (see [[hard_problem]]).
