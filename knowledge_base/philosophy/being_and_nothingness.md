# Being and Nothingness

## Overview
*Being and Nothingness* (*L'Être et le néant*, 1943) is Jean-Paul Sartre's major philosophical work, subtitled "An Essay on Phenomenological Ontology". Drawing on Husserl and Heidegger, it offers an analysis of consciousness, freedom and human relations and is a principal source of twentieth-century existentialism.

## Main themes
- **Being-in-itself and being-for-itself.** Sartre distinguishes the *en-soi*, the inert, self-identical being of things, from the *pour-soi*, the being of consciousness, which is not identical with itself because it is always directed beyond itself and is aware of what it is not.
- **Nothingness.** Negation, absence and questioning, Sartre argues, reveal that consciousness introduces nothingness into the world. A person looking for a friend in a café discovers the friend's absence, which is a real feature of the experience.
- **Freedom.** Because consciousness is not determined by what it is, human beings are described as "condemned to be free". Freedom brings anguish, and it is limited by [[facticity]], the situation one finds oneself in.
- **Bad faith.** Bad faith (*mauvaise foi*) is a form of self-deception in which a person denies either freedom or facticity, as in Sartre's example of the waiter who plays at being a waiter.
- **Being-for-others.** In the analysis of "the look", the experience of being seen by another reveals one's existence as an object for someone else, a source of shame and conflict.

Sartre's later work revised several of these positions, and the book's ontology has been criticised on grounds such as the strong separation of the two kinds of being. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry (Reynolds and Renaudie) gives an overview, and Barnes's translation (1956) was long the standard English text.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct link between Sartre's ontology and Active Inference. Some scholars in the enactive and phenomenological traditions treat the book as a source for claims about the pre-reflective, embodied character of experience, a theme shared with [[existential_phenomenology]]. These are historical and conceptual affinities, not derivations.
