# Specious Present

## Overview
The specious present is the duration of time that is present to consciousness in a single experience. The phrase was introduced by E. R. Clay and taken up by William James in The Principles of Psychology (1890), who described the present of experience as a "saddle-back" with a short breadth, which can be looked at both forwards and backwards. It is called specious because strictly the present instant has no duration, yet experience seems to present us with events that unfold, as when we hear a melody or a spoken word as a unit instead of a series of unconnected points.

## Models
Philosophers distinguish two main kinds of account, as summarised by Dainton (2000):
- **Retentional models.** Husserl, in his lectures on internal time consciousness, analysed experience as having a primal impression of what is now, retention of what has just been, and protention of what is about to occur. Contents are presented in the present as having just passed, even though they are no longer sensed. Only the current impression is strictly experienced.
- **Extensional models.** Experience itself has temporal extent: successive phases are directly experienced together, so that change and persistence can be experienced as such. Broad and other philosophers held views of this type.

A hybrid is also defended. The length of the specious present is not a fixed quantity; estimates in the empirical literature vary with task and method, and range from fractions of a second to a few seconds.

## Debates
Questions include how the specious present relates to continuous change in the brain, whether it is better characterised as a relation among contents or as a feature of acts, and how it fits with the apparent flow of time (see [[time_consciousness]], [[phenomenology]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
Several authors draw a parallel between the Husserlian structure and temporally deep generative models, in which beliefs about the recent past and expected near future are represented alongside beliefs about the present state. Retention may be read as the carrying forward of posterior beliefs about earlier states, and protention as predictions about the next ones. This is a proposed interpretation that relates phenomenology to a formal model; it is not a derivation, and the mapping is disputed (see [[temporal_models]]). Varela (1999) also sought links with neural dynamics.
