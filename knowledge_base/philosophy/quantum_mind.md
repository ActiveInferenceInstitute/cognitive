# Quantum Mind

## Overview
Harald Atmanspacher's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry "Quantum Approaches to Consciousness" begins by noting that quantum events certainly occur in the brain as elsewhere in the material world, but that it is controversial whether they are efficacious for the aspects of brain activity correlated with mental activity. The original motivation in the early twentieth century was philosophical. Collapse-type quantum events introduce a randomness that is primary (ontic) and not due to ignorance, and this was seen as leaving room for conscious free decisions that a deterministic world might seem to exclude. The entry sorts the proposals into several families.

## Quantum brain approaches
- **Stapp.** Conscious acts are linked to quantum state reductions.
- **Beck and Eccles.** Quantum mechanics acts at the synaptic cleft, with the ontic randomness of quantum events providing room for mental causation.
- **Vitiello and Freeman.** A quantum field theory of brain states.
- **Penrose and Hameroff.** Elementary acts of consciousness are non-algorithmic and are realised as gravitation-induced "objective reductions" of coherent superpositions of tubulin states in microtubules. Penrose's starting point is the non-computability of mathematical insight and his view that quantum gravity is needed for a proper theory of quantum measurement; the neurophysiological proposal was developed with Stuart Hameroff (1996). The scenario depends on physics that is not yet established.
- **Decoherence objection.** Max Tegmark (2000) estimated the decoherence time of tubulin superpositions in the brain to be less than $10^{-12}$ s, far shorter than the millisecond time scales of microtubule processes. Responses by Hagan and colleagues (2002) argued for longer times under particular assumptions, and the debate continues.

## Quantum approaches to mind
A second family applies the formalism of quantum theory (complementarity, contextuality, entanglement, non-Boolean logic) to mental systems without claiming that the brain is a quantum system. The entry notes that "non-commutative structures in cognition" might be a more precise name than "quantum cognition". Applications include decision processes, order effects and bistable perception (see [[quantum_cognition]] and [[quantum_logic]]). Atmanspacher argues that quantum features in psychology do not by themselves imply quantum physics in the brain. A third family, dual-aspect approaches such as the Pauli-Jung conjecture, treat mind and matter as aspects of one underlying psychophysically neutral reality (see [[dual_aspect_theory]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is limited. The free energy principle is a classical statistical framework, and it does not require that quantum processes be relevant to consciousness. The quantum-cognition family overlaps with Active Inference only in using probabilistic models of judgment, with the proviso that it replaces the Kolmogorov axioms of classical probability by non-commutative ones. Proposals that consciousness depends on non-computable physics (Penrose) conflict with the computational reading of the brain that is usual in Active Inference. Neither framework is a consequence of the other.
