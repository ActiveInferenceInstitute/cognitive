# Altered Consciousness

## Overview
Altered consciousness, or altered states of consciousness, refers to conditions in which the quality, content or level of awareness differs markedly from ordinary waking experience. Examples include dreaming, hypnosis, meditative absorption, intoxication, psychedelic experience, delirium and general anaesthesia. Tart (1969) helped popularise the term in psychology, characterising such states by a qualitative shift in the overall pattern of mental functioning, not merely a change in intensity.

## Characterisation
Researchers describe altered states along several dimensions. Common ones include changes in perception, sense of time, sense of self, emotional tone, thought content and sense of control. Vaitl and colleagues (2005) reviewed the induction of such states by spontaneous, physical, psychological, pharmacological and pathological means, and proposed dimensions such as activation, awareness span, self-awareness and sensory dynamics. A distinction is often drawn between changes in the level of consciousness (as in sleep or coma) and changes in its contents or character (as in psychedelic states). See [[consciousness]] and [[consciousness_and_awareness]] for the baseline notions.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive-processing and active inference accounts have been applied most actively to psychedelic and meditative states. Carhart-Harris and colleagues proposed the "entropic brain" hypothesis, that psychedelic states are associated with increased entropy in spontaneous brain activity (Carhart-Harris et al., 2014). Carhart-Harris and Friston (2019) later proposed the REBUS model, in which psychedelics relax the precision of high-level prior beliefs so that bottom-up signals exert greater influence and the brain's hierarchical priors are more readily revised. These are hypotheses under empirical test, supported by some neuroimaging and pharmacological data and disputed on details such as the role of specific receptor mechanisms. For other altered states, such as anaesthesia, sleep and dissociative conditions, the connection is less developed and mostly descriptive.
