# Existential Risk

## Overview
An existential risk is a hazard that could cause the extinction of humanity or permanently and drastically curtail its potential. Bostrom (2002) introduced the term in this sense and distinguished it from risks that are large but recoverable. Ord (2020) and others frame the topic partly through the value of the long-term future, which is a contested ethical premise, since the concern also follows from the more widely shared view that extinction would be a grave harm.

## Categories and assessment
- **Natural hazards.** Large asteroid or comet impacts, supervolcanic eruptions and nearby stellar explosions have occurred in Earth's history. Their frequency is better constrained by geology and astronomy than that of other categories.
- **Anthropogenic hazards.** Nuclear war, engineered pandemics, severe climate and ecological change, and advanced technologies including artificial intelligence are the usual candidates. Many authors regard these as more probable in the near term than natural ones.
- **Global catastrophic risk.** Bostrom and Cirkovic (2008) use this wider category for events that kill or harm on a very large scale without necessarily ending the species.
- **Methodological difficulty.** Existential events have no repeat observations, so probability estimates rest on expert judgement, models and reference classes, and published figures differ widely. Observation selection effects complicate inference from the fact that humanity has survived so far.
- **Responses.** Proposals include monitoring, governance and treaty design, safety research, and building resilient institutions (see [[risk_assessment]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is limited. Active Inference is not a theory of catastrophic risk. Contact occurs through [[ai_safety]], where agents that act to minimise expected free energy with explicit preferences are explored as an alternative architecture to reward maximisers, and whether this changes the risk profile is an open research question. Risk-sensitive decision making and uncertainty about the model enter the framework through the expected free energy, but the framework offers no estimate of existential probabilities.
