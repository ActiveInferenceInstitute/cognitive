# Religious Exclusivism

## Overview
Religious exclusivism is the view that the doctrines of one religion are true, and that conflicting claims of other religions are false. In many formulations it also holds that salvation or the highest religious good is available only through that religion. It is one of three positions in a typology widely used in the philosophy and theology of religions, set out by Race (1983) for Christian thought: exclusivism, inclusivism (the one religion is normative but others may partly share its truth or salvific benefit) and pluralism (several religions are valid responses to the divine). The label is descriptive and the position is held, in various forms, within many traditions.

## Forms
- **Soteriological exclusivism**: only adherents of one religion are saved. The historical Christian formula extra ecclesiam nulla salus ("no salvation outside the church") is often cited, though its interpretation has varied and some churches have since adopted inclusivist readings.
- **Doctrinal or epistemic exclusivism**: one set of religious beliefs is true, while remaining silent on salvation.
- **Moderate versions**: other traditions may contain truth or moral insight, but only one is fully accurate.

## Arguments
Plantinga (2000) defended the rationality of exclusivism, arguing that believers who are aware of religious diversity are not thereby irrational in holding their beliefs, given that they may be properly basic or the result of a reliable faculty. He compared the situation to other areas where disagreement among informed people does not oblige one to withdraw one's view. Critics respond that awareness of diversity should lower confidence, that belief seems correlated with place and upbringing, and that exclusivism appears arbitrary or morally troubling if it condemns many. Hick (1989) developed pluralism in part as a response. Exclusivists reply that the epistemic and moral objections do not show their claims to be false, and some argue that exclusivism does not imply hostility toward others.

## Relation to cognitive science
The cognitive science of religion studies how religious beliefs are acquired and transmitted. Its findings concern how beliefs arise and do not by themselves show whether they are true. Active Inference has no specific bearing on exclusivism.
