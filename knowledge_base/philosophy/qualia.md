# Qualia

## Overview

Qualia (singular quale) are the introspectively accessible, subjective qualities of experience: the redness of red, the painfulness of pain, the taste of coffee. The term is used for the "phenomenal" aspect of mental states, as distinct from their functional role in perception, memory or behaviour. Whether qualia exist as a separate category, and whether they can be explained by physical or computational theories, is a central dispute in [[philosophy_of_mind]].

## Arguments and positions

- **Explanatory gap and hard problem**: knowing all physical facts about colour vision seems not to tell us what seeing red is like. Chalmers framed this as the hard problem of consciousness, in contrast with "easy" problems about functions such as discrimination and report.
- **Knowledge argument**: Frank Jackson's thought experiment about Mary, who knows all the physical facts about colour but has never seen colour, is meant to show that experience adds knowledge not captured by physical description. Physicalists reply that she gains an ability or a new mode of access, not new facts.
- **Inverted spectrum and zombies**: conceivability arguments claim that functionally identical systems could differ in experience, so function does not entail qualia. Critics doubt that conceivability shows metaphysical possibility.
- **Eliminativism and illusionism**: Dennett and later illusionists argue that the intuitive notion of qualia is mistaken, and what needs explaining is why we judge that we have them.
- **Representationalism**: phenomenal character is identical to represented content of a certain kind, so qualia are not extra intrinsic properties.
- **Integrated information and other theories** posit specific physical or structural conditions for experience; their empirical status is contested (see [[integrated_information]]).

## Role in Active Inference

Active Inference is a theory of inference, perception and action, not a solution to the hard problem, and most of its literature says so. Its contribution to the topic is narrower. Predictive processing frames perceptual experience as the brain's best hypothesis about the causes of sensation, which gives an account of why experience has the structure it has (for example, colour constancy and perceptual illusions). Work on interoceptive inference links affective qualities to inference about the body's physiological state, and proposals that invoke counterfactual richness or temporal depth of generative models attempt to say which systems are conscious. Whether any of this explains why there is something it is like at all remains disputed, and a deflationary or illusionist reading is available.
