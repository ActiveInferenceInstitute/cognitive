# Bushido Ethics

## Overview
Bushido, "the Way of the warrior", names a code of conduct associated with the samurai of Japan, emphasising loyalty, honour, self-discipline and readiness to die. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy counts it as one of five "fountainheads" of Japanese philosophy and the only one that did not originate from abroad. Its history is more complicated than the common image of an ancient and unchanging code, and a responsible account has to separate the older martial codes from the modern ideology that took the name.

## Roots
For centuries before the Meiji restoration of 1868, Japan had locally produced martial codes and edifying handbooks for warriors. The SEP also mentions the Akō Incident of 1701, in which forty-seven masterless samurai avenged their lord, later lionised in popular literature and drama, as an event that foregrounded self-sacrificial death as the fullest expression of loyalty. Rinzai Zen masters sometimes used the idea of the "death of the ego" in training samurai who joined monasteries during the Tokugawa peace, which may have strengthened a cult of death (see [[chan_zen]]). Bushido also emphasised *makoto*, genuineness or trustworthiness, a term with Shinto associations.

## Formalisation in the modern period
According to the SEP, bushido was formalised only in the modern period as a school of thought with a political, ethical and ethnic ideology in service to the entire nation. Loyalty, originally a generalised lower-order Confucian virtue, was given a special meaning by being linked directly to the emperor within State Shinto. Nineteenth-century European ideologies of ethnic virtue and racial purity were added, so that bushido was a hybrid of Confucian, Buddhist, Shinto and European parentage. It claimed to be a philosophy of the Japanese people reaching back to ancient times, but its philosophical birthdate, the SEP says, is clearly the nineteenth century, and its tenets were couched in terms deemed traditional while often carrying newly acquired nuances. Protected by censorship and institutionalised in the national curriculum, it was almost impervious to philosophical critique, and it affected the flow of other currents of Japanese thought for the first half of the twentieth century. The historian Oleg Benesch (2014) studies this process in detail in *Inventing the Way of the Samurai*.

## Evaluation
Because of this history, bushido is best treated as an ideology and a historical construction as much as a philosophical ethics. Its moral content can be compared with ethical traditions that stress virtues of character (see [[virtue_ethics]]) or duties attached to roles (see [[deontological_ethics]]), but the comparison should respect the fact that the doctrine was shaped by political purposes.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct connection. One could note that the code works as a set of shared norms that shape expectations and conduct within a group, which resembles the way social norms are treated in accounts of social cognition, but this is a general observation and not particular to bushido.
