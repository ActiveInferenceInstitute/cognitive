# Deconstruction

## Overview
Deconstruction is an approach to reading and philosophical analysis developed by Jacques Derrida from the late 1960s. It examines how texts and concepts depend on hierarchical oppositions, such as speech and writing, presence and absence, nature and culture, and shows how the privileged term depends on the one it excludes. The aim is to expose internal tensions and the limits of systems of meaning, not to destroy them or to declare that texts mean anything at all.

## Key ideas
- **Logocentrism.** Derrida argued that Western philosophy has repeatedly treated spoken, present meaning as the origin of truth and writing as a derivative supplement (Of Grammatology, 1967).
- **Différance.** A coined term combining differing and deferral: meaning arises from differences among signs and is never fully present in any one of them.
- **Reading strategy.** A deconstructive reading attends to marginal passages, metaphors and exceptions that undercut a text's explicit claims, and then displaces the opposition instead of simply reversing it.
- **Debates.** The exchange with Austin's followers, notably Searle, over [[speech_acts]], and Derrida's engagement with Husserl's [[phenomenology]], are standard points of entry.

## Reception
Deconstruction influenced literary theory, legal theory, architecture and cultural studies, and is a central strand of [[postmodernism]]. Critics, including many analytic philosophers, regard it as obscure or as encouraging [[relativism]]. Derrida and sympathetic readers deny this, stressing close textual argument and an ethical commitment to what exceeds a given system.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no real link. Deconstruction concerns meaning in language and texts, while Active Inference is a formal account of inference and action in embodied agents. A distant analogy might be drawn between the claim that meaning is differential and the way predictions are scored against alternatives, but neither literature supports such a connection, and it should not be treated as one.
