# Constructivism

## Overview
Constructivism holds that knowers actively build their understanding, and, in stronger versions, the reality they take themselves to know, through their activity, concepts and social interaction. The term is used in several fields: in psychology and education for learning theories, in epistemology for views about the structure of knowledge, and in sociology for the claim that institutions and categories are made by human practice. Other senses, such as constructivism in the foundations of mathematics, are distinct.

## Forms
- **Cognitive constructivism.** Piaget (1952) described development as the child's construction of schemes through assimilation and accommodation, so that knowledge grows through interaction with the world and is not copied from it (see [[cognitive_development]]).
- **Social constructivism.** Vygotsky stressed learning as mediated by culture, language and others. Berger and Luckmann (1966) argued that social reality is produced and sustained by habitualisation and institutionalisation.
- **Radical constructivism.** Von Glasersfeld (1995) held that knowledge does not represent an independent reality but is judged by its viability in organising experience, a stance close to some forms of pragmatism.
- **Criticism.** Critics say strong versions risk [[relativism]] or confuse the construction of concepts with the construction of facts. Moderate forms hold that concepts are constructed while the world constrains which constructions succeed.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive processing is often described as a constructivist theory of perception: the brain does not receive the world but generates hypotheses and corrects them with prediction errors, an idea with Kantian and Helmholtzian roots (see [[bayesian_brain]]). The affinity is real for perception and learning, and the enactive extension connects it with views of knowledge as action-oriented (see [[enactivism]]). It is not a commitment to anti-realism, since models are scored against sensory evidence.
