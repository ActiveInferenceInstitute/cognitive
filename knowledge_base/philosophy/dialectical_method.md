# Dialectical Method

## Overview
Dialectic is a family of methods in which understanding is advanced through the confrontation of opposed claims. The term has different senses in different periods, and the sense should be specified when it is invoked. This page summarises the main traditions and attributes assessments to their authors.

## Traditions
- **Socratic and Platonic.** In Plato's early dialogues the method of question and answer (*elenchus*) tests a claim by drawing out consequences that conflict with the respondent's other beliefs. In the *Republic*, dialectic is presented as the highest form of philosophical enquiry, ascending toward understanding of the Forms.
- **Aristotelian.** Aristotle's *Topics* treats dialectic as reasoning from generally accepted opinions, distinguished from demonstration, which starts from premises that are true and primary.
- **Hegelian.** Hegel's dialectic describes how concepts or forms of consciousness develop through the internal tensions they contain, each stage being negated and preserved in a more comprehensive one, as in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807). The popular triad "thesis, antithesis, synthesis" is often attributed to Hegel, but Hegel did not use it as his general scheme. Maybee describes it as a traditional reading that fits only some of his transitions, and Mueller (1958) is a classic criticism of it. The three terms are associated with Fichte, and the formula was popularised as a label for Hegel's method by later commentators such as Chalybäus.
- **Marxian.** Marx and Engels adapted dialectics to material and social history, describing change as driven by contradictions in economic relations. Interpretations of this adaptation, and its application to nature, are disputed.

## Assessment
Popper (1940) criticised dialectical reasoning as tolerating contradiction in a way that, if taken literally, would make any statement derivable, and he held that science progresses by conjecture and refutation instead. Defenders reply that dialectic uses "contradiction" in a non-formal sense for tensions between aspects of a developing whole. Critical theorists such as Adorno developed a "negative dialectics" that resists synthesis.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no formal connection. A loose analogy is sometimes drawn between dialectical development and the way models are revised when predictions conflict with evidence, but belief updating in Active Inference follows probability theory and not a dialectical logic, and the comparison is a heuristic.
