# Syllogistic Logic

## Overview
Syllogistic logic is the system of deductive reasoning founded by Aristotle in the Prior Analytics. A syllogism derives a conclusion from two premises that share a middle term. In the standard example, "All humans are mortal; all Greeks are humans; therefore all Greeks are mortal", the term "humans" links the premises and is absent from the conclusion. It was the dominant formal logic in the West for roughly two thousand years.

## Structure
Categorical statements have four forms: A, "all S are P"; E, "no S are P"; I, "some S are P"; and O, "some S are not P". The subject and predicate of the conclusion are the minor and major terms, and the premise containing the major term is the major premise. The position of the middle term defines the figure, and the sequence of statement types defines the mood. Medieval logicians named valid moods by mnemonics such as Barbara (AAA in the first figure) and Celarent (EAE). Whether some forms are valid depends on existential import, the assumption that general terms denote non-empty classes: traditional logic accepts it, while modern predicate logic does not, and so counts fewer forms as valid.

## Modern reconstruction
Łukasiewicz (1951) reconstructed the syllogistic as a formal axiomatic system, and Smith's survey describes later developments. Syllogistic can be embedded in first-order logic ([[predicate_logic]]) as a restricted fragment, with the advantage of being decidable, but it cannot express relations with multiple quantifiers, a limitation that motivated the development of modern logic.

## Psychology of syllogistic reasoning
Experiments show that people often accept or reject conclusions on the basis of believability and not logical validity. Evans, Barston and Pollard (1983) reported such belief bias in syllogistic reasoning. Johnson-Laird's mental models theory and probabilistic accounts such as the probability heuristics model of Chater and Oaksford (1999) offer competing explanations (see [[deductive_reasoning]], [[reasoning_problem_solving]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is only indirect. Active Inference describes reasoning in probabilistic terms, and probabilistic approaches to syllogistic reasoning, in which premises are treated as uncertain information, fit better with that tradition than classical deduction. The framework does not itself contain a theory of syllogistic inference.
