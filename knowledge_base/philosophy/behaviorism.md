# Behaviorism

## Overview
Behaviorism holds that psychology should study observable behaviour and its environmental determinants, not inner mental states. The term covers three distinct positions: a methodological rule for science, a theory of learning, and a philosophical analysis of mental vocabulary.

## Core claims
**Methodological behaviorism.** Watson (1913) argued psychology should be an objective natural science whose data are stimuli and responses, rejecting introspection as unscientific. This is closely aligned with [[operationalism]]: constructs must be defined by measurement procedures, a view that appealed to the [[logical_positivism]] of the time.

**Radical behaviorism.** Skinner treated private events such as thoughts as behaviour subject to the same laws, not as causes. Learning is operant conditioning: behaviour is shaped by its consequences through reinforcement schedules. Thorndike's law of effect and Pavlovian conditioning supply the mechanisms of RL's lineage.

**Philosophical (logical) behaviorism.** Ryle and Hempel proposed that statements about mental states are translatable into statements about behavioural dispositions. Objections: a person may be in pain without behaving painfully, and any disposition list needs further mental terms (the circularity objection).

**Decline.** Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* argued stimulus-response accounts cannot explain linguistic productivity; the cognitive revolution reintroduced internal representations. Functionalism ([[philosophy_of_mind]]) kept the behavioural criterion but allowed internal states as causes.

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference is explicitly non-behaviorist in its models: agents have a generative model and beliefs that are not reducible to stimulus-response mappings. Yet the framework can be read in a behaviorism-adjacent way. If beliefs are attributed only because the "as-if" description fits an agent's behaviour, the stance is [[instrumentalism]], close in spirit to Dennett's intentional stance. Preferences in the $C$ vector are likewise inferred from behaviour. Reinforcement learning, behaviorism's computational descendant, is the main contrast: [[reinforcement_learning]] maximises reward, while Active Inference reframes reward as prior preference. The two agree that behaviour is the evidence, but disagree on whether to posit hidden states that explain it.
