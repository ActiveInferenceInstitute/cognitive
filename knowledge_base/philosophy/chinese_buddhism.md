# Chinese Buddhism

## Overview
Buddhism reached China from India roughly two thousand years ago, during the Han dynasty. According to the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's overview, it is generally agreed that it entered along several trade routes in the first century CE, with the northern Silk Road the most prominent. Monastic and study sites grew up at places such as Dunhuang, Chang'an and Luoyang. As early as the second century CE monks such as Lokaksema, from Gandhara, began translating sutras and commentaries from Sanskrit into Chinese, and the most famous of the translators was Xuanzang (602-664), who travelled to India and established a translation school at Chang'an. The *Dhammapada* was translated into Chinese about 224 CE. Over the following centuries Buddhism became, together with Confucianism and Daoism, one of the "three teachings" (*sanjiao*) used to characterise Chinese thought. For the wider setting see [[chinese_philosophy]] and [[buddhist_philosophy]].

## Chinese schools
Persecutions of Buddhism in the 570s disrupted monastic institutions, and, as the SEP entry on Huayan describes, monks who sought forms of Buddhism better suited to a Chinese context turned to scriptures that had received little attention in the established Indian schools. The result was creative appropriation and schools with no direct Indian counterpart.
- **Tiantai.** A school of entirely Chinese origin, flourishing under its fourth patriarch Zhiyi (538-597), who held that the Lotus Sutra contains the supreme teaching. Its distinctive claim is that there is only one reality, which is both everyday phenomenal existence and nirvana, so that the ten ways of existing, from hell beings to Buddhas, share one world. Zhiyi's Threefold Truth holds that things can be truly described, that all things are empty and impermanent, and that the phenomenal world is real and at the same time ultimately empty.
- **Consciousness-only.** Created by Xuanzang, influenced by the Indian Yogacara master Vasubandhu, whose *Thirty Stanzas* Xuanzang commented on in the *Cheng weishi lun*. Its central tenet is that nothing exists but consciousness, a position the IEP calls idealism.
- **Huayan.** The Flower Garland school, which teaches that one is all and all is one (see [[huayan]]).
- **Chan.** A form of Buddhism developed in China between the sixth and eighth centuries and later known in Japan as Zen (see [[chan_zen]]).

## Interaction with other traditions
The three teachings influenced each other. Huayan vocabulary and metaphors shaped Chan and Song and Ming Neo-Confucianism, and moral texts of the Song period combined Daoist, Confucian and Buddhist elements. The Buddhist notion of karma was associated with the older Chinese idea of response to action (*ganying*) in such texts.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is indirect. Buddhist analyses of the self as a construction are sometimes compared with accounts of the self in Active Inference (see [[buddhist_philosophy]]). The Chinese schools add particular metaphysical claims, such as Yogacara idealism or Huayan's mutual inclusion, that the framework neither requires nor tests, and the comparison is an interpretive one only.
