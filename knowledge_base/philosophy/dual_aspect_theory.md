# Dual-Aspect Theory

## Overview
Dual-aspect theory is a position in the philosophy of mind on which the mental and the physical are two aspects, or ways of being described, of a single underlying reality, which is not itself either mental or physical in the ordinary sense. It is a form of monism that aims to avoid both substance dualism and reduction of one side to the other. The view is often traced to Spinoza, for whom thought and extension are two attributes of one substance.

## Variants
- **Spinozist attributes.** Mind and body are attributes of one substance, in parallel and without causal interaction between them.
- **Neutral monism.** Mach, James and Russell proposed a neutral stuff from which both mental and physical arise (see [[neutral_monism]]), and the term dual-aspect is often applied when the neutral basis is given two aspects instead of being constructed.
- **Pauli and Jung.** The physicist Wolfgang Pauli and the psychologist Carl Jung proposed a psychophysically neutral domain underlying both matter and mind. Later authors, including Atmanspacher, have developed this line.
- **Information.** Chalmers (1996) suggested that information may have a physical and a phenomenal aspect, as a speculative way of relating the two.

Objections concern whether the neutral basis is explanatory, how the two aspects are related, and whether the view collapses into [[panpsychism]] or [[materialism]]. Supporters reply that the view reframes the [[hard_problem]] as a problem of two descriptions.

## Relation to Active Inference
In the free energy literature, Friston, Wiese and Hobson (2020) described a "Markovian monism", a dual-aspect view on which the intrinsic (experiential) and extrinsic (physical) descriptions of a system correspond to the inside and outside of a Markov blanket (see [[markov_blanket]]), and Solms (2021) adopts dual-aspect monism in relating affect to the free energy principle. This is a philosophical interpretation advanced by proponents. It does not follow from the mathematics of the framework, and critics question whether it addresses the hard problem or only relabels it.
