# Genealogy

## Overview
In philosophy, genealogy is a way of investigating a concept, value or institution by tracing the historical conditions from which it emerged, usually in order to show that it is neither natural nor inevitable. It is distinct from the ordinary sense of tracing a family tree, and from a search for a single noble origin. The method is associated with Friedrich Nietzsche and, after him, Michel Foucault.

## Nietzsche
Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887) asks where moral values such as "good" and "evil" came from and what interests and psychological types they express. In the account given by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Nietzsche seeks to understand in naturalistic terms the type of person who would bear a given set of values, and he attributes the morality he criticises to *ressentiment*. The inquiry is also an evaluative one: tracing the origin of a value is meant to raise the question of what the value is worth.

## Foucault
Foucault took the term from Nietzsche. In the Stanford Encyclopedia's presentation, his early method, archaeology, described systems of thought as governed by rules operating beneath the awareness of individual subjects, but it could compare periods without explaining how one gave way to another. Genealogy, first deployed in *Discipline and Punish* (1975), was intended to remedy this. Foucault meant the word to evoke Nietzsche's genealogy of morals, with its suggestion of complex, mundane and inglorious origins that form no part of a grand scheme of progress. The aim is to show that a system of thought arose from contingent turns of history, not from rationally inevitable trends. *Discipline and Punish* applies this to modern imprisonment, arguing that the reforms that made punishment gentler also became vehicles of more effective control, through hierarchical observation, normalising judgement and examination. Foucault's later genealogies extended the approach to the body, sexuality and the subject.

## Features of the method
- **Contingency.** A present practice is shown to have had alternatives, which opens it to criticism.
- **Origins as plural and unglamorous.** Genealogy looks for multiple, often low and accidental, sources and for shifts in function, instead of a single founding moment.
- **Power and knowledge.** Foucault's genealogies treat what counts as knowledge as bound up with the exercise of power, which is a main point of contact with [[critical_theory]], [[postmodernism]] and [[deconstruction]].
- **Debated critical force.** A standing objection is that exposing contingent origins does not by itself show a belief or value to be false or unjustified, an issue sometimes discussed under the heading of the genetic fallacy (see [[fallacies]] for informal fallacies in general).

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is loose. One could compare genealogy's claim that present categories carry traces of their history with the idea that an agent's priors are shaped by its past and its niche, but that comparison is an analogy and not a result in either field. Active Inference is a formal framework in the natural-science tradition, and genealogical critique does not rely on it.
