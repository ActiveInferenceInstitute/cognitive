# Biosemiotics

## Overview
Biosemiotics holds that sign processes, in which something stands for something else to an interpreting system, are not confined to human language and culture but are a basic feature of life. A bacterium responding to a chemical gradient, a plant reacting to light, and an animal reading a gesture are all treated as cases where an organism interprets a signal in terms of its own needs and history. The field draws on semiotics, theoretical biology and philosophy, and it is a minority but organised research area with its own journals and conferences.

## Foundations
- **Umwelt.** Jakob von Uexkull argued that each species lives in its own subjective perceptual and action world, built from the features that matter to it. A tick, for example, responds to a small set of cues such as warmth and a butyric acid odour, and these constitute its world.
- **Peircean semiotics.** Most contemporary biosemiotics adopts Peirce's triadic account of the sign: a sign vehicle, the object it stands for, and an interpretant, the effect produced in the interpreter (see [[peircean_semiotics]]). A triadic relation, as opposed to a dyadic cause-effect link, is what is said to distinguish signalling from mere physical interaction.
- **Code and interpretation.** Researchers such as Sebeok, Hoffmeyer and Barbieri discuss genetic coding, signal transduction and cell communication as semiotic processes, with debate over whether the genetic code qualifies as a true code or a convention-like mapping.

## Debates
Critics ask whether treating biochemistry in semiotic terms adds explanatory content or only redescribes known mechanisms, and whether meaning without a conscious interpreter risks anthropomorphism. Proponents respond that functional, goal-related properties of organisms need a vocabulary beyond efficient causation.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is a conceptual parallel. The Umwelt resembles the notion that an agent's generative model determines which features of the environment count as informative, and the Markov blanket formalism specifies the interface through which an organism senses and acts on its niche. Enactive approaches that stress sense-making by organisms are commonly cited as a bridge. Whether the free energy principle supplies a formal account of meaning in the biosemiotic sense is a matter of ongoing discussion and has not been settled.
