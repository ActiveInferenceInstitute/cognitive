# Colonial Discourse

## Overview
Colonial discourse analysis studies how the language, scholarship and imagery of colonial powers constructed the peoples they ruled, and how those constructions supported colonial domination. The field was launched largely by Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), which is credited with initiating postcolonial criticism in the West, and was developed by Homi Bhabha and Gayatri Spivak, who each reworked its assumptions.

## Said and Orientalism
Said analysed Western scholarship and writing about the Arab Islamic world and argued that it presented a biased, stereotyped vision that supported colonial policy. He treated Orientalism as a system of knowledge production that maintains power asymmetries: the West's image of "the Orient" depended on constructing the East as an "other", a process he described through the notion of "imaginative geography". The term itself, which had originally referred to the scholarly study of Asian societies, came after Said to be used critically for simplified and stereotyped conceptions of Eastern cultures. The approach shows the influence of Foucault's account of discourse as bound up with power (see [[genealogy]]).

## Bhabha: ambivalence, mimicry and hybridity
Bhabha, in the essay "Of Mimicry and Man: The Ambivalence of Colonial Discourse" (1984) and in *The Location of Culture* (1994), examined how colonialism produces complex and often contradictory relations between colonisers and colonised. He drew on Foucault, Derrida and Lacan and departed from what he saw as Said's binary opposition of coloniser and colonised.
- **Ambivalence.** The stereotyping of colonised peoples reveals as much about the anxieties of the colonisers as about those described, so colonial authority is never fully secure.
- **Mimicry.** Colonial discourse seeks to have the colonised adopt the colonisers' manners and language, yet the resulting imitation unsettles the authority it was meant to confirm.
- **Hybridity.** Cultures and identities that arise at the point of colonial contact are mixed and cannot be reduced to either side.
## Limits and debate
Bhabha's emphasis on ambivalence and internal division contrasts with the more binary picture he attributed to Said, and the wider field contains further disagreements that this page does not survey. The concept is one of several in postcolonial theory and is complemented by materialist and structural accounts such as [[coloniality_of_power]] and by Spivak's analysis of [[epistemic_violence]].

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is faint. Colonial discourse analysis concerns the social production of stereotypes, and in predictive terms a stereotype can be compared to a prior that is maintained because it is repeatedly confirmed by selective evidence. That comparison is a loose analogy, and neither Said nor Bhabha uses the framework or its formalism.
