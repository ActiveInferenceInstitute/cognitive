# Ubuntu Philosophy

## Overview
*Ubuntu* (also written *botho* and, in Shona, *hunhu*) is a term from the Bantu languages for the quality or essence of being a person. Fainos Mangena's Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry explains that it has attracted philosophical attention in Zimbabwe and South Africa in particular, where home-grown philosophies were sought after the liberation war and apartheid. Its clearest expression is the Nguni/Ndebele maxim *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*, in Shona *munhu munhu muvanhu*: "a person is a person through other persons". The entry presents it as an instance of the communal emphasis of African philosophy, contrasted with an emphasis on the individual in the West.

## Main themes
- **Relational personhood.** Ifeanyi Menkiti's statement, quoted by Kwame Gyekye in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, is that personhood in traditional African societies is something to be attained, "in direct proportion as one participates in communal life through the discharge of the various obligations defined by one's stations". Michael Onyebuchi Eze describes a person's subjectivity as in part constituted by the other persons with whom the social world is shared.
- **Metaphysics.** In Southern African thought, beings are understood relationally: a human being is in communion with other human beings as well as with the spiritual world, which the entry places ancestors and a supreme creator within. The entry cites Sekou Toure's phrase "the communion of persons".
- **Ethics.** In Mangena's account, moral standards arise through a "Common Moral Position", of which the community is source, author and custodian, set by elders in consultation with the spirit world, and personhood is defined as conformity to those standards. Gyekye's survey notes that African moral thought is characteristically humanistic and social, and that the common good takes a central place.
- **Epistemology.** Following Michael Battle, African epistemology "begins with community and moves to individuality". Knowledge is conveyed through elders' stories and proverbs.

## Criticism and debate
Eze cautions against romanticising a unanimous past that precedes the "violence of colonialism", and holds that the individual and the community are engaged in contemporaneous formation instead of being ranked. Mangena disagrees, maintaining that the relationship between the two is not one of equals. Thaddeus Metz has searched for a basic principle that could define African ethics, a project the entry cites among second-generation work. Ubuntu has also been deployed in the public sphere in post-apartheid and post-liberation politics.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is interpretive. A relational view of the self, in which one's identity depends on a network of others, can be compared with accounts of the self as a model that is shaped by social partners (see [[personal_identity]]). This is an analogy. Ubuntu is a moral and social philosophy, and the framework's formalism makes no commitments on ethics.
