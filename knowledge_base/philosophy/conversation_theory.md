# Conversation Theory

## Overview
Conversation theory is a framework developed by the cybernetician Gordon Pask, mainly in the 1970s, which treats learning and knowing as processes that take place in conversation. A conversation need not be between two people: the participants may be separate individuals, or distinct perspectives within one mind. Pask presented the theory in *Conversation, Cognition and Learning* (1975) and *Conversation Theory: Applications in Education and Epistemology* (1976), and tested it in experiments with computer-linked teaching systems. Bernard Scott later gave an account of it as a domain-independent constructivist model of human knowing.

## Main elements
- **Conversation as the unit of analysis.** Pask's summary of the approach is that mental activity can be described in terms of dialogue and behaviour. Understanding is something that has to be demonstrated within a conversation, under defined conditions, and not assumed from a learner's report.
- **Levels of discourse.** Descriptions distinguish talk in an object language about the subject matter from talk in a metalanguage about learning, that is, about how the subject matter is being understood, in addition to general natural-language discussion.
- **Teachback.** A learner shows understanding by explaining or reconstructing a topic back to the other participant. Agreement is reached when each party can confirm that the other's concepts reproduce their own.
- **Entailment meshes.** Subject matter is represented as a network of topics that entail one another. Pask first spoke of entailment structures and later treated them as a restricted case of meshes. A path through the mesh corresponds to a teaching or learning strategy.
- **Learning styles.** In experiments reported in 1976, Pask found variations in learning strategies and effects of matching or mismatching a teaching strategy to an individual's learning strategy. He is associated with the contrast between serialist learners, who proceed step by step, and holist learners, who look for higher-order relations.

## Significance
The theory is a product of the [[cybernetics]] tradition and is constructivist in outlook: knowledge is built and maintained through interaction between observers, not received from outside. It influenced educational technology, since the computer-linked systems it used recorded the strategies of learners in detail, and it is cited in discussions of [[constructivist_learning_theory]]. Its terminology and formal apparatus are idiosyncratic, and its claims were developed within Pask's own research programme, so it is best read alongside secondary accounts.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an analogy. Both frameworks treat a cognitive system as maintaining a model through exchanges with something it can act on and be informed by, and the idea that two parties reach agreement when each can reproduce the other's model resembles accounts of shared generative models in communicating agents. Pask's theory is not formulated in probabilistic terms, and no derivation links the two. See [[cybernetics]] for the shared lineage.
