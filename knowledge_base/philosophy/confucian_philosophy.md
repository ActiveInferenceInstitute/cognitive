# Confucian Philosophy

## Overview
Confucian philosophy is the tradition of thought tracing to Confucius (Kongzi, 551-479 BCE) and the sayings recorded in the Analects. It treats ethics as a practical project of self-cultivation within family, community and political roles, and holds that a good society depends on the moral example of its members, especially its rulers. It developed over more than two millennia and remains influential in East Asian thought and in contemporary comparative ethics.

## Central ideas
- **Ren (humaneness).** The central virtue of benevolent concern for others, expressed in practice and, in the Analects, through the injunction not to impose on others what one would not want oneself.
- **Li (ritual propriety).** Customary forms of conduct and ceremony that shape character and coordinate social life.
- **Roles and relationships.** Filial piety and the relations of parent and child, ruler and subject, and friends define the contexts where virtue is exercised. Some scholars describe this as a role ethics.
- **Junzi.** The exemplary person who cultivates moral excellence through study and reflection.
- **Classical development.** Mencius argued that human nature has innate moral sprouts to be nurtured, while Xunzi held that nature is unruly and is shaped by ritual and education.
- **Neo-Confucianism.** From the Song dynasty, thinkers such as Zhu Xi systematised the tradition around the investigation of principle (li) and self-cultivation, partly in dialogue with [[buddhist_philosophy]].

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no formal link. The most that can be said is interpretive: Confucian emphasis on habituation through repeated practice resembles accounts of skill and habit learning, and its attention to ritual and role resembles accounts of how shared expectations stabilise social behaviour (see [[social_norms]]). These parallels are analogies drawn by commentators, and the tradition makes normative claims that Active Inference does not.
