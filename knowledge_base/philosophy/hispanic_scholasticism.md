# Hispanic Scholasticism

## Overview
Hispanic or Spanish scholasticism is the intellectual movement of theologians and philosophers in sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Spain and Portugal, usually identified with the School of Salamanca. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that the School of Salamanca, in a broad sense, is more or less identical with Spanish Scholasticism, which also includes authors from the Spanish Netherlands, while in a narrow sense it refers to two or three generations of pupils of Francisco de Vitoria, the founding father in both senses. Its members built on the works of Thomas Aquinas but felt free to depart from them, especially in dealing with contemporary issues such as Spanish expansion in the Indies and the challenge of Protestantism. The entry's emphasis is on two Dominicans, Vitoria and Domingo de Soto, and two Jesuits, Luis de Molina and Francisco Suárez.

## Main areas of contribution
- **Natural law and political theory.** The SEP devotes a section to the School's treatment of natural law, political theory, ethics and casuistry, and notes that some of its members were willing to depart from Aristotle's teachings.
- **International law.** The SEP lists the School as among the forefathers of the law of nations (*ius gentium*). The writers separated the *ius gentium* from natural law, with which it had largely been identified since Roman law, developed a theory of just war from Aquinas's *Secunda secundae*, q. 40, and addressed the alleged right to enslave non-European peoples. Vitoria's lectures on the Indies, *De temperantia* (1537), *De Indis* (1539) and *De iure belli* (1539), considered cannibalism, whether a just war could be waged on indigenous peoples and whether Spain's title to the Indies was valid. In *De Indis* he argued that the Amerindians were true owners of their goods even if they were unbelievers or sinners. James Brown Scott called Vitoria the father of modern international law, mainly on the basis of these lectures.
- **Economic theory.** The SEP notes that some members were early theorists of monetarist economics.
- **Metaphysics and logic.** The movement includes Soto's work on logic and Suárez's metaphysics, which had a wide later influence.

## Evaluation
The SEP treats the School's writings on the Indies and on the enslavement of Amerindians, Africans and others at length, and assessments of them differ. This page notes the debate and does not attempt to settle it. For a Latin American philosophical response to this colonial history see [[ethics_of_liberation]].

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct connection. The scholastic tradition belongs to theology, law and metaphysics before the development of probabilistic models of cognition. A remote point of contact is the discussion of how reason and belief relate in ethics and faith (see [[faith_and_reason]]), but that is a topic in philosophy of religion and not in Active Inference.
