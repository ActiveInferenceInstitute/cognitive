# Unity of Science

## Overview
The unity of science is the idea that the different sciences are connected, by shared methods, by common concepts, or by relations of reduction among their theories. The view has a long history, with appeals to a single method in early modern thought, but the programme took its modern form in the logical positivism of the Vienna Circle. Neurath, Carnap and Morris organised the International Encyclopedia of Unified Science from 1938 to promote it.

## Forms of unity
- **Methodological unity.** All empirical sciences share basic standards of evidence and explanation (see [[scientific_method]]).
- **Reductive unity.** Oppenheim and Putnam (1958) proposed unity as a working hypothesis, with theories of higher-level sciences reducible in principle to those of lower levels, from social groups to organisms, cells, molecules and atoms. This is a form of [[reductionism]].
- **Unity through integration.** Disciplines are linked by interfield theories and shared explanatory models without full reduction.

## Disunity and anti-reductionism
Fodor (1974) argued that special sciences such as psychology or economics need not reduce to physics, because their kinds can be realised by many different physical kinds, so that laws of the special sciences cannot be mapped onto physical laws. Dupré (1993) defended the "disorder of things", the view that nature lacks a single hierarchical order and that different classifications serve different interests. Debates about [[emergence]] concern whether higher-level properties are in principle explicable from lower-level ones. Cat's survey in the Stanford Encyclopedia describes the movement from strong unity to pluralist and local approaches.

## Relation to Active Inference
The free energy principle has been presented by Friston (2010) as a candidate unifying theory of brain function, and proponents extend it to action, learning, life and social systems. Whether it unifies sciences by offering a shared formal principle, or only redescribes diverse phenomena in common terms, is contested. The principle's reach is also criticised as too general to yield testable differences. It may best be seen as a proposal for unity through a common formalism and not as a demonstration that the sciences reduce to one.
