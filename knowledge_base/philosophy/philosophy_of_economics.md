# Philosophy of Economics

## Overview
The philosophy of economics examines the concepts, methods and normative commitments of economic science. It overlaps with philosophy of science, ethics and political philosophy. Standing questions include what economic models represent, how theories should be tested, what rationality means for an agent, and how facts about welfare relate to values.

## Methodological questions
- **Positive and normative economics.** Robbins (1932) defined economics as the science of the allocation of scarce means among competing ends, and separated it from ethics. Friedman (1953) argued that assumptions of a model should be judged by the accuracy of its predictions, not by their realism, a position often called instrumentalism and criticised by others.
- **Models and idealisation.** Hausman (1992) described economics as an "inexact and separate science", relying on ceteris paribus laws and on a small set of partly idealised psychological assumptions.
- **Causal inference.** Debates concern when observational or experimental evidence supports causal claims about policies.

## Rationality and welfare
Rational choice theory models agents as holding consistent preferences and maximising expected utility ([[utility_theory]], [[decision_theory]]). Sen (1977) criticised the equation of rationality with the maximisation of self-interest, arguing that sympathy and commitment are part of behaviour. Behavioural economics documents systematic departures from the standard axioms, which prompts debate over whether these are errors or alternative rationalities. In welfare economics, questions of how to compare well-being across persons connect to [[distributive_justice]].

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an active but contested area of work. Active Inference replaces utility maximisation with minimising expected free energy, in which preferences appear as prior beliefs over outcomes, and it has been applied to economic behaviour and to modelling of markets (see [[socioeconomic_active_inference]]). Whether this offers a distinct account of rationality or a reformulation of expected utility with additional information-seeking terms is debated.
