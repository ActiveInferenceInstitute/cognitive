# Ecofeminism

## Overview
Ecofeminism, also called ecological feminism, is a family of positions that investigate the connections between the way women are treated and the way nature is treated. The French feminist Françoise d'Eaubonne coined the term "ecological feminisme" in 1974 to call attention to women's potential to bring about an ecological revolution. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that it first referred generically to a wide variety of women-nature connections drawn from history, literary criticism, political science, sociology and theology, and emerged as a distinctly philosophical position only in the late 1980s and early to mid 1990s. It is one of three kinds of position that entry identifies within feminist environmental philosophy, which is an umbrella for diverse and sometimes incompatible perspectives.

## Characterisation
Karen Warren's common-denominator characterisation of ecofeminist philosophy is that it explores the nature of the connections between the unjustified dominations of women and of nature, critiques male-biased views about women and nature in Western canonical philosophy, and creates alternatives to those views. Many ecofeminist philosophers distinguish between the oppression of women and the unjustified domination of nature, on the ground that only beings with characteristics such as rationality or sentience can be oppressed in the strict sense.

## Main themes
- **Oppressive conceptual frameworks.** Warren argues that the dominations of women, nonhuman animals and nature share conceptual roots in a framework built on hierarchical value dualisms and a "logic of domination", which holds that a characteristic such as reason, possessed by one group and not another, justifies subordination of the other.
- **Language.** Ecofeminists argue that language shapes and reinforces these frameworks, for example when women are animalised and nature is feminised in a culture that treats both as inferior.
- **History.** Carolyn Merchant (1980) argued that the culture/nature dualism is a product of the scientific revolution, which displaced an older image of nature as an organic, nurturing female with an image of nature as inert and mechanical. The SEP entry notes that historical accounts of the origins of these dominations conflict and are inconclusive.
- **Socioeconomic and epistemological perspectives.** Women, especially poor rural women in less developed countries, suffer disproportionate harms from deforestation, water pollution and environmental toxins. Ecofeminist epistemology extends the concerns of feminist epistemology to knowledge of nature.
- **Relation to deep ecology.** Ecofeminism's emergence was linked to deep ecology, but during the 1980s and 1990s a debate arose over deep ecology's criticism of anthropocentrism and its principle of self-realisation. Val Plumwood (1993) offered a prominent critique, which the SEP uses to illustrate basic ecofeminist claims.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is distant. Ecofeminism is a critical and ethical programme, and Active Inference is a formal framework with no stance on the justification of domination. One may note that Active Inference treats an organism and its environment as coupled in a way that resists a sharp culture/nature division (see [[enactivism]]), but this is a point of contact in subject matter, not a shared claim.
