# Panexperientialism

## Overview
Panexperientialism is the thesis that the fundamental constituents of the world have some form of experience, perhaps very rudimentary, without necessarily having minds, thought or consciousness in a human sense. The term is associated with David Ray Griffin, who used it to describe the position of Alfred North Whitehead and Charles Hartshorne, and to separate it from views that attribute full mentality to all things. It is usually treated as a version of, or a close relative to, [[panpsychism]].

## Whitehead's version
In Process and Reality (1929), Whitehead takes the basic units of reality to be momentary "actual occasions" instead of enduring material substances. Each occasion has a subjective aspect: it "prehends" earlier occasions and synthesises them into a unity, a process he describes in terms analogous to feeling. Ordinary objects such as rocks are, on this account, aggregates of occasions and are not themselves subjects, while organisms with a central nervous system contain organised societies of occasions with greater unity and richer experience (see [[process_philosophy]]).

## Motivations and criticisms
Proponents argue that the view avoids both dualism and eliminating experience, by making experience a basic feature of nature from which complex consciousness is built. They claim it explains why consciousness appears continuous with the rest of nature. Critics raise the combination problem, asking how micro-experiences could compose a unified subject, and doubt that the thesis makes testable predictions or adds explanatory value. Others reject it as incompatible with the physical science of inert matter. These objections are discussed in the panpsychism literature (Goff, Seager and Allen-Hermanson).

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no derivation linking the two. Active Inference is a framework for modelling inference and action in systems with Markov blankets, and it takes no stand on whether experience is fundamental. Some authors discuss whether the framework supports continuity between life and mind, which is a different claim from panexperientialism, and the connection is at most thematic.
