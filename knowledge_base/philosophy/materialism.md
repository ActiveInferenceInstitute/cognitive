# Materialism

## Overview
Materialism is the thesis that all that exists is matter, or that everything that exists depends entirely on the physical. Its ancient forms include the atomism of Democritus, Epicurus and Lucretius. Modern discussions usually use the term physicalism, which replaces "matter" with whatever entities and properties an ideal physics would posit, since contemporary physics includes fields and energy as well as particles.

## Forms in the philosophy of mind
- **Identity theory.** Place and Smart argued in the 1950s that mental states are identical to brain states, as lightning is identical to electrical discharge (Smart, 1959).
- **Eliminative materialism.** Everyday psychological concepts such as belief and desire belong to a theory that may be replaced by a mature neuroscience, so that these states are not real (Churchland, 1981).
- **Functionalism.** Mental states are physically realised but individuated by role, which allows many different physical realisers (see [[functionalism]]).
- **Emergentist and non-reductive physicalism.** Mental properties depend on physical ones but are not reducible to them.

## Challenges
Hempel's dilemma asks what "physical" means: if it refers to current physics, the thesis is probably false, and if it refers to a future ideal physics, it risks being empty. The major challenge in the philosophy of mind is accommodating conscious experience, pressed by arguments from [[qualia]], the [[explanatory_gap]] and the [[hard_problem]]. Materialists respond by denying that these arguments show an ontological gap, by offering reductive or illusionist accounts, or by adopting a non-reductive position.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference is generally developed within a naturalistic, physicalist framing: minds are treated as properties of self-organising physical systems that maintain themselves by modelling their environment, with explicit links to thermodynamics and dynamical systems. The mathematical formalism is, however, silent on the metaphysics of mind, and proponents differ in interpretation, from physicalist to dual-aspect (see [[neutral_monism]]). Claims that the framework explains consciousness are research proposals, not settled results.
