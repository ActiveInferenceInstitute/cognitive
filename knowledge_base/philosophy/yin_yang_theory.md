# Yin-Yang Theory

## Overview
Yinyang is one of the dominant concepts shared by different schools throughout the history of Chinese philosophy. Robin R. Wang's entry in the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy says that its influence is easy to observe but its meaning hard to define, and identifies three basic themes that underlie nearly all its uses: yinyang as the coherent fabric of nature and mind, as the interaction (*jiao*) between the waxing and waning of cosmic and human realms, and as a process of harmonisation that keeps all things in constant, dynamic balance. In none of these conceptions is there a ranking in which yin is abstracted from yang, or is held superior to it or metaphysically separate. This is the point at which it is usually contrasted with substance dualisms (compare [[dualism]]), a comparison that is the editor's and not the entry's.

## Development
- **Origins.** In the oracle-bone inscriptions, which date from at least the fourteenth century BCE, yin and yang describe natural conditions: sunlight in the daytime is yang, and its absence at night is yin. Later definitions link yang with the sunny side of a hill and yin with the shady side. At first the two characters were used independently.
- **Yinyang School.** Sima Tan lists a school of yinyang among six teachings of the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, concerned with divination, astronomy and the calendrical arts. By the Han dynasty yinyang was fused with *wuxing*, the "five phases", in a correlative cosmology.
- **Yinyang as qi.** The most enduring interpretation treats yin and yang as two forms of *qi*, vital energy. The *Zuozhuan* lists them among six heavenly *qi*, and the *Zhuangzi* says that when the *qi* of yin and yang are in harmony all things are produced. The *Huainanzi* describes how yang is generated from yin and yin from yang.
- **The Book of Changes.** In the *Yijing* the eight trigrams are formed from solid and broken lines that symbolise the ebb and flow of yin and yang, and a hexagram, made of an upper and lower trigram, represents the interaction of two such configurations (Hon, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).
- **The symbol.** The entry says there is no clear way to date or attribute the familiar yinyang symbol, and it places the earliest such symbol in the fourteenth century, after the diagrams of Song-dynasty thinkers.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is an analogy. The pairing of mutually dependent opposites that alternate, with balance as a dynamical condition, can be compared with homeostatic and allostatic regulation, where deviations in one direction elicit corrective responses in the other (see [[process_philosophy]]). Yinyang is a framework of correlative cosmology and ethics and not a mathematical model, and the comparison with control theory is a loose one.
