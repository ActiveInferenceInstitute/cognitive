# Moral Relativism

## Overview
Moral relativism is the view that there is no single, universally valid morality, and that moral judgments are true or justified only relative to some standpoint, usually a culture, society or moral framework. It is often confused with other claims, so it helps to separate three theses.

## Three theses
- **Descriptive relativism.** Different societies and individuals hold substantially different moral beliefs. This is an empirical claim, supported by anthropological and historical comparison, though the extent of deep disagreement is disputed.
- **Meta-ethical relativism.** The truth or justification of moral judgments depends on a framework. Harman (1975) defended the view that moral judgments are relative to agreements or conventions among groups, and Wong (2006) developed a pluralistic version that allows for some constraints from human needs.
- **Normative relativism.** One ought to tolerate the moral practices of other cultures. This is a first-order moral claim, and is strictly different from the meta-ethical thesis. Indeed, a universal duty of tolerance appears to be an absolute moral principle, which creates tension with the meta-ethical thesis.

Descriptive relativism does not entail the meta-ethical thesis, since people can disagree where only one party is right. Relativism is also distinct from nihilism, which denies that moral claims are ever true, and from error theory (see [[meta_ethics]]).

## Objections
Critics say that relativism cannot make sense of moral reformers who criticise their own societies, that it makes moral disagreement between cultures merely a difference of subject matter, and that it conflicts with the sense that some practices, such as slavery, are wrong wherever they occur. Relativists answer that these intuitions reflect the standpoint of the speaker's own framework. Moral realists reject relativism as a failure to appreciate objective moral facts (see [[moral_realism]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is descriptive only. Work on cultural niche construction and shared generative models treats norms as expectations learned and enforced within a community, which can explain why moral beliefs differ across groups and are transmitted (see [[social_norms]], [[cultural_evolution]]). Such an account supports descriptive relativism at most. It does not decide the meta-ethical question of whether any framework is correct.
