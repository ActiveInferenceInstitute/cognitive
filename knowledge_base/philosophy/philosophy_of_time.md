# Philosophy of Time

## Overview
The philosophy of time asks what time is, whether it passes, whether the past and future are real, why time seems to have a direction, and how temporal experience relates to the physical structure of time. It is a branch of metaphysics that interacts closely with physics and, through the study of temporal experience, with phenomenology and cognitive science.

## Tensed and tenseless theories
McTaggart (1908) distinguished two ways of ordering events. The A-series orders them as past, present or future, which changes as time passes. The B-series orders them as earlier than, later than or simultaneous with one another, which does not change. He argued that the A-series is essential to time but incoherent, and concluded that time is unreal. Most later writers reject the conclusion and divide on which series is basic:
- **A-theories** hold that tense is a real feature of the world, and include [[presentism]] and the growing-block view.
- **B-theories** hold that temporal relations are tenseless, so that "now" is an indexical like "here", and fit naturally with [[eternalism]].

## Further topics
- **Relativity.** The relativity of simultaneity in special relativity is frequently taken to favour a B-theoretic or four-dimensional picture, though defenders of A-theories resist this.
- **Direction.** The asymmetry between past and future is usually tied to entropy increase and the low-entropy early universe, although whether this fully accounts for the asymmetry of causation and experience is disputed (see [[thermodynamics]]).
- **Temporal experience.** James's specious present and Husserl's analysis of retention and protention describe experience as extended in time, not as a sequence of instants (see [[phenomenology]]).

Dainton (2010) and Callender (2011) provide comprehensive overviews.

## Relation to Active Inference
Generative models in Active Inference are explicitly temporal. They represent past, present and expected future states, and planning is inference over sequences of future states, which has been compared with the retention and protention of phenomenology. The framework also connects to thermodynamics, since the free energy principle is formulated for systems in non-equilibrium steady states, so its relation to the arrow of time is a topic of research. It adopts no position on tensed versus tenseless theories, and these links are analogies, not derivations.
