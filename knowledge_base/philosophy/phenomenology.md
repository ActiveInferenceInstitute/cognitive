# Phenomenology

## Overview
Phenomenology is the philosophical study of the structures of experience as it is lived, founded by Edmund Husserl and developed by Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty and Sartre. Its method is to describe how things appear to a subject, setting aside assumptions about what exists independently of that appearance.

## Core claims
**Intentionality.** Experience is always directed at something. Husserl treats this directedness as the basic structure to be analysed.

**Epoché and reduction.** The method suspends the "natural attitude" (the assumption that the world is just there) so that the contributions of consciousness can be described rather than explained away.

**Time consciousness.** Husserl analysed the present as having retention (the just-past held in the now), primal impression, and protention (anticipation of what is about to occur).

**Embodiment and world.** Heidegger's being-in-the-world and Merleau-Ponty's lived body make practical coping, not detached representation, primary. This is the line taken up by [[enactivism]] and [[4e_cognition]]; Varela's neurophenomenology proposed constraining first-person description with neural data.

## Role in Active Inference
The connection is real but mostly interpretive. Three points of contact are worth separating.

1. **Temporal depth.** The protentional structure of experience is often compared to the forward prediction in a generative model: beliefs about the next state are `B` applied to current beliefs. This is an analogy, not an established identity; retention and protention are descriptions of experience, not computational quantities.
2. **Affordances and grip.** Bruineberg and Rietveld interpret free-energy minimization as maintaining an optimal grip on a field of affordances, a Merleau-Ponty-inspired reading that moves away from inner representation.
3. **Computational phenomenology.** Sandved-Smith and colleagues model meta-awareness and attentional control with deeper parametric active inference, one concrete attempt to derive testable structure from phenomenological description.

There is a standing tension: the Bayesian-brain reading treats the brain as inferring hidden causes behind a sensory veil, which phenomenologists in the Husserl-Heidegger line reject. Whether active inference is neutral between these readings is disputed.
