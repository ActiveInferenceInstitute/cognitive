# Distributive Justice

## Overview
Distributive justice concerns how goods such as income, wealth, opportunity, education, health care and political power should be allocated among members of a society, and what makes an allocation fair. It is a central topic in political philosophy and economics. Theorists disagree about the currency to be distributed, the pattern that is fair and the role of individual responsibility, and this page presents the main views without ranking them.

## Main positions
- **Justice as fairness.** Rawls (1971) argued that principles of justice are those that would be chosen behind a "veil of ignorance". He proposed equal basic liberties, fair equality of opportunity and a difference principle permitting inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged.
- **Entitlement.** Nozick (1974) argued that a distribution is just if it arises from just acquisition and transfer, whatever its pattern, and that patterned principles require continual interference with liberty.
- **Utilitarian.** Utilitarian views assess distributions by their contribution to total or average welfare, which can favour equality under assumptions of diminishing marginal utility.
- **Egalitarian, prioritarian and sufficientarian.** Egalitarians hold that equality is of value in itself or is required by equal concern. Prioritarians (associated with Parfit) give extra weight to benefits for the worse off, and sufficientarians (Frankfurt, 1987) hold that what matters is that everyone has enough.
- **Luck egalitarianism.** Authors such as Dworkin and Cohen argue that inequalities arising from choice differ from those arising from unchosen circumstances.
- **Desert and pluralism.** Some theories base distribution on desert or contribution, and Walzer (1983) argued that different goods are governed by different distributive principles in different spheres.

Sen (2009) criticised ideal theories and urged comparative assessment of actual social arrangements, with attention to capabilities. Lamont and Favor survey the field in the Stanford Encyclopedia.

## Relation to Active Inference
The subject has no direct link to Active Inference. Experimental work with ultimatum and public goods games studies how people respond to unequal splits and is relevant to descriptive claims about fairness norms (see [[economic_games]]), but such evidence does not decide normative questions.
