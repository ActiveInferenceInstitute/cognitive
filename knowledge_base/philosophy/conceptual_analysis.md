# Conceptual Analysis

## Overview
Conceptual analysis is a method in philosophy in which a concept is clarified by stating what it is for something to fall under it, classically by giving necessary and sufficient conditions. Familiar examples are analyses of knowledge as justified true belief, of causation or of personal identity. The method is closely associated with [[analytic_philosophy]], and its status is itself a matter of dispute.

## Method and its difficulties
- **Procedure.** An analysis proposes a definition, tests it against intuitions about actual and hypothetical cases, and revises it when counterexamples appear. The history of the analysis of knowledge, from the justified-true-belief account through the counterexamples of Gettier (1963) to later proposals, illustrates this cycle.
- **Paradox of analysis.** If an analysis is correct, the analysing and analysed expressions seem to mean the same, so the analysis appears trivial. If they do not, the analysis appears incorrect. Responses distinguish sense from reference or appeal to different modes of presentation.
- **Quinean criticism.** Quine (1951) rejected the analytic-synthetic distinction on which strict conceptual truths depend, and argued that philosophy is continuous with empirical science (see [[naturalism]]).
- **Empirical criticism.** Psychological work on concepts suggests that many everyday categories are structured by typicality and similarity, not by necessary and sufficient conditions (see [[prototype_effects]]). Experimental philosophers have also asked whether intuitions about cases vary across populations (see [[experimental_philosophy]]).
- **Defence.** Jackson (1998) defended conceptual analysis as necessary for locating items described in one vocabulary within another, such as the moral or mental within the physical.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is methodological. Cognitive science studies how concepts are learned and represented (see [[concept_formation]]), and in Active Inference, categories can be described as latent states of a generative model inferred from experience. This offers a descriptive account of concepts that differs from the classical definitional picture, but it does not settle whether philosophical analysis of concepts is a legitimate or useful method.
