# Symbolic Logic

## Overview
Symbolic logic is the study of logical form and inference using an artificial notation in which arguments are expressed by symbols and manipulated by explicit rules. The notation removes ambiguities of natural language and allows the validity of arguments to be checked mechanically. The term is often used interchangeably with formal or mathematical logic ([[mathematical_logic]]), though it emphasises the symbolic language more than metatheory.

## History
Leibniz envisaged a universal characteristic in which reasoning would be reduced to calculation. Boole's work in the late 1840s and 1850s treated logical operations algebraically. Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) introduced a formal language with quantifiers and a precise notion of proof, which is the source of modern predicate logic. Peirce developed a logic of relations and independently introduced quantifiers, as well as a graphical system, the existential graphs, in which logical structure is shown by diagrams (Roberts, 1973). Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica (1910-1913) aimed to derive mathematics from logical principles in a symbolic system. Lewis and Langford's Symbolic Logic (1932) developed systems of strict implication, the origin of modern modal logic ([[modal_logic]]).

## Significance
Symbolic logic provides the foundation of mathematical logic and of the theory of computation, and it underlies programming languages, hardware design and automated theorem proving. In philosophy it supplied the tools of analytic philosophy for clarifying arguments and for treating questions of meaning and truth.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is by contrast and partial combination. Classical symbolic artificial intelligence represented knowledge in logical form and reasoned by rule-based deduction, whereas Active Inference works with continuous or discrete probability distributions and treats inference as updating beliefs under uncertainty. Neurosymbolic proposals attempt to combine the two, but the framework itself does not use symbolic logic as its core. Peirce's logic and his account of signs are noted as historical background because of his connection to pragmatism and to abduction ([[pragmatism]], [[peircean_semiotics]]).
