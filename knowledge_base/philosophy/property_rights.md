# Property Rights

## Overview
Property rights are rights concerning the use, control and transfer of things, whether land, goods, or intangible items such as inventions. Philosophers ask what ownership consists of, what justifies it, and what limits apply. Honore's influential analysis treats full ownership as a bundle of related incidents, among them rights to possess, use, manage, derive income from, transfer and exclude others, together with duties not to use property harmfully. Systems differ widely, from private through collective to common and state property.

## Justifications
- **Labour**: Locke, in the Second Treatise (1689), held that a person owns their body and labour, and so may acquire unowned things by mixing labour with them, subject to a proviso that enough and as good is left for others. Interpretations of the proviso are disputed.
- **Utility**: utilitarian arguments, associated with Hume and Bentham, defend property because stable ownership supports productive cooperation and predictable expectations.
- **Personality**: Hegel argued that property gives will an external existence, expressing personhood.
- **Entitlement**: Nozick (1974) held that a distribution is just if it arose from just acquisition and just transfer, and he criticised patterned theories of distribution for requiring continuous interference with liberty.
- **Critical views**: socialist and egalitarian writers argue that private ownership of productive resources produces unjust inequalities, and that some goods should be held in common or distributed by need. Waldron (1988) analyses general and special rights to property and the role of the political context in which they are justified.

## Commons and alternatives
Hardin (1968) argued that open-access resources tend to be overused. Ostrom (1990) documented communities that govern common-pool resources through locally designed rules and monitoring, showing that neither private property nor state control is the only workable arrangement. The question of how to treat intellectual property, data and genetic resources continues the debate.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no substantive link between the philosophy of property and Active Inference. The study of institutions as shared rules that coordinate expectations has been explored in cultural and cognitive models, but that work does not address the justification of ownership.
