# Mathematical Formalism

## Overview
Formalism in the philosophy of mathematics is the view that mathematics concerns the manipulation of formulae according to rules of syntax, so that its content is not a realm of abstract objects such as the one posited by [[mathematical_platonism]]. Alan Weir's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry separates two lines. The first, which it follows in most detail, is the "game" and "term" formalism attributed to mathematicians such as Heine and Thomae and made famous through Gottlob Frege's attack on it in the second volume of the *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* (1903). The second is Hilbertian formalism.

## Game and term formalism
Game formalism likens mathematics to a game played with signs. Frege's criticism treated the signs as concrete marks on a surface and was widely taken as a refutation. Weir notes that recent scholarship (Lawrence, 2023) shows Frege's reading of Hankel, Heine and Thomae to be "particularly wide of the mark", since Thomae often uses "sign" to mean a representation, for example an infinite decimal as a sign for a sequence of finite ones. Term formalism takes the subject matter of mathematics to be the expressions themselves. The entry follows later descendants of these ideas in Wittgenstein's *Tractatus*, among the logical positivists, in nominalist formalism, and in Curry.

## Hilbertian formalism
David Hilbert divided mathematical language into a finitary sector, whose sentences express contentful propositions, and an ideal or infinitary sector. Hilbert took an instrumentalist attitude to the ideal sector, whose formulae are treated as uninterpreted symbols to which formal rules apply but that are useful provided the ideal sector conservatively extends the finitary one. The goal of Hilbert's programme was a finitary proof of that conservative extension, which most (though not all) scholars think was shown to be impossible by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem (see [[mathematical_logic]]).

## Curry and contemporary formalism
Haskell Curry's *Outlines of a Formalist Philosophy of Mathematics* (1951) is the most substantial non-Hilbertian formalist philosophy. Curry is closer to term formalism, takes mathematics to have an essentially syntactic subject matter, namely formal systems, and aims to remain neutral about the ontological commitments of mathematics. The entry also discusses the Curry-Howard correspondence. Later developments have been mainly in the Hilbertian wing. After Cohen's independence results, some mathematicians such as Cohen himself and Abraham Robinson treated branches of mathematics whose key questions axioms cannot settle as ideal parts, lacking content. Few philosophers now defend game formalism, although the entry names Gabbay and Azzouni as recent formalists.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct relation. The mathematics used in Active Inference is ordinary analysis and probability theory, and the framework does not take a stand on the philosophy of mathematics. The question of whether mathematical models describe their targets or only provide useful instruments echoes the debate over formalism and [[instrumentalism]], and that echo is an analogy.
