# Social Epistemology

## Overview
Social epistemology is the branch of [[epistemology]] that treats knowledge as dependent on social conditions. Traditional epistemology focused on an individual subject weighing evidence. Social epistemologists point out that most of what any person knows comes from other people, books, institutions and instruments, and ask when such reliance is justified and how social arrangements can improve or damage the pursuit of truth.

## Main topics
- **Testimony.** Under what conditions does being told something give knowledge or justified belief? Reductionists require independent checks on a speaker's reliability, while anti-reductionists treat testimony as a basic source with defeasible default trust.
- **Epistemic dependence.** Hardwig argued that in modern science and expertise, individuals rationally rely on collaborators they cannot check, so that knowledge may be held by a group rather than by any single member.
- **Disagreement.** How should one revise belief on learning that an equally informed peer disagrees? Positions range from conciliation to remaining steadfast.
- **Group and collective belief.** Questions arise about whether groups such as committees or research teams can be said to know or believe, and how individual judgements aggregate.
- **Institutions and information environments.** Peer review, markets for ideas, media systems and network structure can foster or degrade accuracy, including through polarisation and echo chambers.
- **Epistemic injustice.** Fricker identified testimonial injustice, where a speaker's credibility is unfairly discounted because of prejudice, and hermeneutical injustice, where a group lacks shared concepts to make its experience intelligible.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is an active research programme on collective and cultural applications of Active Inference, in which agents inferring one another's beliefs, sharing generative models and building niches are used to model communication, shared narratives and norms. These links concern how beliefs spread and stabilise in populations, and so they connect to the vault's interest in information ecosystems and cognitive security. They are modelling proposals rather than established results, and the philosophical positions above are not derived from the framework.
