# Wuwei

## Overview
*Wuwei* (literally "no action" or "non-action") is a central concept of Daoist thought, in the *Laozi* (also called the *Daodejing*) and in the *Zhuangzi*. Alan Chan's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on the *Laozi* treats it as the practical counterpart of *ziran* (naturalness, see [[ziran_naturalness]]): wuwei "serves to explain naturalness in practice". Because "non-action" is awkward in English, translators have proposed "non-assertive action", "non-coercive action" and "effortless action", and Chan prefers "nonaction" or, better still, the transliterated term, explained from the text.

## What it means
- **Not total inaction.** The *Laozi* uses wuwei broadly as a contrast with any form of action driven by self-serving desire. At the personal level it entails simplicity and quietude, which follow naturally from having few desires. Later Daoists connect it with techniques of spiritual cultivation, such as the "sitting in forgetfulness" and "fasting of the mind" described in the *Zhuangzi*.
- **Skilled examples.** According to the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry by Littlejohn, the *Zhuangzi* uses analogies of craftsmen, athletes (swimmers), ferrymen and other skilled practitioners to illustrate conduct that "has the same feel as acting in wu-wei", though the entry adds that wu-wei is not a cultivated skill.
- **Political application.** The text condemns aggressive measures, among them war, cruel punishment and heavy taxation, as expressions of the ruler's own desire for wealth and power. It declares that if the ruler rids himself of desire, the world will be at peace of its own accord, and it describes the ideal sage-ruler as one who understands and follows *ziran*.
- **Critique of conventional virtue.** The Daoist view opposes the Confucian programme of benevolent intervention (see [[confucian_philosophy]]). Conscious efforts to cultivate moral virtue are said to accentuate the loss of natural goodness, and artificial effort to "improve" people perpetuates a false sense of self. Paradoxes such as "the highest virtue is not virtuous" (chapter 38) are used to push readers out of conventional assumptions.
- **Discernment.** The concept implies that value distinctions are ideological, and that competitive striving springs from the same source. It also carries a critique of language and conventional knowledge.

## Wuwei as a spiritual ideal
Edward Slingerland's *Effortless Action* (2003) reads wuwei across early Chinese thought, Confucian as well as Daoist, as a spiritual ideal of effortless, spontaneous skill, using conceptual-metaphor theory to analyse the language in which it is expressed. His account treats the "paradox of wuwei" (how one can try to become spontaneous) as a problem that different schools addressed in different ways.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is an analogy. The ideal of skilled action that is fluent and free of deliberate effort resembles accounts in which expert behaviour is carried by well-tuned predictions with little need for explicit deliberation (see [[action_theory]]). The comparison is loose. Wuwei is an ethical and soteriological ideal concerned with desire and social order, not a description of motor control.
