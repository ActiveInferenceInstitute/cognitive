# Jain Philosophy

## Overview
Jainism is one of the religious traditions that originated on the Indian subcontinent. By its own account its teachings are eternal and have no founder, but the Jainism of this age is traced back to Mahavira, a teacher of the sixth century BCE and a contemporary of the Buddha. Like the Buddha's teaching, it arose as a reaction to the Brahmanism then taking shape, and it rejects the authority of the Vedas (see [[hindu_philosophy]] and [[buddhist_philosophy]]). The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry by Marie-Helene Gorisse and the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry by Mark Owen Webb present three connected strands.

## Metaphysics and karma
- **Substances.** Reality consists of souls (*jiva*), matter (*pudgala*), the principle of motion (*dharma*), the principle of rest (*adharma*), space (*akasa*) and time (*kala*). The universe is eternal, and neither matter nor souls were created. Jainism is dualist, since souls and matter are entirely different substances, and it is often called atheistic in that it denies a creator god above all, though the universe contains gods who are as subject to karma as humans.
- **Karma as matter.** Souls accumulate karma through their actions, and karma is understood as a kind of matter that draws the soul back into a body after death. Each new life is determined by the kind and amount of karma accumulated. Because karma is material, all karma, good and bad, leads to rebirth, and no karma can help a person achieve liberation.
- **Every soul is alive.** Every living thing has a soul and can be harmed or helped, and living things are classified by the number of their senses.

## Ethics
The goal is release from the cycle of death and rebirth, so Jain ethics aims to purge accumulated karma and to cease accumulating new karma. The moral ideal is ascetic. Monks follow five vows (non-violence or *ahimsa*, truthfulness, not taking what is not given, chastity and non-attachment). Non-violence is the cardinal rule of interaction with other souls (compare [[animal_ethics]]).

## Epistemology: many-sidedness
Jain epistemology rests on the idea that reality is multifaceted (*anekanta*), so that no single statement captures it in its entirety. This grounds a kind of fallibilism (see [[fallibilism]]) and the doctrine of *naya*, or partial predication, according to which any judgment is true only from the viewpoint of the judge. It also generates a sevenfold classification of predications, each marked by *syat* ("perhaps" or "from a perspective"). Critics, Sankara among them, have objected that the combined forms appear inconsistent, and the Internet Encyclopedia entry notes that the doctrine also faces the problem of seeming self-defeating.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is interpretive. The idea that every cognitive perspective is partial and provisional can be compared with the view that an agent's beliefs are approximate models of a world it can only sample (see [[fallibilism]]). The Jain doctrine is an epistemological and soteriological claim, and the framework does not entail it.
