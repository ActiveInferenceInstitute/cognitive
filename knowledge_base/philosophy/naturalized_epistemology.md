# Naturalized Epistemology

## Overview
Naturalized epistemology holds that questions about knowledge should be investigated with the empirical sciences, in particular psychology and cognitive science, instead of by a priori analysis. The programme was proposed by Quine (1969), who argued that the traditional foundationalist project of deriving knowledge of the world from sensory data had failed and should be replaced by a scientific study of how sensory input yields a theory of the world.

## Variants
- **Replacement naturalism.** Quine's proposal, on which epistemology becomes a chapter of psychology and loses its normative role.
- **Reliabilism.** Goldman (1986) retained normativity: a belief is justified when produced by a reliable cognitive process, and psychology can tell us which processes are reliable.
- **Moderate naturalism.** Empirical findings constrain and inform philosophical theories without replacing them, and conceptual analysis keeps a role alongside psychology.

## Objections
Kim (1988) argued that dropping the concept of justification makes epistemology no longer about knowledge, since normative notions such as evidence and justification are essential to it. Critics also charge circularity, because science is used to vindicate the methods on which science depends. Naturalists reply that no non-circular foundation is available to any approach, and that epistemic norms may be grounded in the success of cognitive strategies.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is thematic and fairly close. Treating perception and learning as approximate Bayesian inference supplies a candidate scientific account of how organisms form reliable beliefs, which is the kind of input naturalists seek. This is a research programme in which the framework is used as an empirical theory of cognition. It does not by itself settle the normative questions that remain disputed, such as what justifies a belief.
