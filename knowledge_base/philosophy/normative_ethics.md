# Normative Ethics

## Overview
Normative ethics is the branch of moral philosophy that proposes and defends general standards of right action and good character. It sits between [[meta_ethics]], which studies the status and meaning of moral claims, and [[applied_ethics]], which addresses concrete problems. A normative theory aims to say what makes right acts right, so that it can guide choice and explain particular moral judgments.

## Major families
- **Consequentialism.** The rightness of an action depends on its outcomes; the best-known version is utilitarianism, which ranks outcomes by overall well-being (see [[consequentialism]]).
- **Deontology.** Rightness depends on conformity with duties, rules or rights, some of which constrain action even when breaking them would produce better outcomes (see [[deontological_ethics]]).
- **Virtue ethics.** The central notion is good character, and right action is what a virtuous person would do (see [[virtue_ethics]]).
- **Other approaches.** These include contractualism, which grounds morality in principles that could be justified to all, and the ethics of care, which emphasises relationships and responsiveness (see [[care_ethics]]).

## Method
Theories are tested against considered judgments about cases. Rawls (1971) described reflective equilibrium, a process of adjusting both principles and particular judgments until they cohere. Thought experiments play a large role; the trolley problem, introduced by Foot and developed by Thomson (1985), contrasts cases in which harm is caused by redirecting a threat or by directly using a person, and has been used to test whether the difference between doing and allowing is morally significant. Pluralist and hybrid positions combine considerations drawn from several families.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is formal and limited. Expected utility maximisation in decision theory is a model for consequentialist reasoning, and the expected free energy of a policy also scores policies by anticipated outcomes. But expected free energy mixes preference satisfaction with information gain and is a model of how agents choose, not a theory of how they ought to. Whether any form of Active Inference supports a particular normative theory is not established.
