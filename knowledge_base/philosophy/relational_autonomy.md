# Relational Autonomy

## Overview
"Relational autonomy" is the name given to feminist reconceptualisations of the notion of autonomy. Natalie Stoljar's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry "Feminist Perspectives on Autonomy" explains the background: many feminists criticised the traditional picture of the autonomous agent as self-sufficient, the "self-made man", and questioned whether self-sufficiency ("substantive independence") is a value that normative theories should promote. If autonomy is conceived as hostile to being a woman, because being a woman involves valuing social relationships while being autonomous requires repudiating them, then women are denied the social and political advantages attached to the label. Feminist philosophers therefore attempted to rehabilitate autonomy, and the volume edited by Catriona Mackenzie and Natalie Stoljar (2000) is a central collection.

## Two senses of "relational"
The entry distinguishes two senses. In the weaker sense, "relational" denies that autonomy requires self-sufficiency, and holds that autonomy is compatible with standing in and valuing significant family and social relationships, since relationships of care and interdependence are valuable. In the stronger sense, which the entry uses, it denies the metaphysical notion of atomistic personhood and emphasises that persons are socially and historically embedded, not isolated, and are shaped by factors such as race and class.

## Hard cases and classification
The literature is motivated by "hard cases" in which it is unclear whether autonomy is diminished: self-abnegation, adaptive preference formation, and practices of oppression. A "thin" or minimalist account counts agents as autonomous unless they suffer a serious affliction, cognitive impairment or outright coercion. The entry observes that this is not the norm in the feminist literature, because it overlooks the effects of gender norms and oppressive conditions on agency. Relational theories are classified as procedural, weakly substantive or strongly substantive, and as causal or constitutive. Procedural theories hold that autonomy is achieved when an agent reflects on her motivations and values and revises them, a process that is content-neutral. Substantive theories hold that autonomy is value-laden, and strong versions place normative constraints on the content of preferences. The entry then reviews procedural, normative-competency, emotion-based, dialogical and strongly substantive or social-relational conceptions.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is interpretive. A view on which an agent's preferences and self-understanding arise through interaction with social surroundings parallels the Active Inference treatment of priors as shaped by an agent's niche and history. The framework does not itself say which preferences are autonomous, which is a normative question.
