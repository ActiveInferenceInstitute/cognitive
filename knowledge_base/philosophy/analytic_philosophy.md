# Analytic Philosophy

## Overview
Analytic philosophy is a broad tradition, dominant in the English-speaking world since the twentieth century, characterised less by a doctrine than by a style: careful argument, attention to the logic and meaning of language, and a preference for precisely stated problems. Its origins are usually traced to the work of Frege on logic and meaning and to the early twentieth-century rebellion of Russell and Moore against idealism.

## Development
- **Logical analysis.** Frege's formal logic and Russell's theory of descriptions treated the analysis of sentences as a way to dissolve philosophical puzzles. Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* (1921) developed a picture theory of meaning.
- **Logical positivism.** The Vienna Circle linked meaning to verifiability and sought to unify science (see [[logical_positivism]]).
- **Ordinary language philosophy.** Later Wittgenstein, Ryle, Austin and others held that philosophical problems arise from misuse of everyday language and should be examined by attending to its use.
- **Post-positivist developments.** Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction (1951), Kripke's work on necessity and reference, and the revival of metaphysics and normative theory broadened the tradition. Quine's naturalism tied philosophy closely to empirical science, while pragmatists such as Sellars and Brandom linked language to normativity and justification, a connection noted on the [[pragmatism]] page.

The boundary with other traditions is contested. Some historians regard the analytic and "continental" divide as a sociological, not a methodological, distinction, and several contemporary figures work across it. Soames (2003) and Beaney (2013) give comprehensive histories.

## Relation to Active Inference
Analytic philosophy supplies much of the vocabulary used in the philosophical discussion of Active Inference, including mental representation, content, functionalism and mental causation, drawn from philosophy of mind and philosophy of science. The relation is conceptual and methodological, not a derivation. Authors interpret the free energy principle using analytic tools, for example when asking whether generative models represent or whether Markov blankets mark a real boundary.
