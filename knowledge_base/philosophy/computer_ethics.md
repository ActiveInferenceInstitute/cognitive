# Computer Ethics

## Overview
Computer ethics examines moral questions raised by the design, use and social consequences of computers and information technology. It emerged in the mid-twentieth century with Norbert Wiener's work on cybernetics and society and took shape as an academic field in the 1970s and 1980s. Issues include privacy and surveillance, intellectual property, security, access and the digital divide, professional responsibility, software liability and, more recently, the behaviour of automated systems.

## Foundational accounts
- **Wiener.** In *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950), Wiener discussed the social and ethical effects of automation and communication technologies.
- **Moor.** In "What is computer ethics?" (1985), Moor defined the field as the analysis of the nature and social impact of computer technology and the formulation of policies for its use. He argued that computers generate "policy vacuums", situations where no rule exists for a new capability, and "conceptual muddles", where existing concepts do not clearly apply.
- **Johnson.** Deborah Johnson's textbook (first edition 1985) helped establish the field in teaching and asked whether computing creates new ethical issues or new versions of old ones, a disputed point.
- **Information ethics.** Floridi (2013) proposed a framework in which information objects have a minimal moral standing and ethical analysis can be applied to the information environment, a proposal that has attracted both followers and critics.

Debates continue over the responsibility of designers and operators, the weighing of privacy against security and the question of whether machines can be moral agents.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is indirect. Computer ethics treats the social and moral side of technologies, while Active Inference is a scientific framework that can also be used to build artificial agents. Where such agents are deployed, the questions of accountability, transparency and safety are those of [[ai_ethics]] and are not addressed by the formalism itself. Claims about what artificial agents with generative models may experience belong to [[philosophy_of_ai]].
