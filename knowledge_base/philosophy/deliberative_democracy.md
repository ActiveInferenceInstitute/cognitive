# Deliberative Democracy

## Overview
Deliberative democracy is a family of theories in political philosophy according to which collective decisions are legitimate when they emerge from public deliberation among citizens who offer one another reasons they can accept. It contrasts with aggregative models that treat democracy mainly as a way of counting pre-existing preferences through voting and bargaining. The theory draws on Kantian and Habermasian ideas, and the American pragmatist tradition, especially Dewey, is frequently cited as a precursor (see [[pragmatism]]).

## Main positions
- **Habermas**: in Between Facts and Norms (1996) legitimate law is that which could be accepted by all affected in rational discourse, governed by procedures of communicative action and an informal public sphere.
- **Cohen** (1989) characterised an ideal deliberative procedure by free, equal and reasoned participation aimed at consensus, with the aim that outcomes be justified to all.
- **Gutmann and Thompson** (1996) stressed reciprocity: citizens should offer reasons that others who are similarly motivated can accept, even when moral disagreement persists. Their account gives particular attention to deliberation within constitutional democracies.
- **Dryzek** (2000) extended the idea to civil society and discursive designs beyond formal institutions.

Empirical applications include citizens' assemblies, deliberative polls and mini-publics, which bring randomly selected participants together with information and facilitation.

## Criticisms
Critics argue that deliberation can exclude those less comfortable with formal argument, and Young (2000) proposed a more inclusive range of communication, such as greeting, rhetoric and narrative. Others question whether deliberation produces consensus, whether it scales to large societies, and whether it improves decisions or merely changes how they are made. Proponents answer that the aim is legitimacy and mutual understanding, not unanimity.

## Relation to cognitive science
There is no direct connection to Active Inference. A loose thematic relation exists with work on collective belief formation and [[social_epistemology]], which examines how groups pool and test information, but this is shared subject matter, not a derivation.
