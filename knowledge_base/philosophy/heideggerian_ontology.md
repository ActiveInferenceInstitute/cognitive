# Heideggerian Ontology

## Overview
Heideggerian ontology is the account of Being developed by Martin Heidegger, above all in Being and Time (Sein und Zeit, 1927). Heidegger holds that Western philosophy has forgotten the question of the meaning of Being, and he proposes to raise it by analysing the entity for whom Being is an issue, which he calls [[dasein]] (roughly, human existence). The method is a [[hermeneutic_phenomenology]] that interprets how things show up in everyday life, instead of beginning with a detached subject facing objects.

## Central ideas
- **Ontological difference**: the distinction between beings (entities) and Being, the way entities are intelligible as what they are.
- **Being-in-the-world**: Dasein is always already engaged in a meaningful world, so the subject-object split is derivative (see [[being_in_the_world]]).
- **Readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand**: tools are first encountered in skilful use (ready-to-hand), and become objects of theoretical observation (present-at-hand) mainly when use breaks down.
- **Care and temporality**: Dasein's basic structure is care, and its Being is interpreted through temporality, including finitude, which Heidegger develops in the analysis of [[being_toward_death]].
- **Authenticity**: the contrast between an everyday absorption in "the they" and an owned, individualised existence.

Wrathall's entry in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives an overview. Heidegger's later work shifts towards the history of Being, language and technology, and interpreters differ over the continuity between early and late thought. His involvement with National Socialism remains a significant subject of scholarly dispute about the relation of his life and work.

## Reception in cognitive science
Dreyfus (1991) read Division I of Being and Time as a phenomenology of skilful coping and used it to criticise symbolic artificial intelligence. Embodied, enactive and situated approaches draw on similar ideas.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is interpretive. Some authors compare being-in-the-world and the primacy of engaged coping with the view that agents are embedded in an environment through action-perception loops, and read active inference non-representationally. Others point out that Heidegger's anti-representationalism sits uneasily with generative-model talk. It is a source of analogy and debate, not a derivation.
