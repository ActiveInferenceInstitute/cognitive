# Simulation Theory

## Overview
In this context, simulation theory refers to the proposal that the observable world, including its inhabitants, may be a computer simulation run by a more advanced civilisation. The idea has precursors in Cartesian scepticism about the external world, but its present form derives from the simulation argument of Nick Bostrom (2003). A different use of the term, in philosophy of mind, names the theory that people understand others by mentally simulating their states; that sense is covered under [[theory_of_mind]].

## The simulation argument
Bostrom's argument concludes that at least one of three propositions is true: (1) almost all civilisations at our stage go extinct before developing the capacity to run detailed ancestor simulations; (2) almost none of the civilisations that reach that capacity run such simulations in significant numbers; (3) we are almost certainly living in a simulation. The argument assumes substrate independence, the claim that conscious experience can arise from suitable computation on non-biological hardware, and an indifference principle, according to which one should distribute credence equally across observers with subjectively identical experiences.

## Objections and developments
Critics question both assumptions. Some deny that computation suffices for consciousness (see [[artificial_consciousness]]). Weatherson (2003) objected to the way the indifference principle is applied. Others note that the argument is probabilistic and does not bear on whether simulation can be tested. Chalmers (2022) argues in Reality+ that if we were in a simulation, our ordinary beliefs about tables and persons would remain largely true, because virtual objects are real in their own right, which separates the hypothesis from sceptical scenarios that threaten knowledge. Critics reply that the hypothesis is unfalsifiable and speculative, and physicists treat attempts to find empirical signatures with caution.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is a loose analogy. Active Inference describes the brain as maintaining a generative model that predicts sensory input, and philosophers sometimes describe perception as constrained hallucination or an internal simulation of the world. This concerns the architecture of inference within an organism and is distinct from the claim that the world itself is simulated. The framework makes no commitment on the simulation hypothesis.
