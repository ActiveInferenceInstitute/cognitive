# Radical Empiricism

## Overview
Radical empiricism is the philosophical position developed by William James in essays published from 1904 and collected posthumously as Essays in Radical Empiricism (1912). James held that philosophy should admit only what can be experienced, but that anything directly experienced must be admitted, including the relations between things. In his view, classical empiricism in the line of Hume treated experience as a collection of disconnected items and then supplied connections from reason, while rationalists posited connecting principles beyond experience. Radical empiricism holds that conjunctive relations, such as "next to", "with", "and" and "from", are as much given in experience as the terms they connect. The doctrine is closely tied to [[pragmatism]], though James treated the two as separable.

## Pure experience
James proposed that the world is made of a single kind of stuff, pure experience, which is neither mental nor physical in itself. The same portion of pure experience can function as a thought in one context of associations and as a physical object in another. In "Does 'consciousness' exist?" (1904) he denied that consciousness is an entity, and proposed that it names a function, that of knowing. The resulting view is often called neutral monism, a position later developed in different forms by Mach and Russell. It dissolves the sharp subject-object divide without reducing either side to the other, though critics ask whether the notion of pure experience is coherent or does explanatory work.

Interpreters such as Lamberth (1999) emphasise that James's metaphysics aims at a pluralistic universe in which relations are real and the world is still in the making, in contrast to monistic idealism.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is thematic. James's stream of experience, his emphasis on the continuity of experience, and his rejection of a passive spectator picture resonate with accounts in which perception is shaped by action. Some predictive processing authors cite James on attention and expectation. These are historical and conceptual affinities, and Active Inference does not derive from or depend on radical empiricism.
