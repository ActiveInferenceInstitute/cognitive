# Consciousness Unity

## Overview
The unity of consciousness is the apparent fact that experiences do not occur in isolation. At a given moment, the sight of a red cup, the feel of its warmth and a passing thought are experienced together, as parts of one overall experience, and experiences across time are likewise felt as belonging to a single stream. Kant gave the theme a central place in the transcendental unity of apperception, and it remains a constraint that theories of consciousness are asked to explain.

## Varieties and problems
- **Synchronic and diachronic unity.** The first concerns experiences at one time, the second the continuity of experience over time. Bayne (2010) distinguishes several further senses, including the unity of the subject and the unity of phenomenal content.
- **The binding problem.** Features such as colour, motion and shape are processed in partly separate brain regions, yet are experienced as properties of single objects (see [[temporal_binding]]). Proposed solutions include neural synchrony and attention-based integration, and none is settled.
- **Split-brain cases.** After severing the corpus callosum, patients show behaviour suggesting two partly independent streams of processing, which raises the question of whether consciousness can divide. Interpretations differ, and some deny that unity is ever lost.
- **Theories.** The [[global_workspace]] account explains unity through broadcast of content to a common workspace, and [[integrated_information]] theory makes integration the defining feature of consciousness. Bayne and Chalmers (2003) analyse unity in terms of subsumption, whereby experiences are subsumed under a single total experience.

## Relation to Active Inference
Some authors propose that unity reflects the integration of a single hierarchical generative model that explains multisensory input under one set of causes, so that unity follows from inference about a shared world and a unified self-model. This is an active line of theoretical work, with fit to phenomenology argued by analogy rather than derived from the free energy principle, and it has not been tested against split-brain or other disunity data.
