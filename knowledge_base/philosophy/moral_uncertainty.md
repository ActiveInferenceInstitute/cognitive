# Moral Uncertainty

## Overview
Moral uncertainty is uncertainty about normative questions, as distinct from empirical uncertainty about facts. An agent may be unsure whether a consequentialist or a deontological theory is correct, or how much weight an animal's interests deserve, and yet must still choose. The philosophical question is what it is rational or appropriate to do when one's credence is spread across rival moral views. Recent work in this area has grown out of discussions by Lockhart (2000) and by MacAskill, Bykvist and Ord (2020).

## Proposed decision rules
- **My Favourite Theory.** Act on the moral theory in which one has highest credence. It is simple but ignores how much is at stake under the other theories.
- **My Favourite Option.** Choose the action most likely to be right, summing credence across theories.
- **Maximise expected choiceworthiness.** Weight each option's choiceworthiness under each theory by the credence in that theory and choose the highest total, by analogy with expected utility.
- **Parliamentary models.** Treat theories as delegates who bargain in proportion to credence.

## Open problems
Expected-choiceworthiness approaches require that values be comparable across theories, which raises the problem of intertheoretic comparison, since one theory may not supply cardinal values on the same scale as another. Other problems include regress about higher-order uncertainty and whether the norm for acting under moral uncertainty is itself a moral norm. Views differ on each point, and some philosophers deny that moral uncertainty should affect what is permissible.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is formal analogy only. Handling uncertainty over theories by averaging in proportion to credence resembles Bayesian model averaging, and the free energy principle concerns uncertainty about states of the world, not normative content. The shared structure lies in the decision-theoretic apparatus; Active Inference makes no claim about which moral theory is correct.
