# Hindu Philosophy

## Overview
Hindu philosophy refers to the systematic thought developed in the Indian traditions that accept the authority of the Vedas, in contrast with heterodox traditions such as Buddhism and Jainism. It draws on the Upanishads, epic and ritual texts, and a long commentarial literature. The tradition is conventionally organised into six orthodox schools (darshanas), though the boundaries between them and with other Indian systems were fluid.

## Schools and concepts
- **Nyaya and Vaisheshika.** Nyaya developed logic and theories of valid knowledge (pramana), including perception and inference; Vaisheshika proposed an atomistic ontology of categories.
- **Samkhya and Yoga.** Samkhya describes reality as two principles, conscious purusha and material prakriti, a form of [[dualism]]. Yoga adds a discipline of concentration and ethical practice aimed at liberation (see [[meditation]]).
- **Mimamsa.** The school concerned with ritual interpretation, and with the authority of the Vedic texts.
- **Vedanta.** Interpreting the Upanishads, it divides into Advaita (Shankara), which holds that the self (atman) is identical to ultimate reality (Brahman), Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja), and Dvaita (Madhva), which affirm real difference between the self and God to differing degrees.
- **Shared themes.** Many schools discuss karma, rebirth, dharma and moksha (liberation), and the relation of consciousness to the world (see [[consciousness]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct link. Some authors compare the Advaita analysis of the witness consciousness or the Samkhya distinction of subject and object with discussions of self-models and the observer in [[philosophy_of_mind]], and contemplative-science work on [[meditation]] draws on Indian practices. These parallels are interpretive, are not part of the formalism, and do not imply that the traditions share a theory of mind. The tradition also debated [[buddhist_philosophy]] on questions of self and knowledge.
