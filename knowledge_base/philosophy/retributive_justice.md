# Retributive Justice

## Overview
Retributive justice is the view that punishment is justified because the offender deserves it, and not mainly because it produces good consequences. Its two core claims are that wrongdoing warrants a response in proportion to the offender's culpability, and that the response is directed at the guilty only. The theory is a leading account in the philosophy of criminal law, and it is contrasted with deterrence, incapacitation and rehabilitation as aims of punishment.

## Versions
- **Kantian and Hegelian roots.** Kant held that punishment must be imposed because the offender has committed a crime, not as a means to another good, and that equality of crime and penalty is the standard. Hegel saw punishment as annulling the wrong and honouring the offender as a rational agent.
- **Desert theories.** Modern versions, such as von Hirsch (1976), ground punishment in desert and stress proportionality of severity to the seriousness of the offence, while Moore (1997) argues that desert is a moral fact to which criminal law should respond, with emotions such as guilt and indignation as evidence of it.
- **Limiting retributivism.** Hart (1968) distinguished the general justifying aim of punishment from its distribution, and allowed that retribution may limit who may be punished and how much even if consequences justify the practice.

## Contrasts and challenges
Consequentialists justify punishment by its effects on crime (see [[consequentialism]]), and restorative approaches emphasise repair of harm and reconciliation. Critics of retributivism question whether desert can be given a non-mysterious basis. A further challenge is raised by research on responsibility and the brain: Greene and Cohen (2004) argued that advances in neuroscience may erode intuitions behind retributive punishment, while others reply that desert depends on rational agency, which neuroscience does not undermine (see [[free_will]]). The disagreement remains open.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct connection. Only a tenuous link exists through the empirical study of moral emotions, such as anger and the desire to punish, which can be modelled computationally (see [[moral_emotions]]). Such modelling describes how people behave and does not decide what justice requires.
