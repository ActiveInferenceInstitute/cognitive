# Boundary Objects

## Overview
The concept of a boundary object was introduced by Susan Leigh Star and James R. Griesemer in a 1989 study of the Museum of Vertebrate Zoology at the University of California, Berkeley. The problem they addressed is that scientific work is heterogeneous, requiring many actors with different viewpoints, yet it also requires cooperation and generalisable findings. Boundary objects are one way in which such groups manage the tension: according to the authors' abstract they are both adaptable to different viewpoints and robust enough to maintain identity across them.

## The idea
A boundary object is an artefact, such as a specimen collection, a map or a form, that is used by several communities who may interpret it differently. It has a common enough identity to be recognised by all, while remaining plastic enough to be shaped to the local needs of each. This allows collaboration without requiring a consensus, and it is part of a wider process of translation between viewpoints that also relies on the standardisation of methods. Star and Griesemer extended the Latour-Callon model of interessement, the process by which actors are enrolled in a shared project.

## Four types
In the Berkeley case the authors distinguished four types of boundary object:
- **Repositories**: ordered collections, such as a museum's specimens, that are available to different users for different purposes.
- **Ideal types**: abstractions, such as a diagram or atlas, that do not describe any single locality in detail but are adaptable enough to be used by many.
- **Coincident boundaries**: common objects with the same boundaries but different internal contents, so that groups with different aims can work on one and the same territory.
- **Standardized forms**: methods or forms that make information comparable across dispersed sites.

## Later clarification
Star later reflected on how the concept had been used. In her 2010 article she described three components in the original paper: interpretive flexibility, the structure of informatic and work-process needs and arrangements, and the dynamic between ill-structured and more tailored uses of the objects. She argued that much subsequent use had concentrated on interpretive flexibility alone and had conflated it with the movement back and forth between ill-structured and well-structured uses. The concept has since been applied well beyond science studies, for example in studies of organisational knowledge and product development (Carlile, 2002).

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an interpretive analogy proposed on this page, not a finding from the cited sources. One could read a boundary object as a shared artefact that each community fits into its own model of a common situation while keeping a common reference, but boundary objects were developed in the sociology of science and carry no commitment to the free-energy formalism, and no formal result links them to Active Inference.
