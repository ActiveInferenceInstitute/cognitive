# Philosophy of Liberation

## Overview
The philosophy of liberation is a movement in Latin American philosophy that, in the account given by Eduardo Mendieta in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, belongs to the "maturity" stage of the contemporary period of that history. It sought explicitly to recover distinctly Latin American thought, from Amerindian thought before and after the Conquest to the philosophies of colonialism, independence and nation-building, and so one of its goals is a critical historiography of philosophy. Figures such as Enrique Dussel, Rodolfo Kusch, Arturo Roig and Leopoldo Zea articulated versions of it. The movement is traced to the late 1960s, when Zea and Augusto Salazar Bondy launched a debate with the question "Is there a Latin American philosophy?"

## Antecedents
Following Dussel, Mendieta sketches three earlier periods that formed the "geological subsoil" of the movement. The first begins with the critique of the Conquest, for example Anton de Montesinos's 1511 critique of the way evangelisation was being carried out, and includes the debate at Valladolid in 1550, where Sepulveda argued that Amerindians were natural slaves and Bartolome de las Casas opposed him. The entry treats this debate as the clear emergence of a liberation discourse.

## Currents
Like existentialism, hermeneutics and phenomenology, the philosophy of liberation was never homogeneous. Cerutti Guldberg's typology names four currents.
- **Ontologicist.** Associated with Casalla, Cullen, Kusch and Podetti.
- **Analectical.** Associated with Dussel and Scannone. It criticises Eurocentrism and North American neo-colonialism, and treats modernity as a colonial ideology that has concealed what is distinctly Latin American. It is a metaphysical critique of thinking that proceeds from the totality, from Plato and Aristotle through Hegel and Marx, and holds that the authentic "people" is always outside the totality. The adjective derives from "ana", meaning beyond.
- **Historicist** and **problematising** currents are also distinguished.

## Themes
The entry names recurring themes: the question of the "people" (*pueblo*) as the historical subject of liberation, and how it is to be defined; the relation between the individual subject and the collective subject; and further topics such as the critique of modernity and of Eurocentrism. In English, Dussel's *Philosophy of Liberation* (1985, translated by Aquila Martinez and Christine Morkovsky) is a principal source.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct relation. A parallel exists in the shared emphasis that knowledge and agency depend on one's situation (see [[hermeneutic_phenomenology]]), and the philosophy of liberation adds a political claim about whose situation counts. The framework contains no claims about oppression or liberation.
