# Responsibility Gaps

## Overview
A responsibility gap is a situation in which an outcome, usually harmful, is produced by an artificial or otherwise autonomous system and no human agent can be properly held morally or legally responsible for it. Matthias (2004) introduced the problem for learning automata. Because such systems modify their behaviour through experience, their designers, operators and owners may be unable to predict or control what they do, and it then seems unfair to blame them. If the machine cannot be blamed either, accountability appears to have no place to land.

## The argument
Responsibility is typically ascribed when an agent has control over an act and knowledge of its likely consequences. Matthias argued that for sufficiently autonomous learning machines, neither condition holds for any human. Sparrow (2007) applied the point to autonomous weapons, holding that it is unfair to hold a commander responsible for the unforeseen acts of a weapon, and that machines cannot be punished in a meaningful way because they cannot suffer. He concluded that using such weapons would be unethical, a conclusion that other authors dispute.

## Related gaps
Santoni de Sio and Mecacci (2021) distinguished four gaps: in culpability, in moral accountability, in public accountability, and in active responsibility, and argued that they need different remedies. Danaher (2016) described a retribution gap, in which a community's wish to punish wrongdoers cannot be satisfied when harm results from a robot. A different source of gaps in human institutions is the problem of many hands (Thompson, 1980), where responsibility is dispersed across many contributors.

## Replies
Critics argue that the gap is overstated: tort law already assigns strict liability and vicarious liability without proof of fault, and professional duties of care apply to those who choose to deploy unpredictable systems. Others hold that deployers can be responsible for the risks they accept, or that responsibility can be assigned to institutions or distributed across a socio-technical system. Defenders maintain that some uses remain where such assignments appear unfair or inadequate.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is distant. Active Inference gives a formal account of how agents infer and act on goals, which bears on how intentions might be attributed to artificial agents, but it offers no criteria for moral responsibility. The gap concerns norms for assigning accountability, not mechanisms of agency.
