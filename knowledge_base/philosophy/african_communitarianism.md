# African Communitarianism

## Overview
African communitarianism is a label for a cluster of positions in African philosophy that give community a central role in ethics, politics and the account of the person. It is not a single doctrine. Its best-known statements in English appear in the work of Kwame Gyekye, drawing on Akan thought from Ghana, and of Ifeanyi Menkiti, drawing on traditional thought more broadly. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on African ethics, written by Gyekye, presents several of its characteristic themes. The label should not suggest that Africa is culturally uniform, and the positions it names have been contested within African philosophy (see [[ethnophilosophy]]).

## Main themes
- **Humanism and the common good.** In Gyekye's account, morality in African thought is a humanistic one, centred on human welfare. The Akan image of a two-headed crocodile with a single stomach symbolises the common good, which is not the sum of individual goods but what is essentially good for human beings as such, including the basic conditions for the fulfilment of each individual. On this view there need be no conceptual opposition between the common good and the good of the individual, and individual liberty is itself among the basic goods.
- **Sociality.** Human beings are social by nature. Gyekye cites the Akan maxim that when a human being descends from the heavens, he or she descends into a human town. A social morality follows, in which each person has a dual responsibility, for oneself and for others as co-members of the community.
- **Duty and rights.** Because moral life is embedded in community, African ethics on this account places weight on duties, such as compassion, solidarity, reciprocity, cooperation and interdependence, while not giving short shrift to rights.
- **Personhood as attained.** Menkiti writes that personhood is "the sort of thing which has to be attained", and is attained in direct proportion as one participates in communal life through the discharge of obligations. The Akan word *onipa* means both person and human being, which Gyekye takes to show that the concept of a person carries ethical presuppositions.

## Debate
Positions differ over how strongly community takes priority over the individual. Menkiti's emphasis on personhood as a status achieved through communal obligations is stronger than Gyekye's claim that the common good embraces the basic goods, including liberty, of every member. The wider question of how far such accounts generalise across African societies remains open.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is loose. Active Inference accounts of social cognition hold that agents' models are shaped by their social niche, so that selves are partly constituted by relations, which resembles the claim that personhood is attained in community (see [[care_ethics]] for a Western relational ethics). Active Inference makes no normative claim about the common good, and the comparison concerns only the shared emphasis on relationality.
