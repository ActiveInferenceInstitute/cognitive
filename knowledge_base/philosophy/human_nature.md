# Human Nature

## Overview
Human nature is the idea that there are characteristic features, such as capacities, dispositions or needs, common to human beings and distinguishing them from other things. The question has long been tied to ethics and politics, since views about what people are like have often been used to support views about how they should live. This page describes positions without endorsing any.

## Positions
- **Classical teleological accounts.** Aristotle characterised humans as rational and political animals whose good lies in the exercise of distinctive capacities, in a way that links nature to flourishing.
- **Early modern accounts.** Hobbes, Hume and Rousseau gave competing portraits of people in a state of nature and of the role of passions, sympathy and reason, and used them to ground different views of society.
- **Existentialist rejection.** Sartre claimed that for humans existence precedes essence, so that no fixed nature precedes choice (see [[existentialism]]).
- **Biological and evolutionary accounts.** Evolutionary psychologists propose that humans share universal cognitive adaptations. Pinker (2002) argued against a "blank slate" view of the mind, while critics emphasise the plasticity of development and the diversity of cultures.
- **Challenges from biology.** Hull (1986) argued that species are historical lineages, not natural kinds defined by essences, so that there can be no human nature in the essentialist sense of properties necessary and sufficient for membership. Defenders reply that a statistical or causal-homeostatic notion of shared nature survives this argument.
- **Cultural and developmental views.** Some stress that culture, language and niche construction shape the mind, so that human nature is partly constituted by learned practices (see [[cultural_cognition]], [[cultural_transmission]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The framework makes no claim about an essence of human nature. It offers a general account of adaptive systems and of how generative models are acquired through development and learning. Researchers working on cultural niche construction use it to describe how shared beliefs and practices shape individuals, an approach that bears on the nature-nurture debate but is a research proposal, not a resolution. Any claim about characteristic human dispositions would rest on empirical work in psychology and anthropology.
