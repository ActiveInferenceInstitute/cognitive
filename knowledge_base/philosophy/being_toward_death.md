# Being-Toward-Death

## Overview
Being-toward-death (*Sein zum Tode*) is a concept from Division Two of Heidegger's *Being and Time* (1927). It describes how [[dasein]], the kind of being that humans have, relates to the possibility of its own death. Heidegger treats death not as a future event to be observed but as a possibility that is one's own, cannot be shared or replaced, and is certain yet indefinite in its timing.

## Main points
- **Death as possibility.** Death is the possibility of the end of all possibilities of existing. Anticipation, as opposed to expecting or waiting for death, keeps this possibility open instead of treating it as an actuality.
- **Everyday evasion.** In everyday life the anonymous "they" (*das Man*) treats death as something that happens to people in general, so that one's own mortality is covered over. Heidegger uses the term "fallenness" for this absorption.
- **Anxiety and anticipation.** Anxiety (*Angst*) discloses the groundlessness of existence, and resolute anticipation of death individualises Dasein, releasing it from the sway of the "they". This is linked to [[authenticity]].
- **Temporality.** The analysis leads into Heidegger's account of finitude and temporality as the meaning of Dasein's being.

Heidegger states that the analysis is existential and ontological and does not address what happens after death or the ethics of dying. Interpreters differ about how far the account is formal or carries evaluative commitments, and Sartre and others criticised it. Blattner (2006) and Carman (2003) provide scholarly guides.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct connection. In the free energy framework, living systems persist by keeping themselves within a limited set of expected states, and a few authors have remarked on the parallel between this and the finitude of embodied agents, but the framework offers no account of death as lived possibility. Any connection is a loose analogy.
