# Ajivika Determinism

## Overview
The Ajivikas were an ascetic religious movement of ancient India, a contemporary of early Buddhism and Jainism, which is generally associated with the teacher Makkhali Gosala (Gosala Makkhaliputta). Both early Buddhist and Jain texts present Gosala as a proponent of *niyati*, usually translated as fate or destiny, and later writers described the group as a whole as determinists, *niyati-vadins*. The Ajivikas are of interest to the history of the philosophy of determinism because niyati is an early Indian statement of fatalism, but its details are uncertain.

## Doctrine as reported
Buddhist accounts, such as the discussion of the six rival teachers in the Samannaphala Sutta (Digha Nikaya 2), give Gosala's view as a fatalism on which beings are powerless and suffering is predestined. Buddhist and Jain authors, who held that action has consequences through karma, opposed the view. The Ajivika position is thus often contrasted with the doctrines of karmic causation in [[buddhist_philosophy]] and [[hindu_philosophy]].

## Evidence and its limits
The Ajivika literature is lost. Fragments of their doctrine survive in Buddhist and Jain sources, and the movement is mentioned in several inscriptions from the Mauryan period. Because most of what is known was recorded by rival groups, distortion or deliberate criticism may be present. A. L. Basham's *History and Doctrines of the Ajivikas* (1951) collected and interpreted the surviving references and remains the standard starting point. More recently, Piotr Balcerowicz has examined the passages often identified, notably by Basham, as genuine quotations from lost Ajivika works and concluded that none seems to derive from an Ajivika source, and that the reliability of reports of their views cannot be assessed with certainty. Statements about Ajivika philosophy should therefore be treated as reports about what opponents said they held.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is slight. Fatalism is a thesis about the irrelevance of choice to outcomes, whereas Active Inference models agents whose actions change the probability of future observations, so the framework presupposes that policies matter. The comparison with physical determinism is treated under [[determinism]]; niyati is a metaphysical doctrine of destiny, not a claim about causal laws.
