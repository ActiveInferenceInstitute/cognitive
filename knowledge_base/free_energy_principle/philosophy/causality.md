# Causality and the Free Energy Principle

## Overview

Causality stands at the heart of the Free Energy Principle in two distinct but intertwined senses. First, the FEP is a theory about how systems infer the **causal structure** of their environments -- generative models are, fundamentally, causal models that represent how hidden causes give rise to sensory observations. Second, the FEP itself involves a distinctive form of **circular causality** in which organisms both shape and are shaped by their environments, challenging linear, unidirectional conceptions of causal explanation.

This document examines the philosophical dimensions of causality as they intersect with the FEP. It addresses how the FEP relates to formal theories of causation (Pearl's structural causal models, Woodward's interventionism), the problem of bidirectional and circular causality inherent in active inference, the question of top-down causation and causal emergence, and the role of counterfactual reasoning in intelligent behavior. These issues bear directly on whether the FEP constitutes a genuine causal theory of cognition or merely a redescription of correlational dynamics.

## Key Positions

### Generative Models as Causal Models

The FEP holds that organisms maintain **generative models** of the world -- probabilistic models of how observations are generated by hidden causes. These generative models are inherently causal:

```
Generative model: p(o, s | theta) = p(o | s, theta) * p(s | theta)

Causal interpretation:
  s  = hidden causes (latent variables generating observations)
  o  = observations (effects of hidden causes, mediated by blanket)
  theta = causal parameters (strengths and structures of causal relationships)

Causal direction: s -> o  (causes generate effects)
Inference direction: o -> s  (effects used to infer causes)
```

This structure mirrors a **structural causal model** (SCM) in Pearl's (2000) framework. The generative model specifies a joint distribution that factorizes according to a causal graph, and perceptual inference inverts this causal graph to recover the most probable causes from their effects.

### Pearl's Do-Calculus and Active Inference

Pearl's (2000, 2009) theory of causation distinguishes sharply between **observing** and **intervening**. Observation updates beliefs via conditioning; intervention requires the do-operator:

```
Observation:  p(Y | X = x)       -- conditional probability (what is Y when X is observed to be x?)
Intervention: p(Y | do(X = x))   -- interventional probability (what is Y when X is SET to x?)
```

The critical difference:

```
p(Y | X = x) =/= p(Y | do(X = x))   in general

Example: p(wet_grass | sprinkler_on) includes the possibility that rain caused both
         p(wet_grass | do(sprinkler_on)) isolates the sprinkler's causal effect
```

Active inference provides a natural implementation of the do-operator:

```
Perceptual inference:  q(s) = argmin_q F[q, o]           [observing: condition on o]
Active inference:      a* = argmin_a G[a, pi]             [intervening: do(a)]

Where:
  G = expected free energy under policy pi
  a = action = intervention on the world through active states
```

When an agent acts, it performs an intervention in Pearl's sense: it sets its active states to particular values, thereby changing the causal graph. The agent does not merely *observe* the consequences of its actions but *causes* them. This distinction is crucial:

```
Structural causal model with intervention:

Before intervention:   S -> B_s -> mu        [sensory flow: world causes internal states]
                       mu -> B_a -> S         [active flow: internal states cause world changes]

Intervention do(a):    mu -> B_a = a -> S     [action severs incoming arrows to B_a]
                       The agent sets B_a directly, breaking the observational association
```

This formalization shows that active inference is inherently interventionist: the agent's actions constitute genuine causal interventions that modify the causal graph, not mere observations of passively unfolding correlations.

### The Do-Calculus in Generative Models

The agent's generative model must distinguish between observations and interventions to reason causally. This requires incorporating the do-calculus into the generative model:

```
Observational model:    p(o_t+1 | s_t, a_t = observed)
Interventional model:   p(o_t+1 | s_t, do(a_t))

For correct causal reasoning:
  p(o | s, do(a)) = Sum_s' p(o | s') * p(s' | s, a)    [truncated factorization]
```

The truncated factorization -- the hallmark of Pearl's do-calculus -- arises naturally in the FEP when actions are treated as interventions rather than observations. The agent's generative model must include a **causal model** of how its actions affect the world, distinct from its model of how the world passively evolves.

### Counterfactual Reasoning

Pearl's (2000) three-level hierarchy of causal reasoning maps onto FEP inference:

```
Level 1 - Association:     p(o | o')              What is?
Level 2 - Intervention:    p(o | do(a))           What if I do?
Level 3 - Counterfactual:  p(o_cf | do(a'), o)    What if I had done differently?
```

Under the FEP, counterfactual reasoning requires the agent to:

1. **Infer the actual state**: q(s | o, a) -- what was the world state given what I observed and did
2. **Simulate alternative actions**: p(o' | s, do(a')) -- what would I have observed if I had acted differently
3. **Compare outcomes**: G(a) vs. G(a') -- which action would have led to lower expected free energy

```
Counterfactual free energy:
  F_cf(a') = E_q(s|o,a)[ E_p(o'|s,do(a'))[ ln q(s) - ln p(o', s | a') ] ]

This evaluates what free energy WOULD have been under alternative action a',
given the actual state inferred from the actual action a.
```

Counterfactual reasoning of this kind is essential for learning from experience: the agent updates its generative model (and policy) by comparing actual outcomes with counterfactual alternatives.

## Detailed Discussion

### Circular Causality in the FEP

A distinctive feature of the FEP is its embrace of **circular causality** -- the bidirectional causal relationship between organism and environment:

```
Environment -> Sensory states -> Internal states -> Active states -> Environment
     ^                                                                    |
     |____________________________________________________________________|

This is not a linear chain but a causal loop:
  External states cause sensory states (perception)
  Internal states cause active states (action)
  Active states cause external states (intervention)
  External states cause sensory states (perception)
  ...
```

This circular structure poses challenges for standard causal analysis, which typically assumes acyclicity (directed acyclic graphs, or DAGs). The FEP addresses this in several ways:

**Temporal Unrolling**: The causal loop can be "unrolled" in time into a DAG:

```
s_t -> o_t -> mu_t -> a_t -> s_t+1 -> o_t+1 -> mu_t+1 -> a_t+1 -> ...
```

At each time step, the causal direction is clear: past states cause future states. Circular causality is circular only when considered atemporally; in the temporal unfolding, it is a directed chain.

**Steady-State Analysis**: At the non-equilibrium steady state (NESS), the system exhibits stable circular flows. The causal structure is not a one-time chain but a persistent pattern of reciprocal influence:

```
At NESS:  p(s, o, mu, a) is stationary
          but the flow f(x) is non-zero (solenoidal component)
          Circular causality = persistent solenoidal flow through the partition
```

**Structural Causal Models with Cycles**: Pearl's framework has been extended to cyclic causal models (Bongers et al., 2021). Under these extensions, the FEP's circular causality can be formalized rigorously:

```
Cyclic SCM:
  S := f_s(A, U_s)       [external states depend on active states]
  O := f_o(S, U_o)       [observations depend on external states]
  mu := f_mu(O, U_mu)    [internal states depend on observations]
  A := f_a(mu, U_a)      [active states depend on internal states]

Equilibrium solution: Fixed point of the simultaneous equations
```

### Interventionism and the FEP

Woodward's (2003) interventionist theory of causation holds that X causes Y if and only if there exists an intervention on X that changes Y. This provides a natural framework for understanding active inference:

```
Woodward's criterion: X causes Y iff there exists intervention I on X such that
                      changing I changes the probability distribution of Y

Active inference satisfies this:
  Actions (A) cause environmental changes (S) because
  intervening on A (choosing different actions) changes the distribution of S
```

However, the FEP extends interventionism in an important direction: the agent does not merely *test* causal relationships through intervention but actively *constructs* causal relationships through its engagement with the environment. Through active inference, the agent shapes its niche, creating the very causal regularities it then exploits:

```
Niche construction: Agent actions create environmental regularities
                    Agent models then exploit these regularities
                    Creating further actions that reinforce the regularities
                    -> The agent is both cause and effect of its causal environment
```

This is **reciprocal causation** in the strongest sense: the causal structure of the environment is not a fixed backdrop against which the agent acts, but is co-constructed by the agent's actions.

### The Causal Markov Condition

The **Causal Markov Condition** (CMC) states that, conditional on its direct causes, a variable is independent of all non-descendants. This is the fundamental axiom connecting causal graphs to probability distributions:

```
CMC: p(X_i | parents(X_i)) = p(X_i | parents(X_i), non-descendants(X_i))
```

The FEP's Markov blanket formalism embodies a generalization of the CMC:

```
Markov blanket condition:
  p(mu | b) = p(mu | b, s)    [internal states are independent of external states
                                given blanket states]
```

This is precisely the CMC applied to the partition into internal, blanket, and external states. The Markov blanket IS the set of "causal parents and children" of the internal states -- it screens off the internal states from direct causal influence by external states.

The philosophical significance: the Markov blanket is not merely a statistical construct but a **causal boundary**. It defines the causal reach of the internal states -- what they can be influenced by (through sensory states) and what they can influence (through active states).

### Top-Down Causation

A persistent philosophical puzzle is **top-down causation** -- whether higher-level entities (minds, organisms, societies) can causally influence lower-level entities (neurons, cells, individuals). The FEP provides a formal account:

```
Hierarchical generative model:
  Level 3 (narrative):  s_3 -> s_2        [top-down predictions]
  Level 2 (conceptual): s_2 -> s_1        [top-down predictions]
  Level 1 (perceptual): s_1 -> o          [top-down predictions]

Top-down causation = high-level predictions constraining lower-level inference
                   = precision-weighted prediction errors flowing upward,
                     predictions flowing downward
```

In this framework, "the mind causes behavior" is recast as: high-level generative model states (beliefs, goals, intentions) generate top-down predictions that constrain lower-level inference and, ultimately, motor output. This is genuine top-down causation in Woodward's interventionist sense: intervening on high-level beliefs changes low-level behavior.

### Causal Emergence

Hoel (2017) and colleagues have argued for **causal emergence** -- the idea that higher-level descriptions can be more causally efficacious than lower-level descriptions. The measure of causal emergence uses effective information (EI):

```
Effective Information: EI(X -> Y) = H(Y | do(X ~ uniform)) - H(Y | do(X = x))

Causal emergence: EI at macro-level > EI at micro-level
```

The FEP connects to causal emergence through its hierarchical structure:

```
At micro-level: Individual neurons, high-dimensional, noisy
  -> EI_micro may be low (many micro-states map to same macro-effect)

At macro-level: Population codes, lower-dimensional, more deterministic
  -> EI_macro may be high (macro-states have more deterministic effects)

FEP interpretation: The generative model's hierarchical structure IS the
                    macro-level description. Higher levels of the hierarchy
                    are coarse-grained descriptions with (potentially)
                    higher causal efficacy.
```

This provides a principled account of why high-level mental descriptions (beliefs, desires, intentions) can be genuinely causally explanatory despite supervening on lower-level neural activity: the higher-level description may capture more effective information about the system's causal structure.

### Causal vs. Correlational Models

A critical philosophical question is whether the FEP's generative models are genuinely causal or merely correlational. The distinction matters:

```
Correlational model:  p(o, s) -- joint distribution, no causal direction
Causal model:         p(o | do(s)) -- intervention distribution, causal direction

Key test: Does the model support counterfactual reasoning?
  Correlational: p(o | s) -- what o is associated with s?
  Causal: p(o | do(s)) -- what o would result from setting s?
```

The FEP's generative models are genuinely causal because:

1. **Directed structure**: p(o | s) * p(s) specifies a directed generative process from causes to effects
2. **Active inference**: Actions constitute interventions, not mere observations
3. **Counterfactual support**: The model supports reasoning about what would happen under alternative actions
4. **Learning from intervention**: The agent updates its model based on the consequences of its interventions, not merely on observed correlations

However, a subtlety arises: the generative model is the agent's *hypothesis* about causal structure, which may be wrong. The agent's causal model is always a model of causation, not causation itself. Whether the agent's causal model tracks actual causal structure is an empirical question, resolved through the ongoing process of active inference and model selection.

## Connections to Other Frameworks

### Agency and Causation

The FEP provides a formal account of the intimate connection between agency and causal understanding. An agent, by definition, is something that intervenes in the world -- that performs actions with consequences. The FEP shows that this capacity for intervention is what grounds causal understanding:

```
No agency -> no intervention -> no do-operator -> no causal reasoning
Agency -> intervention -> do-operator -> causal reasoning (at least implicit)
```

This aligns with interventionist philosophy (Woodward, 2003) and with developmental psychology (Gopnik et al., 2004), which finds that children learn causal structure through active experimentation.

### Predictive Coding and Causal Inference

Predictive coding -- the neural implementation of the FEP -- naturally implements causal inference:

```
Bottom-up signals:  Prediction errors (effects)
Top-down signals:   Predictions (causes)
Inference:          Adjusting causes until effects are predicted
                  = inverting the causal model
```

The hierarchical structure of predictive coding mirrors the hierarchical structure of causation: higher levels represent more abstract, temporally extended causes, while lower levels represent more concrete, temporally proximate causes.

### Free Will and Causal Determinism

The FEP's circular causality has implications for the free will debate (see [[free_will|Free Will]]). If actions are determined by internal states, which are determined by observations, which are determined by external states, which are determined by previous actions -- is the agent genuinely free?

The FEP suggests a compatibilist resolution: the agent is "free" in the sense that its actions are determined by its own generative model (its beliefs, preferences, and goals), not by external forces directly. The Markov blanket ensures that external states influence internal states only through the sensory interface, and internal states influence external states only through the active interface. The agent's autonomy is constituted by this causal insulation.

## Open Questions

1. **Cyclic causation**: Can Pearl's framework be fully extended to handle the cyclic causal structures inherent in active inference, or is a new formal framework needed?

2. **Causal discovery**: How do agents learn novel causal structures (not just parameters)? The FEP provides structure learning through Bayesian model comparison, but the computational complexity of causal discovery is formidable.

3. **Causal emergence and consciousness**: Is consciousness an instance of causal emergence? Does the macro-level description (conscious experience) have higher effective information than the micro-level description (neural activity)?

4. **Intervention vs. observation in the brain**: How does the brain distinguish between internally generated signals (interventions, imagination) and externally generated signals (observations)? This is related to the problem of distinguishing agency from passivity.

5. **Cultural causal models**: How are culturally transmitted causal models (scientific theories, folk physics, religious explanations) incorporated into individual generative models through active inference?

6. **Causal responsibility**: If actions are the result of free energy minimizing inference, in what sense is the agent causally responsible for its actions? This connects to ethical and legal questions about moral responsibility.
