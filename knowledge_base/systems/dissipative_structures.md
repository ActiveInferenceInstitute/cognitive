# Dissipative Structures

## Overview

A dissipative structure is an ordered spatial or temporal pattern that forms and is maintained in an open system driven far from thermodynamic equilibrium, by a continuous throughput of energy or matter. The term and the theory come from Ilya Prigogine, who received the 1977 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for work on non-equilibrium thermodynamics. The pattern exists only while the flux continues: switching off the drive returns the system to equilibrium and the structure disappears.

## Mechanism

- **Open system and driving gradient**: the system exchanges energy or matter with its surroundings and is held away from equilibrium by a gradient, such as a temperature difference or a chemical supply.
- **Nonlinearity and feedback**: autocatalytic or amplifying interactions let small fluctuations grow.
- **Instability and bifurcation**: when a control parameter crosses a threshold, the uniform state becomes unstable and the system moves to a patterned state (see [[symmetry_breaking]]).
- **Entropy export**: the structure lowers local entropy while the overall entropy production, including that exported to the environment, remains positive. This is consistent with the second law.

## Examples

- **Benard convection**: a fluid layer heated from below is conductive and uniform until the Rayleigh number passes a critical value, about 1708 for a layer between rigid plates, after which hexagonal or roll convection cells appear.
- **Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction**: a chemical oscillator producing travelling waves and spirals.
- **Laser light**: above threshold, atoms emit coherently, an example Hermann Haken used in developing synergetics.
- **Hurricanes and flames**: self-sustaining patterns sustained by gradients.
- **Living organisms** are often described as dissipative systems, though their organisation also involves genetic and metabolic control that simple convection cells lack.

## Role in Active Inference

The free-energy principle describes a system that persists in a non-equilibrium steady state by resisting dispersion, and its proponents motivate this with the same physical picture: a living system maintains its structure through continual exchange across a boundary, minimising surprise about its own states. In this reading the dissipative structure is the physical substrate, and Active Inference is a description of how a system that persists this way behaves as if it were inferring its environment. The relation is a conceptual bridge and a formal research topic in non-equilibrium steady-state densities; it does not mean that every dissipative structure performs inference. Convection cells, for example, have no meaningful generative model or action selection. Biological organisation adds the capacity to anticipate and regulate, which is where [[autopoiesis]] and Active Inference go beyond purely physical dissipative structures.
