# Cybernetics

## Overview
Cybernetics is the transdisciplinary study of regulation and purposeful behaviour in systems that act on, and are informed by, their environment. The name was proposed by Norbert Wiener in 1948 from the Greek word for steersman. The field grew out of wartime work on control and communication and the Macy Conferences of the 1940s and 1950s, bringing together mathematicians, engineers, biologists, psychologists and anthropologists around a shared vocabulary of feedback, information and goal-directed behaviour.

## Central ideas
- **Negative feedback.** A system compares a sensed quantity to a reference and acts to reduce the difference, giving stable goal-seeking behaviour (see [[feedback_loops]]).
- **Homeostasis.** Ashby analysed organisms as ultrastable systems that keep essential variables within viable bounds (see [[homeostasis]]).
- **Requisite variety.** Ashby's law states that a regulator can only reduce the variety of outcomes to the extent that it has at least as much variety in its actions as the disturbances it must counter.
- **Good regulator theorem.** Conant and Ashby showed that, under their stated conditions, every good regulator of a system must contain a model of that system.
- **Second-order cybernetics.** Heinz von Foerster and others turned the framework on the observer, studying observing systems and their circular relations to what they observe.

## Influence
Cybernetics shaped control engineering, early artificial intelligence, systems theory, family therapy, management science and the study of living systems, including Maturana and Varela's work on autopoiesis. Later specialised fields such as control theory, information theory and cognitive science absorbed many of its results, and the unified field label declined in use.

## Relation to Active Inference
The lineage is explicit and the formal parallels are close. Active Inference can be read as a probabilistic descendant of the cybernetic agenda: the good regulator theorem anticipates the idea that an agent embodies a generative model of its environment, homeostasis corresponds to maintaining expected states, and action reduces prediction error much as a controller reduces deviation from a set point. Friston and colleagues cite this heritage. The theoretical content is more specific, adding a variational formulation of both perception and control, and the precise scope of the correspondence with the Conant-Ashby theorem is debated.
