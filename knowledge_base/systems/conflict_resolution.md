# Conflict Resolution

## Overview

Conflict arises when agents hold goals, resources or beliefs that cannot all be satisfied at once. Conflict resolution is the set of processes by which such incompatibilities are reduced or settled: negotiation between the parties, mediation by a third party, arbitration or adjudication by an authority, and the design of institutions that handle disputes before they escalate. The sense used here is the one relevant to cooperative groups and to shared management of resources, such as watersheds or bioregions, where stakeholders have overlapping claims.

## Sources and structure of conflict

- **Interest conflict**: parties want incompatible outcomes from a limited resource.
- **Information conflict**: parties hold different beliefs about facts or consequences, which can often be reduced by shared evidence.
- **Value conflict**: parties differ on what ought to be pursued, which evidence alone does not resolve.
- **Structural conflict**: roles or rules produce competing incentives, as in commons dilemmas and prisoner's-dilemma payoffs.

## Approaches

- **Interest-based negotiation**: Fisher and Ury's *Getting to Yes* (1981) distinguishes positions from underlying interests, and recommends separating people from the problem and generating options for mutual gain. Many conflicts have solutions in which both sides gain once interests are unbundled.
- **Strategic styles**: the Thomas-Kilmann model describes five modes (competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating) as combinations of assertiveness and cooperativeness. It is a descriptive taxonomy, not a prescription.
- **Mediation and facilitation**: a neutral party controls the process but not the outcome.
- **Institutional design**: Elinor Ostrom's studies of commons found that long-lived self-governing arrangements usually include low-cost conflict-resolution mechanisms and graduated sanctions.
- **Game-theoretic mechanisms**: repeated interaction, reputation, commitment devices and side payments change the incentives so that cooperation becomes stable (see [[game_theory]]).
- **Adaptive management**: where conflict stems from uncertainty about ecological outcomes, shared monitoring and revisable agreements, as in [[adaptive_comanagement]], reduce disputes over facts.

## Role in Active Inference

Conflict can be modelled as agents with different generative models and different prior preferences over outcomes: one agent's expected free energy is minimised by actions that raise another's. Resolution then involves either aligning the beliefs (communication, shared evidence, modelling the other agent as in [[theory_of_mind]]) or aligning the preferences, or finding policies that satisfy both. Multi-agent Active Inference simulations of shared goals and communication illustrate this, but conflict resolution as a dedicated treatment is not a settled part of the formalism. It is best read as an application area that draws on the framework's account of social inference. The bioregional state-space pages in this repo name conflict resolution among coordination mechanisms without formalising it.
