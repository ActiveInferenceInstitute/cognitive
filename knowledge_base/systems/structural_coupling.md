# Structural Coupling

## Overview
Structural coupling is a concept introduced by the biologists Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela in their theory of autopoiesis. It describes a history of recurrent interactions between a system and its environment, or between two systems, in which each triggers structural changes in the other. The system is not instructed by the environment: what an interaction does is determined by the system's own structure, and the environment only triggers changes. Over time the structures of the coupled partners become mutually congruent, while the organisation that defines each system as the kind of system it is stays invariant.

## Key distinctions
- **Structure and organisation.** Organisation is the set of relations that makes a system a member of a class, such as a living cell. Structure is the particular components and relations that realise it at a given moment. Structure can change, but a change of organisation means the system disintegrates (see [[organizational_closure]]).
- **Triggering and instruction.** The environment perturbs, and the system's structure specifies the response, so there are no inputs in the sense of an information transfer.
- **Fit as a history.** Adaptation is described as congruence resulting from a history of coupling and not as a representation of the environment. Maturana and Varela used the notion to account for ontogeny, evolution as drift of lineages with their niches, and linguistic interaction between organisms.

## Reception
The concept was taken up in enactive cognitive science, where cognition is understood as the activity of a coupled agent-environment system (see [[enactivism]]), and in the social systems theory of Niklas Luhmann. Critics find the notion difficult to operationalise and question whether it explains specific adaptations.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an active, and contested, conceptual programme. The free energy principle describes an agent and its environment as coupled dynamical systems separated by a Markov blanket, and discussions by Kirchhoff and colleagues relate this to autopoiesis and enactive autonomy. Both frameworks hold that organisms maintain themselves through continuous interaction with a niche. Whether a statistical blanket captures the biological sense of organisational closure and coupling is disputed, and the correspondence remains an interpretation.
