# Social Coordination

## Overview
Social coordination is the alignment of behavior among members of a social group through social means: shared attention, imitation, communication, norms, roles, and institutions. It is the sub-case of coordination in which the agents are socially embedded and the solution relies on mutual knowledge, shared history, or shared expectations.

## Mechanism
At the fine-grained level, interacting people synchronize without explicit instruction. Interpersonal synchrony arises in rhythmic activity such as rocking chairs or walking, and in conversation, where partners align posture, speech rate, and syntax. Joint attention, the co-orientation of two individuals to the same object with mutual awareness, is a developmental prerequisite for collaborative action; Tomasello and colleagues argue that shared intentionality, the capacity to form joint goals, distinguishes human cooperation from that of other apes. In joint action, partners predict one another's contributions and adjust their own, an ability often described as co-representation.

At the group level, norms and conventions stabilize expectations about what others will do. Where many equilibria exist, a convention selects one, and it persists because deviation by an individual is costly when others conform. Roles and institutions fix expectations further and reduce the cost of deliberation. Ostrom's analysis of common-pool resources documented how communities coordinate over shared resources through monitoring, graduated sanctions, and locally devised rules without either privatization or central control. Reputation and gossip support coordination in larger groups by spreading information about reliability. In social insects, comparable results arise without norms or mutual understanding, through pheromones, contact rates, and thresholds, which is why the comparison with human coordination is typically drawn in terms of function rather than mechanism.

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference treatments of social behavior assume agents model one another, so that coordination emerges from shared or aligned generative models and from reciprocal prediction. Proposals include shared narratives or cultural priors that constrain the policies agents consider plausible, and the idea that norms act as high-level priors that make others' behavior predictable. These ideas are theoretically developed and illustrated with small simulations; strong empirical tests in real human groups are limited. The repository treats the individual-agent and multi-agent cases in separate pages, which should be read together with this one.
