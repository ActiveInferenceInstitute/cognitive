# Geometric Control Theory

## Overview
Geometric control theory treats a control system as a family of vector fields on a manifold and asks which states can be reached by switching among them. Instead of linearising around an operating point, it analyses the nonlinear system with the tools of [[differential_geometry]], above all the Lie bracket of vector fields. Standard references are Jurdjevic (1997) and Agrachev and Sachkov (2004).

## Setting
A control-affine system on a manifold $M$ has the form
$$\dot x=f(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_i\,g_i(x),\qquad x\in M,$$
where $f$ is the **drift** and the $g_i$ are the control vector fields. If $f=0$ the system is **driftless**. The **Lie bracket** $[X,Y]$ of two vector fields is the vector field that measures the failure of their flows to commute. Moving first along $X$, then $Y$, then back along $X$ and $Y$ displaces the state approximately in the direction $[X,Y]$, so brackets give directions of motion that are not available directly.

## Controllability and the Lie algebra rank condition
Let $\mathfrak{L}$ be the Lie algebra of vector fields generated by the $g_i$ and, if present, the drift, using repeated brackets. The **Lie algebra rank condition** requires that the values of $\mathfrak{L}$ at a point $x$ span the tangent space $T_xM$. For a driftless system the Chow-Rashevskii theorem says that, on a connected manifold, this condition holding at every point implies that any two points can be joined by a trajectory. For systems with drift, the rank condition ensures that the reachable set has nonempty interior (accessibility), and Sussmann and Jurdjevic (1972) gave additional conditions under which it yields full controllability.

## Example: a car
A planar car with position $(x,y)$ and heading $\theta$, driven forward and turned, has the driftless form with
$$g_1=(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta,\ 0)^{\top},\qquad g_2=(0,\ 0,\ 1)^{\top}.$$
There are only two controls in a three-dimensional state space, and neither $g_1$ nor $g_2$ moves the car sideways. Their bracket is $[g_1,g_2]=\pm(\sin\theta,\,-\cos\theta,\,0)^{\top}$, the sideways direction, and the determinant of the matrix with columns $g_1$, $g_2$ and the bracket has absolute value $1$ at every state. The rank condition holds everywhere, so the car can reach any configuration, which is the mathematical content of parallel parking.

## Links to sub-Riemannian and optimal control
For a driftless system, choosing a metric on the span of the controls defines a sub-Riemannian structure, and minimum-length or minimum-energy trajectories are its geodesics (see [[sub_riemannian_geometry]]). Optimal control problems are then analysed with the Pontryagin maximum principle on the cotangent bundle, which carries a [[symplectic_geometry|symplectic structure]] (Agrachev and Sachkov, 2004). Systems whose state evolves on a Lie group, such as a rigid body on $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, have controls expressed through the group structure (see [[lie_groups]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
No established derivation connects geometric control theory to Active Inference. The two are related in subject matter, since both concern how an agent steers a system through a state space, but Active Inference casts action selection as inference with a generative model and expected free energy (see [[optimal_control]] for the contrast with value-based control), whereas geometric control analyses reachability of a given dynamical system. Geometric treatments of belief dynamics are discussed in [[geometric_active_inference]].
