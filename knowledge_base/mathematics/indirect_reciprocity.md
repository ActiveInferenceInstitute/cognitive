# Indirect Reciprocity

## Overview
Indirect reciprocity is cooperation based on reputation: an individual helps a recipient because of the recipient's past behaviour toward others, and expects to be helped later by someone else who has observed or heard of this help. Where direct reciprocity needs repeated meetings between the same two individuals, indirect reciprocity needs only that actions are observed and remembered by third parties. Alexander (1987) proposed it as a basis for human moral systems.

## Models
In the standard donation game, a donor can pay a cost $c$ to give a recipient a benefit $b>c$. Each individual carries a reputation, and donors decide whether to help based on the recipient's reputation. Nowak and Sigmund (1998) analysed image scoring, in which helping raises and refusing lowers one's score. Image scoring can sustain cooperation but faces a problem: refusing to help a recipient with a bad score is itself scored as defection, which discourages justified punishment. Later models use norms that take both the donor's and the recipient's reputations into account, such as stern judging and standing. A systematic study of third-order norms identified a small set of strategies that can maintain cooperation.

Under simple assumptions, cooperation is favoured when $q>c/b$, where $q$ is the probability that a donor knows the recipient's reputation (Nowak, 2006). The result links the evolution of cooperation to how reliably information about conduct is shared, and in humans it is argued to depend on language and gossip.

## Limits
Outcomes depend on the assessment norm, on whether views of reputation are private or shared, and on errors in perception and action. Results differ between models, and the empirical evidence is mixed, with experiments showing reputation effects on giving and also considerable variation between individuals.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no derivation from the free energy principle. The link is that reputation is an inference about other agents' types and policies, and Active Inference models of social interaction represent such beliefs explicitly. Accounts of shared norms and cultural niche construction use this vocabulary. These are modelling frameworks that can host indirect reciprocity, not explanations of why it evolved.
