# Canonical Transformations

## Overview
A canonical transformation is a change of phase-space coordinates $(q,p)\mapsto(Q,P)$ under which Hamilton's equations keep their form, with a new Hamiltonian $K(Q,P,t)$ in place of $H(q,p,t)$. Because Hamiltonian dynamics is not tied to a single choice of coordinates, such transformations allow a problem to be rewritten in variables where it is easier to solve.

## Characterisations
Several equivalent conditions define the class:
- the transformation preserves the fundamental Poisson brackets, $\{Q_i,P_j\}_{q,p}=\delta_{ij}$ and $\{Q_i,Q_j\}_{q,p}=\{P_i,P_j\}_{q,p}=0$;
- the Jacobian matrix is symplectic, so the symplectic two-form is preserved;
- the differential form $\sum_i p_i\,dq_i-\sum_i P_i\,dQ_i+(K-H)\,dt$ is exact.

The last condition leads to generating functions. For a type-one generating function $F_1(q,Q,t)$ the transformation is given by
$$p_i=\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial q_i},\qquad P_i=-\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial Q_i},\qquad K=H+\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial t}.$$
Three further types $F_2,F_3,F_4$ are obtained by Legendre transforms and use mixed arguments such as $(q,P)$.

## Consequences
Canonical transformations preserve phase-space volume, which is Liouville's theorem, and they map Hamiltonian flows to Hamiltonian flows. The time evolution of a system from $t_0$ to $t$ is itself a canonical transformation. The Hamilton-Jacobi method seeks a transformation to coordinates in which $K=0$, so that the new coordinates and momenta are constant. For integrable systems this leads to action-angle variables. Numerical schemes known as symplectic integrators are designed so that each step is a canonical map, which explains their good long-time energy behaviour.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is formal and indirect. Hamiltonian and symplectic structure appears in geometric treatments of the free energy principle, where the flow of states is decomposed into dissipative and solenoidal parts, and canonical transformations are the natural changes of variable for the Hamiltonian part. These treatments are an area of ongoing research, and the correspondence with biological systems is a modelling assumption, not an established result. The Hamilton-Jacobi equation of mechanics should not be confused with the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation of optimal control.
