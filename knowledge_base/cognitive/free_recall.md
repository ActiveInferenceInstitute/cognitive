# Free Recall

## Overview
Free recall is a memory test in which a person studies a list of items, such as words, and then reports as many as can be remembered, in any order, without being given retrieval cues. Because nothing is provided to guide retrieval, it is considered a demanding test of episodic memory that depends on how the items were organised and linked to the context in which they were studied. It contrasts with [[cued_recall]], in which a cue is provided, and with [[recognition_memory]], in which items are re-presented.

## The serial position curve
A classic result is that items from the start and end of the list are recalled better than items from the middle. Murdock (1962) showed that the form of the curve depends on list length and presentation rate. The advantage for the first items is called the primacy effect, and the advantage for the last items is called the recency effect. Glanzer and Cunitz (1966) found that a delay filled with a distracting task removed recency but left primacy intact, whereas slower presentation increased primacy without affecting recency. This was taken as evidence for two storage mechanisms: a short-term store supporting recency, and a long-term store supporting earlier items. Later work showed recency effects across long time scales when the time between items is proportionally scaled, which challenged a purely store-based account.

## Dynamics of recall
The order of output is informative. People often recall in the order of study and tend to recall items studied close together in succession, a pattern called the contiguity effect, in which successive recalls are more likely to be from neighbouring list positions than distant ones. Retrieved-context models explain this by assuming that studying an item updates a slowly drifting representation of context, and that recalling an item reinstates the context in which it was studied, which then cues its neighbours. Output also shows clustering by semantic similarity.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is limited. Active Inference describes retrieval as inference of a stored scene or sequence from partial cues (see [[pattern_completion]] and [[scene_construction]]), and in this view free recall is retrieval with a minimal cue. It has not been developed into a model of serial position or contiguity effects that competes with the retrieved-context models.
