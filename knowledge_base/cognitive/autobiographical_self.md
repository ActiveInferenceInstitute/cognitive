# Autobiographical Self

## Overview
The autobiographical self is the aspect of selfhood constituted by a person's history: remembered episodes, enduring traits, goals and a sense of continuity from past to anticipated future. It contrasts with the minimal self, a pre-reflective sense of being the subject of present experience (see [[minimal_self]]). The term is associated with Damasio (1999), who proposed a hierarchy of protoself, core self and autobiographical self, in which the last depends on the first two and on stored records of personal experience.

## Accounts
- **Damasio.** The protoself maps the current state of the body, the core self arises as an organism relates to an object and the autobiographical self draws on memory of that history to give an extended identity. Damasio argued that it is lost or reduced in conditions with severe memory loss, though the claim is debated.
- **Self-memory system.** Conway and Pleydell-Pearce (2000) described autobiographical memory as a system constrained by a working self, whose current goals shape which memories are constructed and retrieved, and by a knowledge base ranging from lifetime periods to specific events (see [[autobiographical_memory]]).
- **Narrative views.** Some philosophers and psychologists hold that people understand themselves by constructing a story that integrates events (see [[narrative_self]]). Others, such as Gallagher (2000), distinguish a narrative self from a minimal self and doubt that narrative is necessary for selfhood.

## Evidence
Patients with dense amnesia, such as those with hippocampal damage or Korsakoff syndrome, often retain a stable sense of personality and identity while losing the ability to recall new episodes, which shows that the sense of self depends on more than episodic recollection (see [[amnesia]] and [[korsakoff_syndrome]]). Neuroimaging links self-referential processing to midline cortical regions of the [[default_mode]] network.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is interpretive and in development. In predictive-processing terms, the self can be understood as a high-level model of the organism that predicts interoceptive and exteroceptive signals, while a temporally deep model carries expectations about past and future states (see [[self_modeling]] and [[selfhood]]). Under this view the autobiographical self corresponds to the slowly changing, high-level priors that give coherence to behaviour over long time scales. These ideas are theoretical proposals without decisive tests.
