# Distributed Practice

## Overview
Distributed practice, also called spaced practice, arranges learning episodes with intervals between them. Its counterpart, massed practice, concentrates the same amount of study into one session. For a fixed total study time, distributed schedules generally produce better long-term retention. The advantage, first documented by Ebbinghaus in the nineteenth century, is one of the most robust findings in learning research and is known as the spacing effect.

## Findings
- **Retention.** Cepeda and colleagues (2006), in a quantitative synthesis of verbal recall studies, found that spacing improves recall relative to massing, and that the benefit holds across many materials and age groups. A review of learning techniques by Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated distributed practice as having high utility.
- **Optimal gap.** The best gap between sessions depends on how long the information must be retained. In a large study, Cepeda and colleagues (2008) found that the ideal gap rose with the retention interval, and that a gap that was too long cost less than one that was too short.
- **Expanding versus uniform spacing.** Studies comparing the two schedules have mixed results, and the benefit of expanding gaps is not consistently found.
- **Retrieval.** Combining spacing with retrieval practice, as in spaced testing, tends to be more effective than spacing with restudy alone.

## Proposed explanations
Accounts include **study-phase retrieval**, in which a repeated item after a delay evokes retrieval of the earlier presentation, strengthening the trace; **encoding variability**, in which different contexts provide more retrieval routes; **deficient processing**, in which massed repetitions receive less attention; and consolidation-based accounts relating gaps and sleep to stabilisation. No single account explains all results.
