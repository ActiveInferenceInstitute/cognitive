# Alexia

## Overview
Alexia is an acquired impairment of reading in a person who could previously read, caused by brain damage such as stroke, trauma or neurodegeneration. It differs from developmental [[dyslexia]], which is a difficulty in learning to read. Alexia can occur alone or alongside spoken-language impairment (see [[aphasia]]), and the written word may be affected more than the spoken word, or the reverse. The name is sometimes used interchangeably with acquired dyslexia, particularly for the central subtypes.

## Subtypes
- **Peripheral alexias.** These arise from impaired visual processing of the word form. Pure alexia, also called alexia without agraphia or letter-by-letter reading, leaves writing intact while reading is slow and length-dependent. It is classically associated with damage to left occipitotemporal cortex and its connections (see [[pure_alexia]]). Neglect alexia, associated with spatial neglect, affects one side of words (see [[spatial_neglect]]).
- **Central alexias.** These involve the linguistic processing of written words. In surface alexia, regular words and nonwords are read adequately but irregular words are regularised (reading *pint* to rhyme with *mint*). In phonological alexia, real words are read better than nonwords. In deep alexia, reading errors include semantic substitutions, such as reading *ship* as *boat*, with a severe problem for nonwords (Coltheart et al., 1980).
- **Neural basis.** Imaging work identifies a region of left ventral occipitotemporal cortex that responds selectively to written words, often called the visual word form area. The extent to which it is specialised for reading is debated.

## Models
Dual-route models of reading, with a lexical route for known words and a sublexical route that converts letters to sounds, account for the double dissociation between surface and phonological alexia. Connectionist triangle models explain similar patterns through the division of labour between orthographic, phonological and semantic representations.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is exploratory. Predictive-coding accounts propose that ventral occipitotemporal cortex combines bottom-up visual input with top-down predictions about the word from phonological and semantic systems (Price and Devlin, 2011; see [[predictive_coding]]). On this view, damage to the sources of top-down prediction or to the visual pathway degrades the inference. This is a theoretical interpretation of reading and has not been used to derive predictions for the specific alexia subtypes.
