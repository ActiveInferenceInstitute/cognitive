# Scene Construction

## Overview
Scene construction is the mental generation of a coherent, spatially organised scene, assembled from stored elements, that can be viewed from a particular perspective. Hassabis and Maguire (2007) proposed that this capacity is a core process behind remembering the past, imagining the future and navigating, and that the hippocampus is central to it. On this view, recalling an event is a reconstruction in which a scene is rebuilt, not a replay of a stored recording.

## Evidence
Patients with bilateral hippocampal damage and amnesia were found to produce impoverished imagined scenarios, which lacked spatial coherence and detail, even when asked to imagine fictitious situations (Hassabis et al., 2007). Neuroimaging in healthy participants shows overlapping activity during remembering, imagining the future and imagining fictitious scenes, in a network that includes the hippocampus, parahippocampal cortex, retrosplenial cortex and medial prefrontal regions. The finding has been influential, though how far the hippocampus is specific to scenes as opposed to relational binding more generally is still discussed.

## Relation to Active Inference
Scene construction is explicitly part of the Active Inference literature. Mirza and colleagues (2016) modelled it as the inference of a scene, the hidden state that explains what is seen, from a series of saccades. The agent holds beliefs about possible scenes and chooses fixations that reduce uncertainty about which scene is present, a form of [[epistemic_foraging]]. The same generative model that renders a scene to explain sensory samples can also render it from memory or in imagination, which gives a unifying reading of perception, recollection and planning. Friston and Buzsaki (2016) discuss related ideas for the representation of sequences, what and when, in the hippocampus. These are working models with simulated agents, and they do not by themselves settle the neuroscientific dispute about hippocampal function.
