# Social Punishment

## Overview
Social punishment refers to sanctions that members of a group impose on others who violate norms or free-ride, ranging from gossip and ostracism to fines and physical harm. It is distinct from institutional punishment by a state in that the sanctioner is a peer who typically acts without formal authority. Of particular interest is costly punishment, in which the punisher pays a price to reduce another's payoff while gaining no direct material benefit.

## Experimental evidence
In public goods games, participants contribute to a shared pool whose returns are split equally, so free-riding is the individually payoff-maximising choice when players care only about their own material payoff. Fehr and Gächter (2002) found that when participants could pay to reduce the earnings of other group members, many did so, directing punishment mainly at low contributors, and that contributions rose and stayed high when punishment was available. They called this altruistic punishment because the punisher bears a cost while benefits accrue to the group. Punishment has also been observed in third-party settings, where the punisher is not personally affected by the violation.

Cross-cultural work reports substantial variation. Herrmann, Thöni and Gächter (2008) found that in several societies participants also punished high contributors, termed antisocial punishment, and that this weakened the cooperative benefits of punishment. Whether costly punishment increases overall welfare once its costs are counted is disputed, and some studies find that group earnings with punishment do not exceed those without it in short games. Theoretical models, such as Boyd and Richerson (1992), analyse how punishment can stabilise cooperation in large groups, and also show that it can stabilise other behaviours.

Explanations for why people punish include inequity aversion, a preference for enforcing norms, anger, and reputational concerns. Neuroimaging studies link punishment decisions to reward-related and affective regions, though interpretation remains debated.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. In Active Inference terms, punishment may be viewed as action to restore a preferred or expected state of the social world when others violate shared expectations; norms function as shared priors and violations generate prediction error (see [[social_norms]], [[prediction_error]]). Detailed process models that fit punishment data have not been established within the framework.
