# Simultanagnosia

## Overview
Simultanagnosia is a rare disorder in which a person can perceive a single object, or a single part of an object, at a time but cannot take in several objects or the structure of a whole scene. The name comes from Wolpert (1924), building on the earlier description by Bálint (1909) of a patient with bilateral brain damage. It is one component of Bálint's syndrome, which also includes optic ataxia and impaired voluntary eye movement to targets.

## Clinical features
Patients typically have large bilateral lesions in the parieto-occipital regions, caused by stroke, hypoxia, posterior cortical atrophy or other degenerative disease. Basic visual functions such as acuity can be relatively spared. Typical signs are:
- describing a picture one detail at a time and failing to grasp its overall meaning;
- difficulty counting or reaching for objects in a cluttered display, with performance improving when items are few;
- better performance when parts belong to one object or are connected by lines, showing a role for grouping;
- reading by letter, so that long words are difficult.

Luria (1959) described a patient who could see one item at a time, and subsequent work distinguished a dorsal form, with deficits in spatial attention linked to superior parietal damage, from a ventral form, in which impaired processing of object parts follows ventral occipitotemporal damage (Farah, 2004; see also Rizzo & Vecera, 2002). The disorder is distinct from visual agnosia, in which a single object is not recognised, and from hemispatial neglect, in which one side of space is ignored.

## Theoretical significance
The condition has been used to argue that perception of multiple items requires spatial attention to bind features into objects and distribute processing across a scene. It supports accounts in which the posterior parietal cortex builds a spatial map for attention and object-based selection, although the underlying mechanisms of the different forms remain debated.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no specific Active Inference account of simultanagnosia. In general predictive-processing terms, attention allocates precision across a spatial or object-based representation, and a loss of that distribution would be expected to restrict what can be inferred about a scene at once. This is an interpretive remark, not a modelling result.
