# Cognitive Offloading

## Overview
Cognitive offloading is the use of bodily action or external resources to reduce the information-processing requirements of a task. Writing a shopping list, setting a phone reminder, rotating a puzzle piece instead of imagining the rotation, counting on fingers and consulting a search engine are all examples. Risko and Gilbert (2016) defined it in these terms and reviewed its occurrence in laboratory tasks and everyday life.

## Forms
- **Storage offloading.** Placing information in an external store, such as notes or digital reminders, which may lower the load on [[working_memory]] and [[prospective_memory]].
- **Computation offloading.** Using tools or the body to perform operations, such as calculators, diagrams or gesturing.
- **Intrinsic and extrinsic action.** Some bodily actions, such as tilting the head, change the task itself, while others, such as tapping a screen, merely store results.
- **Social offloading.** Relying on other people to hold knowledge, a process described as transactive memory.

## Costs and benefits
Offloading generally improves performance on the immediate task and frees capacity for other activity, and people offload more as the demand rises or when the external aid is easy to use. Costs include weaker memory for offloaded content in some studies, the possibility of failure of the external store and dependence on it. Sparrow, Liu and Wegner (2011) reported that people who expect information to be available on a computer remember the information itself less well and remember where to find it better. Findings of this kind have varied across replications and designs, so the generality of effects on memory is uncertain. People's decisions to offload are guided by metacognitive judgments of their own memory and the cost of the aid, and these judgments can be biased (see [[metacognition]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is conceptual and has been argued in the predictive processing literature. Offloading is a form of action that changes the environment to make future inference easier, which corresponds to epistemic action and niche construction in Active Inference, and to Clark and Chalmers' extended mind thesis that external resources can form part of the cognitive system (Clark and Chalmers, 1998; see [[extended_mind]], [[epistemic_foraging]] and [[niche_construction]]). An agent can be described as choosing policies that minimise expected free energy, including policies that use external structure to reduce uncertainty. Quantitative models of offloading in this framework are limited.
