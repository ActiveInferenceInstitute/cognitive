# Dysdiadochokinesia

## Overview
Dysdiadochokinesia is difficulty in performing rapid, alternating movements smoothly and regularly. Diadochokinesia, the normal ability, refers to successive opposing movements such as turning the palm up and down. When it is impaired, the movements become slow, irregular in rhythm and amplitude, and clumsy. The complete absence of the ability is sometimes called adiadochokinesia. It is a clinical sign, not a disease.

## Clinical examination
The sign is assessed by asking a patient to pronate and supinate the forearm quickly, tap the palm of one hand with the back of the other hand in alternation, or tap the fingers rapidly on a surface. The clinician observes rhythm, speed and regularity on each side. Dysdiadochokinesia occurs ipsilateral to a cerebellar hemisphere lesion, and it is usually seen with other cerebellar signs such as dysmetria, intention tremor and ataxia of gait. It is not specific to the cerebellum: slowed or irregular rapid movements also occur in Parkinsonism and with pyramidal or sensory deficits, so interpretation relies on the overall examination. This entry is descriptive and not medical advice.

## Underlying mechanisms
Rapid alternation requires precise timing of the reversal between agonist and antagonist muscle activation. The cerebellum is thought to contribute to this timing and to coordination across joints. Holmes' classic clinical descriptions of cerebellar disorders linked such signs to impaired control of the start and stop of movements. Computational accounts propose that the cerebellum supports forward models that predict the sensory consequences of motor commands, so that corrections can be made without waiting for sensory feedback (Wolpert, Miall and Kawato, 1998). Loss of this predictive timing would lead to delayed or mistimed reversals.

## Relation to Active Inference
In Active Inference, movement is generated by proprioceptive predictions that spinal reflex arcs fulfil, and the precision of predictions governs coordination (Adams, Shipp and Friston, 2013). A cerebellar deficit could be framed as degraded prediction of movement dynamics. This is an interpretive framing of a well-established clinical sign, and not a clinically validated explanation.
