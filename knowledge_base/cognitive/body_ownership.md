# Body Ownership

## Overview
Body ownership is the experience that a particular body or body part is one's own. It is usually distinguished from the sense of agency, which is the experience of causing one's own actions, and from the body schema, an implicit representation of posture used to guide movement. Ownership is normally taken for granted and becomes noticeable mainly when it is altered by experiment or by disease.

## Experimental manipulation
The rubber hand illusion is the standard paradigm. A participant's real hand is hidden while a visible artificial hand is stroked in synchrony with the hidden one. Many participants then report that the artificial hand feels like part of their body, and they tend to mislocate their own hand toward it (Botvinick and Cohen, 1998). The effect weakens with asynchronous stroking, with large spatial offsets, and when the artificial object does not resemble a hand. Related full-body illusions use virtual reality or video to shift the sense of the self toward an avatar or another body.

## Mechanisms and disorders
Tsakiris (2010) proposed that ownership arises when multisensory signals are matched against an internal model of the body, with the model taking precedence over incoming signals only when its content is body-like. Imaging work has implicated premotor cortex, the posterior parietal cortex and the insula. Clinically, loss or distortion of ownership occurs after some right-hemisphere lesions, as in somatoparaphrenia, where a limb is attributed to another person, and in other conditions affecting body awareness.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive-processing accounts treat ownership as the result of inference about the causes of multisensory and interoceptive signals. Apps and Tsakiris (2014) describe self-recognition as the minimisation of prediction error under a model of the body, with illusions arising when synchrony makes the artificial hand the best explanation of the touch. Seth and colleagues extend the account to interoceptive signals and the sense of bodily presence. These are active theoretical programmes with supporting behavioural and some neuroimaging evidence, but they are formulated mostly qualitatively and compete with other models of multisensory integration.
