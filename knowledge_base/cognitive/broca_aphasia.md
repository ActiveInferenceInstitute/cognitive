# Broca Aphasia

## Overview
Broca aphasia, also called expressive or nonfluent aphasia, is a language disorder after brain damage, usually in the left hemisphere, in which speech is slow, effortful and reduced in grammatical structure while comprehension of everyday conversation is relatively good. It is named for Paul Broca, who in 1861 described a patient who could produce almost only the syllable "tan". Examination of the preserved brains of Broca's two original patients showed lesions that extended beyond the cortical area now named after him (Dronkers et al., 2007; see [[brocas_area]]).

## Clinical features
- **Nonfluent output.** Utterances are short, produced with effort and sometimes with disrupted articulation, which can include apraxia of speech.
- **Agrammatism.** Function words and inflections are omitted or misused, giving telegraphic speech such as "walk dog park".
- **Repetition and naming.** Repetition is impaired, and naming difficulties are common, with relative preservation of content words.
- **Comprehension.** Understanding is usually good for simple sentences, but patients may fail on sentences whose meaning depends on syntax, such as reversible passives, where word order must be used to decide who did what to whom.
- **Awareness.** Patients often know their speech is faulty, and frustration or depression can follow. Right-sided weakness often accompanies the left frontal lesion.

## Lesion and interpretation
Persistent Broca aphasia is generally associated with large lesions of the left inferior frontal gyrus, the underlying white matter, the insula and nearby cortex, not with damage to the inferior frontal gyrus alone. Smaller lesions limited to the classical area often produce milder and transient problems. Interpretations of agrammatism differ: some propose a central syntactic deficit, others a limitation of processing resources or of mapping between syntax and thematic roles (see [[syntactic_parsing]] and [[thematic_roles]]). Standard descriptions and tests are given by Goodglass, Kaplan and Barresi (2001).

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no established Active Inference account of Broca aphasia. Hierarchical generative models of speech production and comprehension, in which higher levels predict sequences of words and sounds, offer a general framework in which impaired sequencing or prediction at one level would affect fluency (see [[predictive_processing]] and [[language_comprehension]]). That remains a hypothetical reading, and clinical description and treatment rely on neuropsychological evidence.
