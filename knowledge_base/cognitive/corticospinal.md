# Corticospinal Tract

## Overview
The corticospinal tract is the main descending pathway by which the cerebral cortex influences the spinal cord. Its axons arise from pyramidal cells in layer 5 of primary motor cortex, premotor and supplementary motor areas, and also from parts of somatosensory and parietal cortex. The tract is especially developed in primates and is closely tied to skilled voluntary movement of the hand (Lemon, 2008).

## Course and organisation
Fibres descend through the corona radiata and the posterior limb of the internal capsule, then through the cerebral peduncle and the base of the pons, and form the medullary pyramids. At the pyramidal decussation in the lower medulla most fibres cross and continue as the lateral corticospinal tract, which supplies limb muscles. A smaller uncrossed bundle forms the anterior corticospinal tract, which is associated with axial and proximal muscles. Fibres end in the intermediate zone and ventral horn of the spinal cord. In primates a distinct population makes direct corticomotoneuronal connections onto motor neurons, which supports fractionated movements of individual fingers. Many corticospinal fibres also modulate sensory transmission and spinal interneuron activity, so the tract is not solely a motor command line (Kandel et al., 2021).

## Clinical features
Damage to the pathway, for example from stroke or spinal injury, is associated with weakness, loss of dexterity, increased muscle tone, brisk reflexes and an extensor plantar response. These are grouped as upper motor neuron signs. Recovery depends on the extent of the lesion and on the integrity of the remaining descending pathways.

## Role in Active Inference
Adams, Shipp and Friston (2013) proposed that in Active Inference the descending signals from motor cortex are not commands but predictions of proprioceptive input. Spinal reflex arcs then act to fulfil these predictions, so that movement results from minimising proprioceptive prediction error. The agranular structure of motor cortex, which lacks a classical layer 4, is cited as consistent with a region that emits predictions and receives little ascending error. This is a published theoretical proposal and an active research programme, not a settled account of corticospinal function, and the equivalence with motor command and efference copy accounts is still debated (see [[efference_copy]]).
