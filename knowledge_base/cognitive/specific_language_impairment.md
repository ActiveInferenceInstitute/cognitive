# Specific Language Impairment

## Overview
Specific language impairment (SLI) is a persistent difficulty in acquiring and using spoken language that cannot be attributed to hearing loss, intellectual disability, autism, a motor speech problem or obvious neurological damage, and occurs despite adequate exposure. The label emphasises that nonverbal ability is typically in the normal range. In recent clinical consensus the same population is increasingly described as having developmental language disorder, a broader term that does not require a large gap between language and nonverbal ability. It contrasts with [[aphasia]], where language is lost after having developed normally.

## Features
Children with SLI usually begin to talk late and progress slowly. The profile varies, but common findings include:
- **Grammar**: difficulty with morphology, especially marking of tense and agreement in English, and with complex sentences.
- **Vocabulary**: slow word learning and limited lexical depth.
- **Comprehension and discourse**: weaker understanding of spoken sentences and narratives in many children.
- **Associated skills**: weaker phonological short-term memory, as measured by repeating nonsense words, and a high rate of later reading difficulty (see [[dyslexia]]).

The disorder is heritable, with family aggregation and multiple genetic contributions rather than a single gene. Prevalence in early school years is of the order of a few percent. Support through speech-language therapy and classroom adaptations is standard, and outcomes vary: some children catch up while many continue to show language, literacy or social difficulties into adolescence.

## Accounts
Proposals differ in where the primary deficit lies. Grammar-specific accounts posit a deficit in language-particular knowledge. Processing accounts instead point to limited processing speed, short-term memory capacity, or difficulties with rapid auditory input. Other work suggests weaker statistical or procedural learning of sequential regularities. None of these fully explains the heterogeneity, and many researchers expect several partly overlapping causes.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no established Active Inference account of SLI. A loose connection exists through the claim that language learning involves acquiring a hierarchical generative model of sound and sequence statistics, so that weak statistical learning could slow the acquisition of useful priors. This remains an analogy and a research question, and the disorder is better understood through the developmental and psycholinguistic literature. See [[language_learning]].
