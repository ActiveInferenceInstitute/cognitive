# Ambivalence

## Overview
Ambivalence is the simultaneous presence of positive and negative evaluations, or of conflicting motives, toward the same object, person, goal or option. In attitude research it is separated from indifference. An indifferent person has weak evaluations on both sides, whereas an ambivalent person holds strong positive and strong negative reactions together (Thompson, Zanna & Griffin, 1995). In decision making it appears as difficulty committing to an option that is attractive and costly at once.

## Forms and measurement
- **Potential (objective) ambivalence** is inferred from separate ratings of positive and negative reactions. It is high when both are strong and similar in size.
- **Felt (subjective) ambivalence** is the reported experience of conflict or mixed feeling. The two measures do not always agree, so they are treated as distinct constructs.
- **Mixed emotions** are the affective counterpart. Larsen, McGraw and Cacioppo (2001) reported that people can feel happy and sad at the same time in bittersweet situations, which counts against a strictly bipolar model of affect, although the interpretation remains debated.

## Consequences for choice
Lewin (1935) described approach-avoidance conflict as arising when a single goal has both attractive and repellent aspects. Ambivalent options are commonly associated with slower choices, postponement and greater sensitivity to framing and context. Conflict of this kind is thought to be tracked by medial frontal monitoring systems (see [[conflict_monitoring]] and [[response_conflict]]). The size of these effects depends on the person, the stakes and the way the conflict is measured, so general claims should be treated with caution.

## Role in Active Inference
In discrete-state Active Inference, preferences are prior beliefs over outcomes, and choices follow the expected free energy of policies (see [[prior_beliefs]]). An option that leads to outcomes that are both preferred and dispreferred can have an expected free energy close to that of its alternatives. The posterior over policies then stays broad, and selection is slow or variable, which resembles behavioural indecision. This is an interpretive analogy, not an established model of felt ambivalence, and how mixed feelings should map onto quantities in the generative model is not settled.
