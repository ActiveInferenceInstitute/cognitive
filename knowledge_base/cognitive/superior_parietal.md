# Superior Parietal Lobule

## Overview
The superior parietal lobule lies on the upper surface of the parietal lobe, above the intraparietal sulcus and behind the postcentral gyrus. It corresponds largely to Brodmann areas 5 and 7 and forms part of the posterior parietal cortex. It receives somatosensory input from the anterior parietal cortex and visual input from the dorsal visual pathway, and it projects to premotor and motor regions, which places it at an interface between sensing and acting.

## Functions
- **Spatial attention.** The superior parietal lobule and the neighbouring intraparietal sulcus, together with the frontal eye fields, form the dorsal frontoparietal network associated with voluntary, goal-directed orienting (Corbetta and Shulman, 2002). The network is distinguished from a more ventral system engaged by unexpected, behaviourally relevant stimuli.
- **Visuomotor transformation.** Parietal neurons represent target locations in reference frames suited to reaching and grasping, and combine visual, proprioceptive and motor-related signals (Andersen and Buneo, 2002). Damage to dorsal parietal and parieto-occipital cortex is associated with optic ataxia, an impaired ability to reach to visual targets despite preserved recognition.
- **Manipulation of spatial information.** Imaging studies report activation during mental rotation, spatial working memory and reordering of items held in memory, which suggests a role beyond immediate sensorimotor control.
- **Body representation.** Area 5 contributes to the representation of limb position and posture.

Bilateral lesions of posterior parietal and parieto-occipital cortex can produce Balint syndrome, which combines impaired spatial attention, gaze control and visually guided reaching. The functional boundaries between the superior parietal lobule and the intraparietal sulcus are blurred in much of the literature, so claims about one region often refer to both.

## Relation to Active Inference
In predictive processing accounts, attention is modelled as the optimisation of precision, the confidence assigned to sensory prediction errors (see [[precision_weighting]]). Dorsal frontoparietal regions are a natural candidate for the source of such top-down gain on spatial channels, and parietal visuomotor transformations fit the view that movement fulfils predictions about the body in space. This mapping is an interpretation of imaging and lesion data, not a result derived from the theory.
