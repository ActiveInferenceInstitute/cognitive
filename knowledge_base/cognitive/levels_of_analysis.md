# Levels of Analysis

## Overview
Levels of analysis are distinct kinds of question that can be asked about a system that processes information. The best known scheme is due to Marr (1982), who argued that a complete account of a perceptual or cognitive system needs three descriptions: a computational theory stating what problem is solved and why, an algorithm and representation specifying how, and a physical implementation specifying how this is realised in hardware or neural tissue. The scheme is a framework for organising explanation, not an empirical claim.

## The three levels
Marr's standard illustration is a cash register: at the computational level it performs addition, with properties such as commutativity and associativity that make addition the right operation for totalling a bill; the algorithm could be written in decimal or binary; the implementation could be gears or electronics. In vision, the computational level asks what information about the world can be recovered from images and under what constraints, the algorithmic level proposes representations such as the primal sketch, and the implementational level concerns neurons. Different levels can be studied somewhat separately, though Marr did not claim they are wholly independent.

## Criticisms and alternatives
Critics argue that the levels are less separable than the framework suggests, since knowledge of neural implementation constrains plausible algorithms (Churchland & Sejnowski, 1992). Craver (2007) contrasts Marr's levels, which are levels of description of one system, with levels of mechanism, in which components are nested parts of larger wholes. Newell (1982) proposed a different hierarchy including a knowledge level. Poggio (2012) later argued for extending the framework with levels for learning and evolution. Philosophers still disagree about how far the levels map onto each other or onto reduction.

## Relation to Active Inference
The scheme is routinely used to say what kind of theory the free energy principle is. It is usually placed at the computational level, as a statement of what a self-organising system must do, while predictive coding and variational message passing are proposed algorithmic-level process theories and cortical microcircuits are proposed implementations. This is a way of positioning the theory, and critics contest whether the principle makes empirically distinguishing claims at its level.
