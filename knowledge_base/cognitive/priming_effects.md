# Priming Effects

## Overview
Priming is a change in the speed, accuracy, or bias of responding to a stimulus because of an earlier encounter with the same or a related stimulus, usually without the person intending or remembering the earlier encounter. It is a standard demonstration of implicit memory, and it also functions as a tool for probing unconscious processing ([[implicit_memory]]).

## Types
- **Repetition priming**: faster or more accurate identification of a previously seen item, which can persist for days to months and is thought to rely on perceptual and conceptual representations.
- **Semantic priming**: a word is recognized faster after a related word (doctor, then nurse), first shown in lexical decision by Meyer and Schvaneveldt (1971) and typically explained by spreading activation among related concepts or by overlapping features ([[semantic_memory]]).
- **Masked priming**: primes presented too briefly to be reported can still influence responses. Whether such effects reflect truly unconscious processing depends on rigorous tests of prime visibility ([[consciousness]]).
- **Affective priming**: evaluative congruence between prime and target speeds judgment.
- **Behavioural and social priming**: claims that incidental exposure to concepts changes complex behaviour (for example, that exposure to words related to old age slows walking) were prominent in the 1990s, but several high-profile effects failed to replicate in later large or preregistered attempts. These findings are widely regarded as unreliable, and the contrast with reliable perceptual and semantic priming is important.

**Neural correlates.** Repetition typically reduces activity in the responsive cortex, termed repetition suppression, which accompanies behavioural priming and has been interpreted as neural efficiency, fatigue, or sharpening of representations ([[neural_efficiency]]).

## Role in Active Inference
Predictive coding offers a mechanism-level account of repetition effects. A previously encountered stimulus changes the priors or context for the next one, so that prediction error is reduced, producing both suppressed responses and faster processing. Auksztulewicz and Friston (2016) argued that repetition suppression depends on expectations established by context, which fits studies showing that the effect varies with how probable the repetition is. Semantic priming can be viewed as prior beliefs over related causes being raised by the prime. The framework reproduces these patterns, but other explanations such as adaptation and sharpening also fit much of the data, and the account does not rescue the contested social priming findings.
