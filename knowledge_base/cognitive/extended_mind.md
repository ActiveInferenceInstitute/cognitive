# Extended Mind

## Overview
The extended mind thesis holds that the machinery of cognition can reach beyond the skull and skin to include parts of the environment, such as notebooks, instruments and digital devices, when these play the right functional role. It was proposed by Andy Clark and David Chalmers (1998). The claim concerns the location of cognitive processes and, in its stronger form, of mental states such as beliefs.

## The parity principle and Otto
Clark and Chalmers state a parity principle: if a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognising as cognitive, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process. Their example is Otto, a person with memory impairment who writes information in a notebook he consults reliably. When Otto looks up the address of a museum, the notebook entry plays the role that a stored belief plays for another person, so they argue that Otto believes the museum is on that street. Conditions often invoked are that the resource is reliably available, its content is automatically endorsed, and it is easily accessible. Clark (2008) develops the broader view in Supersizing the Mind.

## Objections
- **Coupling-constitution.** Adams and Aizawa (2001) argue that the thesis confuses causal coupling with constitution. An external resource may causally support cognition without being part of it, and they hold that cognition is marked by distinctive intrinsic, non-derived content.
- **Parity problems.** Critics note that internal and external memory differ in properties such as reliability, format and integration, so the parity argument may overreach.
- **Proliferation.** Without limits, the principle might count a large range of external resources as parts of a mind.
Defenders reply that reliability and integration criteria provide principled limits.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relationship is an active but unsettled debate. Clark has argued that predictive processing supports extended cognition, since agents act to shape their sensory environments in order to reduce prediction error. Critics point out that Active Inference defines an agent by a Markov blanket, a boundary that separates internal from external states, which seems to place cognition inside the boundary (see [[markov_blanket]]). Responses argue that blankets can be nested or can include tools and other agents. There is no consensus on whether the formalism favours extension or internalism.
