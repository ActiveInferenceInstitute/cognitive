# Phobias

## Overview
A phobia is an intense and persistent fear of a particular object or situation, accompanied by avoidance or endurance with distress, that is out of proportion to the actual danger. In the DSM-5 classification, specific phobia is divided by type into animal, natural environment (such as heights), blood-injection-injury, situational (such as flying or enclosed spaces) and other. Social anxiety disorder and agoraphobia are classed as separate conditions. Specific phobias are among the most common anxiety disorders, and many people recognise that their fear is excessive without being able to control it.

## Origins and treatment
- **Conditioning.** Watson and Rayner (1920) reported a classic demonstration in which an infant acquired fear of a neutral object after it was paired with a loud noise. Conditioning is an important route, but it is not the only one, since fears can also be acquired by observing others or by being told about danger.
- **Preparedness.** Seligman (1971) proposed that humans are biologically predisposed to acquire fears of certain stimuli, such as snakes and heights, more readily than others. Evidence for selective, resistant-to-extinction learning is reviewed by Ohman and Mineka (2001), who link it to a fear module centred on the amygdala (see [[amygdala]]).
- **Maintenance.** Avoidance prevents the person from discovering that the feared outcome does not occur, and so sustains the fear.
- **Treatment.** Exposure therapy, in which the person is gradually and repeatedly confronted with the feared stimulus, is the best-supported treatment. It is thought to work by extinction learning, which forms a new inhibitory memory that competes with the original fear memory rather than erasing it, and this explains why fear can return (see [[behavioral_therapy]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
In a predictive framework, a phobia can be described as a strongly held prior that a stimulus predicts danger, together with high expected cost of encountering it. Avoidance is an action policy that keeps the agent from sampling evidence that would update the prior, so the belief is never tested. Exposure supplies disconfirming observations, and extinction corresponds to building a context-dependent belief that the stimulus is safe. This is a conceptual reading consistent with learning theory, not a validated quantitative model of phobia.
