# Joint Action

## Overview
Joint action is any form of social interaction in which two or more individuals coordinate their actions in space and time to bring about a change in the environment. Carrying a table together, playing a duet, shaking hands and conversing are examples. Sebanz, Bekkering and Knoblich (2006) framed the research question as what individuals need to be able to do in order to coordinate, and as how perception, representation and action interact in doing so.

## Cognitive requirements
Sebanz and colleagues identified several ingredients:
- **Joint attention**, which establishes a shared focus on the same object or event (see [[joint_attention]]).
- **Action observation and prediction**, since a partner's action must be anticipated in order to be met in time (see [[goal_inference]]).
- **Task sharing**, in which participants represent the partner's part of the task as well as their own, which is shown in experiments where a partner's response affects one's own reaction time even when it is irrelevant.
- **Action coordination**, which uses mechanisms such as entrainment, and agents may make their own movements easier to predict, for instance by exaggerating them, a behaviour called signalling or sensorimotor communication.

## Philosophical analysis
Bratman (1992) described shared cooperative activity as requiring mutual responsiveness, commitment to the joint activity, and commitment to mutual support. His analysis separates joint action from mere coordinated individual actions that happen to coincide, such as people walking in the same direction on a pavement. Whether the shared intentions required are special group-level states or can be reduced to individual attitudes is debated among philosophers.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an active modelling programme. Friston and Frith (2015) simulated two agents who each hold a generative model of the other and show how synchronisation can emerge between them, with a model of communication built on one agent inferring the other's intentions from the signals they produce. In this view, joint action is an inference problem on a shared generative model, with each agent selecting actions that make its own behaviour predictable. The simulations use simple scenarios, such as birdsong-like exchanges, and empirical tests with human dyads are only starting (see [[multi_agent_active_inference]]).
