# Perspective Taking

## Overview
Perspective taking is the ability to represent the world from a viewpoint other than one's own. The term covers visual or spatial perspective taking (what another person can see, and how a scene looks from where they stand), cognitive perspective taking (what they believe or know), and affective perspective taking (what they feel). It is closely related to [[theory_of_mind]] and to empathy.

## Mechanism
Developmental work distinguishes levels. Flavell described Level 1 perspective taking (knowing that another person can or cannot see an object) emerging in early childhood, and Level 2 (knowing how an object looks from another's position) emerging later. Piaget's three-mountains task was long read as showing early egocentrism in young children, though subsequent work shows that task demands partly drive the poor performance.

In adults, perspective taking is not automatic and costs effort. Samson and colleagues' dot-perspective task showed that the number of dots another person can see interferes with one's own judgement, taken as evidence of rapid, involuntary computation of what others see, although later work has argued that some of this interference reflects attentional cuing rather than mental-state attribution. Epley and colleagues proposed that people anchor on their own perspective and adjust insufficiently, which explains common egocentric biases. Imaging studies implicate the temporoparietal junction and [[inferior_parietal]] cortex, the [[medial_prefrontal]] cortex, and for spatial rotation of the self, parietal and vestibular-related regions. Perspective taking draws on [[inhibitory_control]] to suppress one's own view, and on [[working_memory]].

## Role in Active Inference
In an Active Inference view, adopting another's perspective means running a generative model of the world conditioned on the other's position or beliefs, and inferring what observations they would receive and what they would infer from them. Egocentric anchoring corresponds to using one's own beliefs as the default prior for the other's model, with adjustment as belief updating that costs effort; the failure to inhibit the self-perspective is a precision problem ([[precision_weighting]]). Accounts of interpersonal inference and of shared generative models in dyads provide the formal context ([[multi_agent_active_inference]]). These links are conceptual: there is little direct Active Inference modelling of standard perspective-taking tasks.
