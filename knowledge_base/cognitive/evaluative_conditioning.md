# Evaluative Conditioning

## Overview
Evaluative conditioning is a change in the liking of a stimulus that results from pairing it with another stimulus that is already liked or disliked. A neutral image shown repeatedly beside pleasant pictures tends to be rated as more positive afterwards, and the same image paired with unpleasant pictures tends to be rated as more negative. The effect is usually measured with explicit ratings or with indirect measures of attitude, and it is treated as one route by which preferences and attitudes are formed.

## Key findings
Meta-analytic work (Hofmann et al., 2010) reports a reliable effect of moderate size across many studies. Several features distinguish it from the classical conditioning of fear (see [[fear_conditioning]]):
- Effects can persist when the learner does not remember which stimuli were paired, and are sometimes reported without awareness of the contingency. Whether awareness is required remains debated.
- The effect is often reported to be slow to extinguish when the pairing ceases, although results vary across paradigms.
- It differs from signal learning, in which a conditioned stimulus acquires the power to predict an outcome. The liking changes even where the first stimulus predicts nothing useful about the second.

## Theoretical accounts
Associative accounts hold that pairing forms a link between the representations of the two stimuli so that activating one activates the valence of the other. Propositional accounts hold that learners form and evaluate beliefs about the relation between stimuli, and that the effect depends on the relation inferred, not only on contiguity. De Houwer, Thomas and Baeyens (2001) reviewed the first twenty-five years of evidence and argued that evaluative conditioning is a distinct phenomenon from Pavlovian signal learning. Evidence for and against each account remains mixed.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an analogy. Valence is treated in Active Inference as part of prior preferences over outcomes, and evaluative conditioning can be read as a shift in the preferred or aversive status of a stimulus through its association with other stimuli whose value is already encoded. This reading has not been developed into a specific model or tested against the competing associative and propositional accounts.
