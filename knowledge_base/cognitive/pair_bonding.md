# Pair Bonding

## Overview
Pair bonding is the formation of a selective and lasting social attachment between two individuals, typically mates. It is characterised by a preference for the partner over unfamiliar individuals, proximity seeking, distress on separation and, in some species, shared care of offspring. Pair bonds are found in a minority of mammal species and in many birds, and they have evolved independently several times.

## Neurobiological model
The best-studied model is the prairie vole (*Microtus ochrogaster*), a monogamous rodent. Mating and cohabitation lead to a partner preference, and manipulations of neuropeptides alter its formation. Work reviewed by Young and Wang (2004) implicates oxytocin in females and vasopressin in males, acting together with dopamine signalling in the reward circuitry of the ventral forebrain, notably the nucleus accumbens ([[oxytocin]], [[vasopressin]]). Administration of vasopressin to male prairie voles promoted partner preference without mating, while an antagonist blocked it (Winslow et al., 1993). Closely related promiscuous species such as montane voles show different receptor distributions, which is one reason the system is used for comparative work. The proposed mechanism is that mating events associate the sensory identity of the partner with a rewarding state, so that the partner acquires lasting incentive value (see [[social_reward]]).

## Humans
Romantic attachment in humans engages reward-related and social-cognitive regions in imaging studies, and oxytocin and vasopressin are measurable in association with affiliative behaviour. Evidence that results from voles transfer directly to humans is limited, and human pair bonding is shaped by culture, language and individual history. Claims about a single hormonal mechanism of love should be treated with caution.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an analogy at present, with some proposals. A partner can be described as a source of highly predictable, mutually adaptive sensory input, and a bond as the coupling of two agents' generative models so that each predicts the other's behaviour. Friston and Frith (2015) formalised such coupling in a simulation of two agents that come to share a communication code, although their example concerned birdsong rather than mating. How neuropeptide effects on bonding map onto variables such as precision or expected free energy has not been established.
