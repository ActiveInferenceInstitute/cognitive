# Wh-Movement

## Overview
Wh-movement is the process, in generative syntax, by which a question word such as "what", "who" or "where" appears at the front of a clause even though it is interpreted in a position further inside. In "What did Mary buy?", "what" is understood as the object of "buy", and the sentence is analysed as having the wh-phrase moved from the object position to the clause-initial position, leaving a gap, trace or copy behind. The same operation is used in relative clauses ("the book that Mary bought") and in other constructions. The term comes from the English question words, most of which begin with "wh".

## Properties
- **Filler-gap dependency.** The fronted phrase (the filler) and the position where it is interpreted (the gap) can be separated by many words and by embedded clauses ("What did John say that Mary bought?").
- **Island constraints.** Ross (1967) observed that wh-phrases cannot be extracted from certain structures, which he called islands, including complex noun phrases, coordinate structures and embedded questions. "*What did you wonder whether Mary bought?" is unacceptable. Chomsky's later work, such as subjacency, tried to capture these restrictions in terms of the number of boundaries crossed.
- **Cross-linguistic variation.** English fronts one wh-phrase obligatorily. Chinese and Japanese leave question words in place (wh-in-situ), and some languages front all wh-phrases in multiple questions. Whether in-situ languages involve covert movement is debated.
- **Analyses.** Chomsky (1977) analysed movement to a clause-initial position as a general operation. Other frameworks describe the same dependencies without movement, using feature passing or constraints.

## Processing and Active Inference
The dependency has a clear cost in comprehension: the listener must hold the filler in memory until a gap is found, and difficulty rises with the distance and the number of intervening referents (Gibson, 1998). Evidence suggests that parsers actively predict gap positions after a wh-word, which treats the filler as a source of expectation about upcoming structure. This predictive view fits the general Active Inference and predictive processing picture of language comprehension, in which the parser forms and updates beliefs about structure. The match is conceptual, and no model has derived the island constraints from free energy principles.
