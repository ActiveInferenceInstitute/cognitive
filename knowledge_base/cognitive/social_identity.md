# Social Identity

## Overview
Social identity is the portion of an individual's self-concept that derives from knowledge of belonging to a social group, together with the value and emotional significance attached to that membership (Tajfel, 1981). Social identity theory, developed by Tajfel and Turner (1979), proposed that people strive for a positive self-concept and so seek positive distinctiveness for the groups to which they belong, through comparison with other groups.

## Core ideas
- **Categorisation.** People sort themselves and others into categories such as nationality, profession or team, which accentuates similarities within and differences between groups.
- **Identification.** People take on the identity of the groups they belong to and adopt their norms and goals.
- **Comparison.** Groups are evaluated against other groups, and favourable results support self-esteem. Minimal-group experiments found that even arbitrary assignment to groups can lead to in-group favouritism.
- **Self-categorization theory.** Turner and colleagues (1987) added that the level at which people define themselves shifts between personal and group identity depending on context, and that when a group identity is salient, people act in terms of group-based norms. This is sometimes called depersonalisation, though it does not mean loss of self.

Social identity helps explain in-group bias, collective action, stereotyping and the influence of group norms. It is contested at points: critics note that the evidence for self-esteem as the motive behind in-group favouritism is mixed, and that individual and cultural differences moderate the effects. Its relation to the philosophical question of what makes a person the same over time is limited; that is treated under [[personal_identity]].

## Relation to Active Inference
Accounts in the Active Inference tradition describe social identity as membership in a community whose members share a generative model and therefore make mutually predictable behaviour possible; identification reduces uncertainty about what to expect of others and what to do (Veissière et al., 2020). This is a theoretical proposal with some support from modelling of cultural learning, and is not an established derivation of the empirical findings of social identity theory.
