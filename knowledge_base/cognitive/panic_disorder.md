# Panic Disorder

## Overview
Panic disorder is diagnosed when a person has recurrent unexpected panic attacks, followed by at least a month of persistent concern about further attacks or their consequences, or by a significant maladaptive change in behaviour related to them. A panic attack is an abrupt surge of intense fear or discomfort that peaks within minutes and is accompanied by several physical and cognitive symptoms, such as palpitations, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, chest discomfort, dizziness, and fear of losing control or dying (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Many people have an occasional attack without developing the disorder.

## Mechanisms and treatment
- **Cognitive model.** Clark (1986) proposed that panic arises from the catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations, for example reading a racing heart as a sign of an impending heart attack, which raises anxiety, intensifies the sensations and creates a vicious circle.
- **Interoceptive conditioning and sensitivity.** Learned fear responses to internal bodily cues, and heightened sensitivity to such cues, are also thought to contribute, and the disorder often leads to avoidance of situations such as exercise or crowded places, which may develop into agoraphobia.
- **Neural findings.** Imaging implicates the amygdala, insula and brainstem structures, but findings are heterogeneous (see Paulus & Stein, 2006, for the insula).
- **Treatment.** Cognitive behavioural therapy, which includes education, cognitive restructuring and deliberate exposure to feared bodily sensations, and treatment with serotonergic antidepressants, both have good evidence of benefit (Barlow, 2002).

## Relation to Active Inference
The cognitive model has a natural interpretation in interoceptive inference. Paulus and Stein (2006) described anxiety as arising from an exaggerated mismatch between predicted and actual bodily states processed in the insula, and Seth (2013) developed the more general proposal that emotion arises from inference about the causes of interoceptive signals. In this reading, panic involves a high-precision prior that bodily changes signal danger, so that ambiguous signals are explained by a catastrophic cause and the resulting fear then produces further signals that confirm the belief. Therapy aims to revise this prior through disconfirming experience. The account is theoretical and consistent with the clinical picture, but it remains a hypothesis awaiting direct tests.
