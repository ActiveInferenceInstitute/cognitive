# Stimulus-Response

## Overview
A stimulus-response (S-R) analysis explains behaviour by relating it to the stimulus conditions that precede or accompany it. In its simplest form it describes reflexes and fixed action patterns, in which a defined stimulus reliably elicits a defined response. In learning theory it also describes acquired associations between stimuli and responses, as in Thorndike's law of effect, which grew out of his puzzle-box studies (Thorndike, 1898) and holds that responses followed by satisfying outcomes in a given situation become more likely to recur in that situation.

## Traditions
- **Behaviourism.** Watson, Hull and others sought to explain behaviour with S-R connections, without appeal to unobservable mental states. Skinner (1938) distinguished respondent behaviour, elicited by stimuli, from operant behaviour, emitted and controlled by its consequences, and treated stimuli as setting the occasion for responses (see [[operant_conditioning]]).
- **Ethology.** Tinbergen (1951) described innate releasing mechanisms, in which simple stimuli, such as the red spot on a gull's bill, trigger species-typical responses. Such signs of stimulus-based control are now seen as often depending on internal state, such as hunger or hormonal condition.
- **Insect behaviour.** Many collective behaviours of social insects can be described as responses to local stimuli. An ant encountering a pheromone trail follows it with a probability that depends on its concentration, and the aggregate pattern emerges without a global plan (see [[trail_pheromones]]). Response thresholds that differ between workers can explain division of labour.

## Limits
Pure S-R accounts were criticised for failing to explain latent learning, the effects of expectation, and flexible, goal-directed behaviour. Tolman's cognitive maps and later work on goal-directed versus habitual control showed that behaviour can be sensitive to the current value of outcomes. Modern views accordingly treat S-R habit as one control system alongside model-based planning, with the balance depending on experience.

## Relation to Active Inference
S-R behaviour resembles a fixed habit, in which a policy is selected with little deliberation given a cue. In Active Inference, habits correspond to strong learned priors over policies, whereas goal-directed behaviour involves evaluating expected free energy under a generative model (see [[automaticity]]). Simple insect behaviours are sometimes modelled in this framework, but whether they require a model of their own is debated; the S-R description can often be recast as inference without implying that the animal represents it.
