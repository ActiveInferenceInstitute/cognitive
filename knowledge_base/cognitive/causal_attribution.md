# Causal Attribution

## Overview
Causal attribution is the process by which people infer the causes of events, in particular of their own and other people's behaviour. Attribution theory began with Heider (1958), who described people as naive scientists who seek stable explanations to make the world predictable. The central distinction is between internal (dispositional) causes, such as traits, abilities or intentions, and external (situational) causes, such as circumstances, luck or the task.

## Theories
- **Correspondent inference.** Jones and Davis proposed that observers infer a disposition from an action when it was freely chosen, and when it has distinctive consequences that other options lack.
- **Covariation model.** Kelley (1973) proposed that, like an analysis of variance, observers consider consensus (do others act the same way), distinctiveness (does the person act this way in other situations) and consistency (over time), and assign the cause to the person, the stimulus or the circumstances accordingly. When there is only one observation, observers use causal schemata, such as discounting a cause when another plausible cause is present.
- **Achievement attribution.** Weiner (1985) classified explanations for success and failure by locus, stability and controllability, and linked them to emotions and expectations.

## Biases
Observers tend to underweight situational influences on others' behaviour, a pattern called the fundamental attribution error or correspondence bias (Ross, 1977). Actors and observers often differ, with actors citing the situation and observers the actor's disposition, though the evidence is mixed. The self-serving bias refers to taking credit for success and blaming circumstances for failure. The size and cross-cultural generality of these patterns varies across studies, and some researchers explain them as reasonable inferences under incomplete information.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is conceptual. Attribution is an inference about hidden causes of observed behaviour, which a Bayesian observer could carry out by weighing hypotheses about dispositions and situations against the evidence, and covariation information has a Bayesian reading (see [[causal_inference]] and [[goal_inference]]). Active Inference extends this to social settings by modelling other agents as having beliefs and preferences that must be inferred (see [[theory_of_mind]]). It also treats the sense of agency as an inference about whether one's own actions caused sensory events (see [[agency]]). These are modelling frameworks, and human attributions often depart from normative inference.
