# Social Comparison

## Overview
Social comparison is the process of evaluating oneself by comparing one's abilities, opinions, attributes or outcomes with those of other people. Festinger (1954) proposed that people have a drive to evaluate themselves accurately and that, where objective standards are unavailable, they use others as a yardstick, preferring to compare with similar others. The theory became one of the central frameworks in social psychology for explaining self-evaluation.

## Forms and effects
- **Upward comparison** targets people who are better off or more capable. It can motivate by suggesting what is attainable, or discourage by highlighting a shortfall.
- **Downward comparison** targets people who are worse off. Wills (1981) argued that it can protect or raise subjective well-being, especially under threat.
- **Lateral comparison** with similar others tends to provide the most diagnostic information.

Whether a comparison produces assimilation (feeling closer to the target) or contrast (feeling further from it) depends on factors such as how attainable the standard seems and how relevant the dimension is to the self. Suls, Martin and Wheeler (2002) organised these questions as why people compare, with whom, and with what effect. Comparison has also been studied in relation to envy, pride, and mood, and, in recent research, to use of social media, where findings on well-being are mixed and effect sizes small.

## Neural correlates
Neuroimaging studies have associated comparison of outcomes with activity in reward-related regions such as the ventral striatum, which responds to relative as well as absolute gains, and in medial prefrontal regions involved in self-referential thought. These findings are consistent with comparison engaging reward and self-processing circuits, though interpretation differs between studies.

## Relation to Active Inference
Social comparison can be interpreted as using other agents as a source of evidence about one's own parameters, such as ability, in a generative model where those parameters are uncertain. The link is an analogy: it follows from the general idea that agents infer their own properties from observations, and no standard Active Inference model of Festinger's theory exists.
