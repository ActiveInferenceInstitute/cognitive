# Onset and Rime

## Overview
Many phonological analyses divide a syllable into two constituents: the onset, which is the consonant or consonants before the vowel, and the rime (also spelled rhyme), which consists of the vowel nucleus and any following consonants, the coda. In the word "stamp", the onset is /st/ and the rime is /amp/. The division is intermediate between the syllable and the single phoneme (see [[syllable_structure]]), and it matches the units that matter for poetic rhyme, where words share a rime, and for alliteration, where words share an onset.

## Evidence
Several lines of evidence support the units as psychologically real. In word games and sound-manipulation tasks, speakers more readily split a syllable between onset and rime than elsewhere, and novel word games that break the syllable at the onset-rime boundary are learned more easily than those that break it elsewhere (Treiman, 1983). Phonotactic restrictions are stronger between the nucleus and coda than between onset and nucleus, as shown in analyses of English syllables (Kessler & Treiman, 1997). In reading development, young children often detect shared rimes before they can isolate single phonemes, and sensitivity to rimes has been linked to later reading progress (Goswami & Bryant, 1990). In English, rime units are also more consistent in their spelling-to-sound correspondences than single vowels are.

## Contested points
Not all phonologists accept a hierarchical syllable. Flat models treat the syllable as a sequence of segments, and the evidence for onset-rime constituents varies across languages and tasks. The claim that rime-based units are the natural beginning of decoding is also qualified: phoneme awareness is a stronger predictor of reading in many studies, and the importance of rimes appears to depend on the orthography and on instruction.
