# Spotlight

## Overview
The spotlight metaphor describes visual attention as a beam that can be moved across the visual field, enhancing processing of whatever falls within it. Stimuli inside the beam are detected and discriminated faster and more accurately, while those outside are processed less well. The metaphor is one of the most widely used pictures of selective attention, and it has been refined, challenged and in part replaced.

## Evidence
Posner (1980) used cues that indicated where a target was likely to appear. Targets at the cued location were detected faster than those at uncued locations, and the effect occurred while the eyes stayed fixed, which established that attention can shift covertly, independent of gaze (see [[covert_attention]]). Invalid cues produced costs and valid cues produced benefits relative to a neutral cue. Behavioural work suggests that the focus can move between locations, and that attention is often directed to a region around the cued point.

## Variants and challenges
- **Zoom lens.** Eriksen and St. James (1986) proposed that the attended area can be widened or narrowed, with processing resources spread more thinly when it is large, so that focus trades off against extent.
- **Object-based attention.** Attention can spread across a whole object, including parts outside the nominal spotlight, so the unit of selection is not only a region of space (see [[object_based_attention]]).
- **Multiple foci.** Evidence suggests that attention can sometimes be divided between non-contiguous locations, which is difficult for a single beam.
- **Feature-based attention.** Selection by colour or motion acts across the field, not in one place.
- **Neural effects.** Attention raises firing rates, reduces variability and sharpens tuning for neurons whose receptive fields overlap the attended location, and the effect is considered by Carrasco (2011) in a broad review of the field.

## Relation to Active Inference
In predictive-processing models, attention is identified with the optimisation of precision, the weight placed on prediction errors from a given source. A spotlight corresponds to high precision at a selected location, and Feldman and Friston (2010) showed that this idea reproduces the facilitation by valid cues and the cost of invalid cues in a Posner-type task. This is a model-level account with a computational simulation, and the neural mapping of precision to attentional gain is still being tested.
