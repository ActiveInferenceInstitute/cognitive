# Voluntary Action

## Overview
A voluntary action is one that an agent initiates in accordance with its own intention, as opposed to a reflex or a response driven directly by an external stimulus. Neuroscience asks how such actions are generated, when and how they are experienced as willed, and which brain systems select between alternatives. The issue of whether this makes human behaviour free in a philosophical sense is a separate question (see [[free_will]]).

## Key findings
- **Readiness potential.** Self-paced movements are preceded by a slowly rising negative scalp potential. Libet and colleagues (1983) asked participants to report the time at which they first felt the urge to move, and found that the potential began several hundred milliseconds before the reported time of awareness. Libet interpreted this as showing that the brain initiates voluntary acts before conscious intention. The interpretation is disputed. An accumulator model proposes that the potential reflects fluctuations in ongoing neural activity that, when they cross a threshold, trigger movement, and not a decision already made (Schurger, Sitt and Dehaene, 2012). Reports of the time of awareness are also open to criticism on methodological grounds.
- **Intentional binding.** The interval between a voluntary action and its sensory consequence is perceived as shorter than the same interval for a passive movement or an involuntary one, which is taken as an implicit marker of the sense of agency (Haggard, Clark and Kalogeras, 2002).
- **Neural systems.** Self-initiated action engages the supplementary and pre-supplementary motor areas, premotor and prefrontal cortex, parietal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex and the basal ganglia. Stimulation of medial frontal regions in people can evoke an urge to move, and damage can reduce spontaneous movement (see [[supplementary_motor]]). Haggard (2008) reviewed this work as the neuroscience of volition.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference treats action as part of inference: policies are selected according to expected free energy, and movement results when descending proprioceptive predictions are fulfilled (see [[policy_selection]]). Voluntary action in this sense is behaviour driven by an agent's own prior preferences. The sense of agency has been related to the precision of predictions about the sensory consequences of action (Friston, 2012). These accounts offer a formal vocabulary for volition and are still being tested against experimental data, including the readiness potential findings.
