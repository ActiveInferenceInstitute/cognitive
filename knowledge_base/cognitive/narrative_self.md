# Narrative Self

## Overview
The narrative self is the self as it is presented and understood through a story: an account of who one has been, who one is and where one is going, built from remembered episodes and interpreted in terms of motives, relationships and values. Gallagher (2000) contrasts it with the immediate [[minimal_self]], describing it as extended in time and dependent on memory and language. Philosophers and psychologists differ over whether such stories merely describe a self or help constitute it.

## Psychological research
- **Autobiographical memory.** The narrative self relies on [[autobiographical_memory]], including episodic memories and general knowledge about one's life periods (see [[episodic_memory]]).
- **Life-story research.** McAdams (2001) treats identity in adulthood as an internalised, evolving life story with recurring themes, such as redemption or contamination sequences, that are linked to well-being in some studies.
- **Development.** Narrative abilities develop through childhood and adolescence, alongside language and social understanding, and are shaped by the way caregivers and culture talk about the past.
- **Disruption.** Conditions affecting memory or the sense of continuity alter the narrative self, though the relation to the minimal self varies (see [[amnesia]]).

## Philosophical positions
Dennett described the self as a centre of narrative gravity, a useful abstraction generated by the stories an organism tells, comparable to a centre of gravity in physics. Others, including Schechtman, defend narrative accounts of personal identity (see [[personal_identity]]). Strawson (2004) argued against the claim that self-experience must be narrative, since many people do not experience their lives as a story, and the view that a good life requires one. The debate separates a descriptive claim, that people construct narratives, from normative ones.

## Relation to Active Inference
Some authors describe the self as a hierarchical, temporally deep model that represents the agent's states and policies over long timescales, with higher levels encoding stable self-related expectations. A narrative could then be a high-level prior that organises the interpretation of events. This is an emerging theoretical proposal, closer to analogy than derivation, and few empirical tests exist.
