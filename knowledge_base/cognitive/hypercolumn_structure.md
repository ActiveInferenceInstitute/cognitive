# Hypercolumn Structure

## Overview
A hypercolumn is a unit of organisation in primary visual cortex (V1) proposed by Hubel and Wiesel. It groups the cortical columns needed to analyse one small region of the visual field: a full set of orientation columns, covering the range of preferred orientations, together with the ocular dominance columns for the two eyes. Neighbouring hypercolumns then represent neighbouring locations, so that the cortical sheet tiles visual space with a repeating, roughly uniform machinery.

## Columns and maps
Neurons recorded along a path perpendicular to the cortical surface in cat and macaque V1 tend to share a preferred stimulus orientation, whereas penetrations parallel to the surface show orientation preference changing gradually. Alternating bands of cortex respond preferentially to input from the left or right eye. Hubel and Wiesel (1977) described how these two systems intersect across the surface of macaque cortex. Cytochrome oxidase blobs, identified separately, are associated with colour-related processing. Position in the visual field is mapped retinotopically, with more cortex devoted to central vision (cortical magnification), so a hypercolumn covers a smaller visual angle near the fovea than in the periphery.

## Evidence and limits
Optical imaging later showed orientation preference arranged as smooth maps containing pinwheel points, around which all orientations converge, rather than as sharply bounded slabs. Some species lack the columnar layout while still having orientation-selective neurons: in rodents, neighbouring neurons often differ in preference ("salt and pepper" organisation). This indicates that columns are not required for orientation tuning, and the functional significance of columnar organisation, as opposed to being a by-product of development and wiring economy, remains debated. The hypercolumn is therefore best treated as a useful description of the primate and carnivore cortex, not a universal computational module.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an interpretive one. Predictive coding schemes place populations of prediction and error units in cortical columns, and canonical microcircuit proposals map their message passing onto laminar connectivity (Bastos et al., 2012). A repeating hypercolumn structure fits this picture, since each unit would hold local estimates for one position, but the anatomical mapping is hypothesised and not established.
