# Open Monitoring

## Overview
Open monitoring is a category of meditation practice in which the practitioner stays receptive to whatever arises from moment to moment, such as sensations, thoughts and emotions, without selecting or dwelling on any one of them. In the taxonomy proposed by Lutz and colleagues (2008) it is contrasted with focused-attention practice, in which attention is held on a chosen object such as the breath. Practices described as mindfulness, choiceless awareness or open presence are commonly placed in this category, although the traditions that teach them differ in aims and vocabulary.

## Features
Descriptions of the practice usually include several components:
- **Non-reactivity**: noticing experience without evaluating it or acting on it.
- **Meta-awareness**: monitoring the quality of one's own mental state, for example noticing that attention has drifted.
- **Reduced object-fixation**: attention is not captured by a single stimulus, so the breadth of awareness is widened.

The two styles are not strictly separate. Many training programmes begin with focused attention to stabilise attention and then move to open monitoring, and some skills overlap.

## Empirical findings
Studies compare practitioners and novices using behavioural tasks, electrophysiology and neuroimaging. Reviews report differences in activity of regions involved in attention regulation and in self-referential processing, and suggest that the two styles load different mechanisms (Lutz et al., 2008; Dahl, Lutz, & Davidson, 2015). Findings are heterogeneous, samples are often small, and the definition of the practice varies across studies, so conclusions about specific neural signatures should be treated as provisional.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is a theoretical proposal, not an established result. Predictive accounts of meditation suggest that focused attention increases the precision of sensory evidence at a chosen target, while open monitoring reduces the precision of deeply held priors so that the practitioner can observe thoughts and sensations as events rather than act on them. Laukkonen and Slagter (2021) develop this idea as a hypothesis about the plasticity of the predictive mind. Predictions about attention and precision follow from the framework (see [[precision_weighting]] and [[predictive_processing]]), but direct tests remain limited.
