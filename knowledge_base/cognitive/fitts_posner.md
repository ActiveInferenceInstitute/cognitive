# Fitts and Posner Stages of Skill Acquisition

## Overview
Fitts and Posner (1967) proposed that the learning of a skill passes through three overlapping phases, now usually called the cognitive, associative and autonomous stages. Developed in the context of human performance research, the scheme has become a standard description of how people move from effortful, error-prone attempts to fluent performance, and it is widely used in teaching, coaching and rehabilitation. The stages describe tendencies and are not sharply bounded.

## The three stages
- **Cognitive stage.** The learner works out what the task requires. Performance depends on verbal instructions, demonstration and deliberate attention, is slow and variable, and contains many gross errors. Improvement is rapid. Strategies are tried and discarded.
- **Associative stage.** The learner links the appropriate actions to the relevant cues and refines them. Errors become fewer and smaller, performance becomes more consistent, and less verbal mediation is needed. This stage may last a long time.
- **Autonomous stage.** The skill requires little attention and can be carried out alongside other activities. Improvement is slow, performance is stable, and the learner is more resistant to interference. This corresponds to the properties described in [[automaticity]]. Extended practice may still improve performance.

Anderson (1982) developed a closely parallel account for cognitive skills, with a declarative stage, a knowledge-compilation stage and a procedural stage, in which declarative knowledge is converted into procedures.

## Evidence and criticism
The stage idea is supported by changes in attentional demand, variability and the dominance of verbal control during practice, and by neuroimaging findings that activity shifts from prefrontal regions towards motor and striatal areas with learning. Critics note that the model is descriptive and does not specify mechanisms, that stage boundaries are hard to identify, and that learning curves are often smooth and not step-like. Task type matters, so the sequence may differ for discrete and continuous skills.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is an analogy. The progression can be redescribed as a model becoming more precise, with prediction errors shrinking as the generative model of the task improves, and as the policy being stored as an empirical prior (compare habit learning under [[automaticity]]). No Active Inference account separately derives the three stages.
