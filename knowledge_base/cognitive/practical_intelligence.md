# Practical Intelligence

## Overview
Practical intelligence is the ability to apply knowledge and skills to the demands of everyday life, such as managing a job, dealing with other people and choosing a workable solution among imperfect options. The term is most closely associated with Robert Sternberg, who proposed it as one of three aspects of intelligence in his triarchic theory (Sternberg, 1985). The other two are analytical intelligence, used in abstract problem solving and standard tests, and creative intelligence, used in coping with novelty.

## Definition and tacit knowledge
In the triarchic account, practical intelligence involves adapting to an environment, shaping it or selecting a new one, in order to achieve one's goals. Its central empirical construct is tacit knowledge, which Wagner and Sternberg (1985) defined as action-oriented knowledge usually acquired without direct instruction and not easily put into words, such as how to deal with a difficult supervisor. They measured it with situational judgement tests in which participants rate courses of action in realistic work scenarios, and they reported that scores related to job performance in several occupations, with only modest links to scores on conventional intelligence tests. Sternberg and colleagues (1995) summarised this research and argued that practical intelligence is distinct from academic intelligence.

## Criticism
The construct has been criticised on empirical and conceptual grounds. Gottfredson (2003) argued that the evidence for practical intelligence as a distinct ability is weaker than claimed, that tacit-knowledge measures relate to general mental ability and to experience, and that the theory is not clearly separated from general cognitive ability. Defenders reply that conventional tests sample a narrow range of skills. The question remains contested, and a reader should treat strong claims in either direction with care. The topic is related to, but not the same as, the distinction between [[fluid_intelligence]] and [[crystallized_intelligence]] in psychometric models.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no established formal link. By analogy, practical competence could be described as having a generative model adapted to a particular niche, built by accumulated experience and used to select actions that satisfy goals under uncertainty. That description resembles the notion of [[adaptive_expertise]] and the view that expert behaviour reflects well-tuned priors, but it is a loose analogy and has not been tested as a theory of practical intelligence.
