# Duncker Problem

## Overview
The term refers to problems introduced by the Gestalt psychologist Karl Duncker in his 1945 monograph On Problem-Solving. Two are best known. The candle problem, the one most often meant, asks participants to attach a lit candle to a wall so that wax does not drip on the table, given a candle, matches and a box of tacks. The radiation problem asks how a tumour can be destroyed with rays without harming the surrounding healthy tissue.

## The candle problem
The solution is to empty the box, tack it to the wall and stand the candle in it as a platform. Participants often fail because they see the box only as a container for the tacks, a phenomenon Duncker called functional fixedness: an object's familiar function makes alternative uses harder to perceive. In his experiments, performance improved when the box was presented empty, so that it was no longer in use as a container. The task has become a standard demonstration of how mental set impedes restructuring of a problem, which is central to insight research.

## The radiation problem
A ray strong enough to destroy the tumour would also destroy tissue it passes through, while a weaker ray would leave the tumour intact. The solution is to direct several weak rays from different directions so that they converge on the tumour. Gick and Holyoak (1980) used it to study analogical transfer: participants given an analogous story about an army dividing its forces to converge on a fortress were far more likely to find the solution, but typically only after being told to use the story, which showed that spontaneous retrieval of analogies is difficult.

## Interpretation
Gestalt theory attributed insight to the restructuring of how a problem is represented. Later accounts emphasise relaxing overly constraining assumptions and the role of prior knowledge. Some replications of the candle problem with incentives and presentation changes have produced mixed results, so claims about the effects of specific manipulations should be treated cautiously.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct treatment of Duncker's problems in Active Inference. A loose parallel treats functional fixedness as a strong prior over an object's affordances that must be revised for the problem to be solved. This is a suggestion and not an established model.
