# Alzheimer's Disease

## Overview
Alzheimer's disease is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia in older adults. It was first described by Alois Alzheimer in 1906 from the case of a patient with memory loss and behavioural change whose brain showed plaques and tangles. The typical course is a gradual decline in memory and other cognitive abilities that eventually impairs independent living. A rarer early-onset form is in some families associated with inherited mutations, for example in the APP, PSEN1 or PSEN2 genes.

## Pathology
Two lesions define the disease at autopsy: extracellular plaques composed of aggregated amyloid-beta peptide, and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. Neuronal and synaptic loss accompany them, with brain atrophy and inflammation. Braak and Braak described a staging of tangle pathology that begins in the transentorhinal and entorhinal cortex, spreads to the hippocampus and later reaches the neocortex. The amyloid cascade hypothesis, formulated by Hardy and Higgins, proposes that amyloid deposition is the initiating event. It has been influential but also challenged, since amyloid burden correlates only partly with cognitive decline, and tau pathology and synaptic failure track symptoms more closely.

## Clinical features
The earliest and most characteristic symptom is impaired learning of new episodic information, which reflects early damage in medial temporal structures (see [[episodic_memory]] and [[hippocampus]]). As the disease advances, semantic memory, language, visuospatial skills, judgement and executive function decline, and spatial disorientation is common. Procedural memory is relatively preserved until later. Diagnosis combines clinical assessment with imaging and biomarkers in cerebrospinal fluid or blood, and treatments currently available mainly address symptoms or, in some cases, aim to reduce amyloid.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is exploratory. Computational work has proposed that cognitive decline can be modelled as impaired inference, for instance through degraded precision, loss of synaptic gain or disconnection between hierarchical levels, and that spatial disorientation reflects damage to entorhinal systems supporting spatial models. Such models can reproduce some behavioural patterns, but no account in this framework has been established as an explanation of the disease, and the pathology itself is described in molecular terms.
