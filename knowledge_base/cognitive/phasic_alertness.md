# Phasic Alertness

## Overview
Phasic alertness is a transient state of heightened readiness to respond that is triggered by a warning signal or other cue announcing that a target is about to occur. It is distinguished from tonic alertness, the slowly varying general level of arousal that fluctuates over minutes to hours and is the subject of vigilance research ([[vigilance]], [[sustained_attention]]). In the scheme of Posner and Petersen (1990) it is the function of the alerting network, one of three attentional networks.

## Measurement
In warning-signal tasks, a cue such as a tone precedes a target by a short interval, and responses are compared with trials without a cue. A warning typically shortens reaction time ([[reaction_time]]) once the interval exceeds a few tens of milliseconds, and the benefit depends on the interval between cue and target. It can come with an increase in errors if responses are made before the stimulus has been fully processed, a pattern described as a speed-accuracy trade-off. The attention network test of Fan and colleagues (2002) estimates alerting efficiency by subtracting reaction times on trials with a warning cue from those without one. Posner (2008) reviews the measure and its limits.

## Neural basis
The alerting network is associated with the thalamus and with right frontal and parietal regions, and its modulation is attributed principally to norepinephrine released from the locus coeruleus ([[locus_coeruleus]]). Phasic activity of this nucleus occurs in response to salient and task-relevant events, and Aston-Jones and Cohen (2005) proposed that it facilitates task-relevant processing and behavioural responses. Pharmacological studies that block noradrenergic transmission tend to reduce the alerting effect, though the evidence is not uniform across paradigms. Alerting is partly independent of orienting ([[orienting_network]]), although the networks interact.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an interpretation in terms of uncertainty and precision. Yu and Dayan (2005) proposed that noradrenaline signals unexpected uncertainty, meaning a violation of the current context that calls for a revision of beliefs, whereas acetylcholine signals expected uncertainty. In the predictive-processing literature, neuromodulators are likewise tied to the precision assigned to prediction errors, so a phasic noradrenergic burst could raise the gain on incoming evidence in preparation for a response (see [[precision_weighting]] and [[neuromodulation]]). This proposal is plausible and widely discussed, but it is not an established derivation of the alerting effect.
