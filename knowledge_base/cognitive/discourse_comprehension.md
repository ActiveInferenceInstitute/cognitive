# Discourse Comprehension

## Overview
Discourse comprehension is the understanding of language units larger than a sentence, such as stories, explanations, conversations and expository texts. Comprehension requires more than decoding individual sentences: a comprehender must link ideas across sentences, resolve references, draw inferences and integrate the content with prior knowledge to form a coherent representation of what the discourse is about.

## Levels of representation
The classic framework of van Dijk and Kintsch (1983) distinguishes three levels. The **surface form** keeps the exact wording and is lost quickly. The **textbase** consists of the propositions explicitly expressed and the connections among them. The **situation model** represents the described state of affairs, including people, places, time, causes and goals, and integrates it with world knowledge. Situation models are updated when the text shifts along these dimensions, and they better predict long-term memory than surface form (Zwaan & Radvansky, 1998).

## Processes and models
- **Reference resolution.** Pronouns and other anaphors are linked to earlier entities.
- **Inference generation.** Bridging inferences that connect statements are made routinely, whereas elaborative inferences that go beyond the text are made less consistently.
- **Coherence monitoring.** Readers detect and try to repair inconsistencies.
- **Construction-integration.** Kintsch (1988) proposed that comprehension first activates a loosely constrained network of concepts and propositions from text and knowledge, and a settling process then strengthens coherent elements and suppresses irrelevant ones.

Working memory limits and reading skill affect these operations, and impairments of discourse processing are reported after right-hemisphere damage and in several developmental and acquired conditions, though patterns vary between individuals.

## Role in Active Inference
Prediction-based accounts of language comprehension propose that upcoming content is anticipated at several levels, and mismatches such as the N400 response reflect the cost of integrating unexpected input (Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016). This fits a hierarchical generative model with situation models at the top. It is an active research programme, but formal Active Inference models of extended discourse are limited, and debate continues about how much anticipation is routine.
