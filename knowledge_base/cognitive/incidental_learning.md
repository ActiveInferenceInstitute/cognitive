# Incidental Learning

## Overview
Incidental learning is learning that takes place without a deliberate intention to learn and without instructions to remember. A person who is asked to rate words for pleasantness and is then unexpectedly tested on them is an example. It is contrasted with intentional learning, in which the learner knows that a memory test will follow and may use strategies such as rehearsal.

## Experimental approach
The standard method is the orienting-task paradigm. Participants perform a task on a list of items, for example judging the pleasantness of each word, counting the letters it contains or checking its first letter, with no warning of a later memory test. A group of intentional learners is told to learn the words. Hyde and Jenkins (1969) reported that recall following some incidental orienting tasks, particularly those involving meaning such as rating pleasantness, was about as high as recall by intentional learners, whereas tasks directing attention to letters produced lower recall. The result indicated that what matters for retention is the processing carried out, and not the intention to learn.

## Depth of processing
Craik and Lockhart (1972) generalised this pattern in the levels-of-processing framework, in which retention depends on the depth of analysis: semantic processing yields more durable traces than shallow processing of physical or phonological features (see [[levels_of_processing]]). The framework has been criticised for circularity in defining depth, and later work emphasised the match between encoding and retrieval, but the basic advantage for semantic encoding is robust. Incidental learning can therefore be strong when the task requires meaningful engagement, and weak when attention is placed on irrelevant features.

## Related forms
Incidental learning overlaps with implicit learning, such as the acquisition of artificial grammar rules without awareness (Reber, 1967), and with statistical learning of regularities in speech or visual scenes (see [[statistical_learning]]). Incidental refers to the absence of intent; implicit refers to the absence of awareness of what was learned.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is general. Active Inference models treat learning of model parameters as a continuous by-product of inference, which proceeds whenever observations are processed and does not require an intention to learn. Regularities that improve prediction are incorporated as a consequence. This is a conceptual correspondence and not a specific model of orienting-task effects.
