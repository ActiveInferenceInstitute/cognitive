# Meditation

## Overview
Meditation refers to a diverse family of practices, with roots in several contemplative traditions, that train attention, awareness and emotional regulation. In cognitive science it is studied as a set of mental skills rather than a belief system. Lutz and colleagues (2008) distinguish two broad styles. In focused attention practice, the practitioner sustains attention on a chosen object such as the breath, notices when the mind wanders, and returns to the object. In open monitoring practice, the practitioner attends to the flow of experience as it arises, without fixing on any one object. Other practices cultivate compassion or kindness toward oneself and others.

## Effects and evidence
- **Attention.** Studies report improvements in sustained attention and reduced attentional lapses with training, along with changes in how attention is allocated to successive stimuli.
- **Emotion and stress.** Mindfulness-based programmes, notably Kabat-Zinn's mindfulness-based stress reduction, are used to reduce stress, and its derivative mindfulness-based cognitive therapy is used to prevent relapse in recurrent depression, with modest benefits reported in several trials.
- **Brain activity and structure.** Neuroimaging links practice to changes in regions involved in attention control, interoception and self-referential processing, including the anterior cingulate, insula and default mode network (Tang, Holzel, & Posner, 2015).
- **Limits of the evidence.** Many studies have small samples, rely on self-selected practitioners, lack active control conditions, and use varying definitions of the practice, so effect sizes are uncertain and long-term effects are less well established. Adverse experiences are also reported by some practitioners and are under-studied.

## Relation to Active Inference
Several predictive-processing accounts have been offered. In one reading, focused attention practice involves repeatedly increasing the precision assigned to a chosen sensory channel while down-weighting the prediction errors generated by wandering thought, with noticing mind-wandering as a metacognitive inference that the current attentional policy has failed. Open monitoring has been linked to flattening high-level priors so that experience is less constrained by habitual interpretation (compare [[psychedelics]]). These proposals suit the phenomenology and the attention-regulation literature, but they are theoretical interpretations with limited direct testing.
