# Anterograde Amnesia

## Overview
Anterograde amnesia is the inability to form new lasting memories for events and facts encountered after the onset of the causative injury or disease, while the ability to recall events from before onset may be partly or fully preserved. A person with a severe form can talk coherently and follow an immediate conversation, yet minutes later cannot remember the conversation, the people met or the tasks done. It is the core deficit in many cases of amnesia (see [[amnesia]]).

## Causes
Typical causes are bilateral damage to the hippocampus and surrounding medial temporal structures, or to diencephalic structures such as the mammillary bodies and thalamus. Specific aetiologies include herpes simplex encephalitis, anoxia, stroke, head trauma, Korsakoff syndrome and surgical resection. Transient anterograde amnesia occurs after high doses of some sedatives and during alcohol-related blackouts, and during an episode of transient global amnesia.

## Preserved and impaired abilities
The pattern of what remains intact is more informative than the loss itself.
- **Preserved:** immediate or short-term memory span, language, general knowledge acquired before onset, and nondeclarative memory. Corkin showed that H.M. improved at a mirror-tracing motor task across days without remembering previous sessions.
- **Priming:** Warrington and Weiskrantz found that amnesic patients showed normal facilitation of word completion by recently seen words, even when they could not recognise the words as familiar.
- **Impaired:** explicit recall and recognition of new events, which depend on episodic memory, and often the learning of new facts (see [[episodic_memory]] and [[implicit_memory]]).

This dissociation is one of the main sources of evidence for multiple memory systems and for a role of the hippocampus in forming and consolidating declarative memories (see [[memory_consolidation]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is an interpretive link. In generative-model terms, anterograde amnesia is a failure to update the long-term parameters of the model, and so to incorporate new episodes, while the current inference of hidden states in working memory proceeds normally. The hippocampus is also proposed to support construction of scenes and sequences, which would explain related deficits in imagination. These accounts redescribe the established data, and no quantitative model in the framework has yet replaced standard neuropsychological accounts.
