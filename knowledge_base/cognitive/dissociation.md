# Dissociation

## Overview
Dissociation refers to a disruption or discontinuity in the usual integration of consciousness, memory, identity, emotion, perception, body representation, motor control and behaviour. The term covers a spectrum. At one end are common, benign experiences such as absorption in a book or highway hypnosis. At the other are dissociative disorders, which cause distress or impairment.

## Forms
In the DSM-5-TR, dissociative disorders include dissociative amnesia (inability to recall important autobiographical information, beyond ordinary forgetting), dissociative identity disorder (disruption of identity with distinct self-states), and depersonalization/derealization disorder. Symptoms are often described as negative, such as loss of memory or function, or positive, such as intrusions into awareness. Dissociation is associated with trauma and stress in many studies, though the direction and nature of causal relations are debated, and dissociative symptoms occur in other conditions as well. This entry is descriptive and not clinical guidance.

## Historical and conceptual accounts
Pierre Janet proposed in the late nineteenth century that overwhelming experiences could fail to be integrated into a unified personal consciousness, leaving fragments split off. Hilgard's neodissociation theory described hypnosis in terms of divided control within a hierarchy of cognitive systems. Holmes and colleagues (2005) argued that the term mixes two phenomena: detachment, as in depersonalization, and compartmentalisation, as in deficits in voluntarily controllable functions. The validity and prevalence of some diagnoses remain contested.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive-processing work has proposed that some dissociative and functional symptoms arise when imprecise or aberrantly weighted beliefs fail to be revised, or when attention reduces the precision of sensory evidence. Edwards and colleagues (2012) gave a Bayesian account of functional motor symptoms, and Seth and colleagues applied interoceptive inference to depersonalization. These are active research programmes with limited direct evidence, not accepted explanations.
