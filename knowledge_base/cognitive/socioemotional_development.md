# Socioemotional Development

## Overview
Socioemotional development is the process by which children come to experience, express, understand and manage emotions and to form relationships with others. It brings together emotional and social growth, because emotions are communicated within relationships and relationships in turn shape how emotions are regulated. It overlaps with [[social_development]], from which it differs mainly in its emphasis on emotion.

## Components
- **Temperament.** Individual differences in reactivity and self-regulation that are evident early and shape how a child responds to caregiving.
- **Attachment.** The emotional bond to caregivers that supports comfort and exploration (see [[secure_base]]).
- **Emotional competence.** Saarni (1999) described skills including awareness of one's own emotions, understanding of others' emotions, using emotion vocabulary, empathic involvement and capacity for emotional self-efficacy.
- **Emotion regulation.** Thompson (1994) defined it as the processes involved in monitoring, evaluating and modifying emotional reactions to achieve goals. In early childhood, regulation is largely supplied by caregivers through soothing and then gradually internalised, with strategies such as attention shifting and cognitive reappraisal appearing later.
- **Identity and psychosocial tasks.** Erikson (1950) proposed a sequence of stages, from basic trust in infancy to identity formation in adolescence, each with a characteristic psychosocial challenge. His stage model is influential but is treated as a descriptive framework with limited empirical support for strict stage sequences.

## Influences and outcomes
Development is influenced by caregiving, family climate, peer relationships, culture and biological factors. Children with stronger emotion regulation and emotional understanding tend, on average, to show better peer relationships and school adjustment, although these are associations and causal direction is often unclear. Atypical development may be seen in a range of conditions; assessment and support are matters for qualified professionals.

## Relation to Active Inference
In interoceptive-inference accounts, emotion arises from inference about the causes of bodily states, and regulation by caregivers can be seen as external help in reducing the uncertainty a child cannot yet manage alone. This is an interpretive link between developmental theory and a theoretical framework and does not yet have a robust empirical test.
