# Bistable Perception

## Overview
Bistable perception is the spontaneous alternation between two distinct interpretations of a single ambiguous stimulus. In the Necker cube a line drawing is seen as a cube with one face toward the viewer, then as a cube with the other face forward. Other familiar examples are Rubin's vase-face figure and the rotating-cylinder illusion. The physical input is constant, so changes in experience reflect processing of the stimulus and not changes in the stimulus. Bistability is one case of the broader class of multistable phenomena that includes binocular rivalry (see [[binocular_rivalry]]).

## Properties
At any moment observers perceive one interpretation, not a blend, and perception switches without voluntary control, although attention and intention can bias the durations. Dominance durations vary from one episode to the next, with a skewed, roughly gamma-like distribution, and successive durations are nearly independent. Leopold and Logothetis (1999) argued that similar dynamics across different kinds of ambiguity point to shared principles. Prolonged viewing can slow or change switching, and intermittent presentation stabilises one interpretation for long periods, evidence that memory of the recent percept influences perception. Prior experience and context also bias which interpretation comes first.

## Neural correlates
Imaging shows that activity in visual areas specific to the competing interpretations follows the reported percept. Sterzer, Kleinschmidt and Rees (2009) review evidence that frontoparietal regions are active around switches, which has led to debate over whether switches are triggered by higher-level areas or arise from local competition and adaptation in sensory cortex. Both bottom-up and top-down contributions are probably involved.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an established interpretation within the predictive coding tradition. Perception is described as selecting the hypothesis that best explains the sensory input. When two hypotheses explain it about equally well, the system cannot settle, and as prediction error accumulates against the current hypothesis the other becomes favoured, producing alternation. Models of this kind reproduce qualitative features such as the distribution of dominance durations, but they compete with models based on mutual inhibition and neural adaptation, and the data do not yet separate them.
