# Consciousness Studies

## Overview
Consciousness studies is the interdisciplinary research field, spanning neuroscience, psychology, philosophy, and computer science, that investigates subjective experience and its relation to the brain and other physical systems. This page maps the field's methods and the landscape of theories; the concepts themselves are covered in [[consciousness]], [[consciousness_and_awareness]], and [[consciousness_theory]].

## Mechanism
**Core distinctions.** Block separated phenomenal consciousness (what it is like) from access consciousness (availability for reasoning, report, and control). Chalmers distinguished the "easy problems" of explaining functions from the "hard problem" of explaining experience. Researchers also separate the level of consciousness (wakefulness, anaesthesia, disorders of consciousness) from its content (what one is conscious of).

**Methods.** The central empirical strategy is the contrastive method: compare brain activity when a stimulus is consciously perceived against matched trials where it is not, using masking, binocular rivalry, attentional blink ([[attentional_blink]]), or blindsight-type conditions. The target is the neural correlates of consciousness (NCC), defined by Crick and Koch, and reviewed by Koch and colleagues, as the minimal neural mechanisms jointly sufficient for a specific experience. Because reporting a percept confounds consciousness with cognitive processes of report, no-report paradigms try to infer awareness from reflexive indicators. Clinical measures, for example responses to perturbation of cortex, are used with unresponsive patients.

**Theories.** Major families include workspace theories ([[global_workspace]]), integrated information ([[integrated_information]]), recurrent processing, higher-order theories, and predictive-processing and Active Inference-based accounts. Adversarial collaborations, in which proponents of rival theories agree in advance on predictions and a design, were introduced to make progress testable.

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference contributes a family of accounts in which conscious experience is related to inference in deep, hierarchical generative models, with proposals tying it to temporal depth, to precision-weighted selection of beliefs, and to self-modelling and interoceptive inference. Seth, Hohwy, and others have developed predictive-processing approaches, and Whyte and Smith have combined workspace theory with Active Inference. These are active research programmes; the framework is not, as a whole, a validated theory of consciousness, and its relation to the hard problem is disputed. See [[predictive_processing]] and [[self_awareness]].
