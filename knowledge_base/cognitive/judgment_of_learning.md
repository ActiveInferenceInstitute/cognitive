# Judgment of Learning

## Overview
A judgment of learning (JOL) is a prediction, made during or shortly after study, of the likelihood that an item will be remembered on a later test. JOLs are a core measure in research on metamemory. Ratings are compared with later test performance. Accuracy is commonly assessed by relative accuracy, the within-person correlation between ratings and recall, and by calibration, the match between average prediction and average performance.

## Framework
Nelson and Narens (1990) proposed that cognition has an object level and a meta level, linked by monitoring (information flowing up from the object level) and control (instructions flowing down) (see [[nelson_narens]]). JOLs are a form of monitoring made during acquisition, and they inform control decisions such as which items to restudy, how long to study them, and when to stop. Learners generally allocate more time to items they judge as less well learned.

## Accuracy and the delayed-JOL effect
Immediate JOLs, made right after studying an item, are often only modestly accurate. Nelson and Dunlosky (1991) reported the delayed-JOL effect: when the judgment is made some time after study and the cue alone is presented, prediction of later cued recall becomes much more accurate. A common explanation is that delayed judgments require attempted retrieval from long-term memory and so draw on diagnostic information, whereas immediate judgments are influenced by information still held in working memory.

## Cue utilisation
Koriat (1997) proposed that JOLs are inferential, based on cues such as the ease with which an item is processed, its apparent familiarity, or the perceived difficulty of the material. Cues related to the intrinsic properties of the items, to the encoding operations carried out, and to the learner's beliefs all contribute. Because some cues are not diagnostic of memory, such as the font size of a word, people can show illusions of competence, in which fluent processing during study is mistaken for durable learning.

## Relation to Active Inference
This relation is an analogy. A JOL can be described as a belief about the precision of a future retrieval, which is a second-order inference in a hierarchical model of one's own memory, and control of study can be framed as choosing actions that reduce expected uncertainty about the material (see [[metacognition]]). No Active Inference account of the delayed-JOL effect or of cue utilisation has been developed.
