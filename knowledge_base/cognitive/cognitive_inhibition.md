# Cognitive Inhibition

## Overview
Cognitive inhibition is the set of processes that suppress, restrain or filter information that is irrelevant to current goals, such as distracting stimuli, unwanted memories, outdated information in working memory and intrusive thoughts. It is distinguished from response inhibition, the stopping of motor actions, which is covered under [[inhibitory_control]]. Inhibition is one of the executive functions that are widely studied, though its status as a single unified function is contested (see [[executive_functions]]).

## Forms and measures
- **Interference control.** Resolving conflict from competing stimuli or responses, measured with the Stroop task and flanker tasks.
- **Prepotent response inhibition.** Overriding a dominant response, measured with the antisaccade task.
- **Removal from working memory.** Discarding information that is no longer relevant, measured with tasks such as directed forgetting and negative priming.
- **Memory suppression.** In the think/no-think paradigm, instructing people not to think about a memory reduces later recall of it, which Anderson and Green (2001) interpreted as executive control over retrieval (see [[thought_suppression]]).

## Theories
Hasher and Zacks (1988) proposed that inhibitory mechanisms control access to working memory, restrict what is activated and suppress what has become irrelevant, and that reduced inhibition accounts for age and individual differences in comprehension and memory. A latent-variable study found that resistance to distractor interference and inhibition of prepotent responses were related abilities, whereas resistance to proactive interference was separable from them, so inhibition is not a single unitary ability (Friedman and Miyake, 2004). Some theorists question whether active suppression is needed at all, proposing that competition and selection alone can explain the effects. Inhibitory deficits are discussed in ageing, ADHD and schizophrenia, with results varying by task (see [[adhd]] and [[schizophrenia]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an interpretation. In predictive processing, attention is described as the optimisation of precision, the gain applied to prediction errors, and suppression of irrelevant input corresponds to reducing the precision of the signals or the beliefs associated with them (see [[precision_weighting]] and [[attention_mechanisms]]). Selecting among competing policies can be read as inhibition of alternatives through the posterior over policies. These parallels are conceptual, and they do not show that inhibition tasks are reducible to precision control.
