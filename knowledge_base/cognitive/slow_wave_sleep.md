# Slow Wave Sleep

## Overview
Slow wave sleep (SWS), also called deep sleep or stage N3, is the stage of non-REM sleep in which the cortical EEG is dominated by high-amplitude, low-frequency activity, with delta waves in the range of about 0.5 to 4 Hz. Arousal thresholds are highest and dreaming reports are least frequent compared with REM sleep. It is concentrated in the first half of the night and declines with age.

## Oscillations and consolidation
- **Slow oscillation.** Cortical neurons alternate between a depolarised up state of firing and a hyperpolarised down state of silence at roughly one cycle per second or slower, producing the large slow waves seen on EEG.
- **Nested rhythms.** Thalamocortical spindles at around 11 to 16 Hz and hippocampal sharp-wave ripples tend to occur coupled to the slow oscillation. This coordination is thought to carry hippocampal replay of recent experience to the neocortex.
- **Active systems consolidation.** The hypothesis that reactivation during SWS gradually shifts memories from hippocampal to neocortical stores is supported by studies in humans and rodents, including manipulations that boost slow oscillations or cue reactivation (Diekelmann & Born, 2010).
- **Synaptic homeostasis.** The synaptic homeostasis hypothesis proposes that slow waves drive net down-scaling of synaptic strengths that accumulated during waking, restoring capacity and improving signal-to-noise (Tononi & Cirelli, 2014). The two accounts are compatible but differ in emphasis and are both still under test.

## Relation to Active Inference
Hobson and Friston (2012) suggested that non-REM sleep serves to reduce the complexity of the brain's generative model. In Bayesian terms this resembles Bayesian model reduction, in which redundant parameters are removed after learning so as to increase model evidence without new data. Synaptic down-scaling during SWS provides a candidate physiological implementation. The proposal is theoretical and is one reading of the empirical findings, not a demonstrated mechanism; see also [[friston_2017_curiosity]] for related treatments of insight and model reduction.
