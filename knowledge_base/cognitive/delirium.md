# Delirium

## Overview
Delirium is an acute neuropsychiatric syndrome of disturbed attention and awareness. Its core features in the DSM-5 are a reduced ability to direct, focus, sustain and shift attention, reduced orientation to the environment, development over a short period (usually hours to days), a change from baseline, and a tendency to fluctuate in severity during the day. Additional cognitive disturbance, such as impaired memory, disorientation, language or visuospatial deficits, or perceptual disturbance, is also present. The disturbance must not be better explained by a pre-existing neurocognitive disorder and must have evidence of a physiological cause.

## Presentation and course
Three motor subtypes are commonly described. Hyperactive delirium involves agitation and restlessness, hypoactive delirium involves withdrawal and reduced responsiveness, and a mixed form alternates between them. The hypoactive form is easily missed. Hallucinations, delusions, sleep-wake disruption and emotional lability are frequent. Delirium is common in hospitalised older adults and after surgery, and is associated with worse outcomes (Inouye, Westendorp and Saczynski, 2014). Predisposing factors include advanced age, dementia, sensory impairment and frailty. Precipitating factors include infection, medications, metabolic disturbance, withdrawal from alcohol or sedatives, and pain. Management centres on identifying and treating the cause and on supportive, non-pharmacological care.

## Proposed mechanisms
No single mechanism is established. Hypotheses include cholinergic deficiency, excess dopaminergic activity, neuroinflammation, impaired oxidative metabolism and disrupted connectivity between large-scale brain networks, probably acting in combination. These factors may explain why delirium often occurs in people with underlying dementia, and why it can accelerate cognitive decline.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is speculative. A predictive-processing reading treats delirium as a failure to maintain stable hierarchical beliefs and to weight sensory evidence appropriately, with hallucinations and misperceptions arising when priors and prediction errors are mis-weighted. This is consistent with the prominence of cholinergic and dopaminergic hypotheses, because these systems are proposed to encode precision. However, there are few formal models or experimental tests, and the account is better regarded as an analogy that organises existing observations than as an established theory.
