# Social Attention

## Overview
Social attention is the selective processing of social stimuli and the use of social cues to direct attention. It includes orienting to faces and eyes, following another person's gaze to the same object, and monitoring what others are attending to. Gaze is an especially informative cue because it signals a person's focus, interest and likely next action, and humans are sensitive to eye direction from early infancy.

## Phenomena
- **Gaze cueing.** An image of a face looking left or right shifts attention toward the gazed-at side, speeding detection of targets there, even when the gaze direction does not predict target location (Friesen & Kingstone, 1998). The effect arises quickly, and its degree of automaticity is debated, since it is modulated by context and by the observer's beliefs about the gazer. Frischen and colleagues (2007) reviewed these effects and their dependence on individual differences.
- **Preferential orienting.** Faces and eyes tend to capture and hold gaze in scene viewing, although the strength of the bias depends on the task and on cultural and individual factors.
- **Joint attention.** Two individuals attend to the same object and are mutually aware of doing so, a capacity that develops in infancy and is related to language learning.

## Neural basis
Regions linked to social attention include the superior temporal sulcus, the temporoparietal junction, the amygdala and the intraparietal cortex. Neurons in the macaque temporal cortex respond to gaze and head direction. Reduced orienting to eyes or faces is reported in autism, though findings vary across tasks and ages (Emery, 2000, reviews the primate and human evidence on gaze).

## Relation to Active Inference
In Active Inference, attention to another agent can be understood as sampling the cues that most reduce uncertainty about that agent's goals. Theoretical treatments of communication and joint action propose that interacting agents infer one another's generative models and may converge on a shared one, as in the "duet for one" proposal of Friston and Frith (2015). This is a research programme with simulations; it has been less tested against behavioural data on gaze cueing than the standard accounts.
