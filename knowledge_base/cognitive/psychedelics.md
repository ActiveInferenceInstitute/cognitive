# Psychedelics

## Overview
Psychedelics are a class of psychoactive substances that produce marked changes in perception, mood, thought and the sense of self. The classic serotonergic psychedelics include psilocybin, lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), mescaline and dimethyltryptamine. Other drug classes, such as dissociative anaesthetics, produce overlapping but distinct effects and are not covered here.

## Pharmacology and effects
- **Receptor action.** The classic psychedelics act as agonists at the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor, and blocking this receptor attenuates their subjective effects (Nichols, 2016). The receptor is densely expressed on cortical pyramidal neurons.
- **Subjective effects.** Reported effects include altered visual perception, changes in the experience of time, intensified emotion, and in some cases a dissolution of the boundary between self and world, which varies strongly with dose, setting and individual.
- **Brain activity.** Neuroimaging with psilocybin found decreased activity and altered coupling in hub regions of the default mode network, in contrast to what a simple excitation account would predict (Carhart-Harris et al., 2012). Later work reports increased global functional connectivity and a less segregated network organisation.
- **Clinical research.** Trials examine psilocybin and related compounds for depression and other conditions. Results are still being evaluated, trials are small and difficult to blind, and risks include acute distress and, for people vulnerable to psychosis, adverse outcomes.

## Relation to Active Inference
Carhart-Harris and Friston (2019) proposed the REBUS model ("relaxed beliefs under psychedelics"). It holds that psychedelics reduce the precision of high-level prior beliefs, so that bottom-up signals carry more weight in perceptual and cognitive inference, which is expressed as a flattening of the brain's free-energy landscape and a loosening of rigid priors. The idea ties a pharmacological mechanism to the precision-weighting machinery of predictive processing, and it motivates the suggestion that therapeutic effects might involve revising entrenched beliefs. It is an influential hypothesis that has found partial support from neuroimaging, but it is not settled, and competing accounts of psychedelic action exist.
