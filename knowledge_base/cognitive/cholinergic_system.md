# Cholinergic System

## Overview
The cholinergic system comprises neurons that release acetylcholine (ACh) and the receptors that respond to it. In the brain, projection neurons in the basal forebrain, including the nucleus basalis of Meynert and medial septum, innervate cortex and hippocampus, while brainstem cholinergic nuclei innervate thalamus and other targets. Outside the brain, motor neurons release ACh at the neuromuscular junction to activate skeletal muscle, and the parasympathetic nervous system uses it as a transmitter. Mesulam's work mapped these projections.

## Receptors and actions
- **Nicotinic receptors** are ligand-gated ion channels producing fast excitation, and mediate transmission at the neuromuscular junction.
- **Muscarinic receptors** (M1 to M5) are G-protein-coupled and modulate neuronal excitability and synaptic transmission more slowly.

ACh acts as a neuromodulator, changing how cortical neurons respond rather than carrying specific content. Reviews associate it with attention, arousal and learning. Sarter and colleagues (2005) described cholinergic input as supporting detection of signals and the allocation of attention to cue-relevant inputs. Hasselmo (2006) proposed that high ACh levels during active waking favour encoding by enhancing afferent input and suppressing recurrent excitation, whereas low levels during quiet waking and slow-wave sleep favour consolidation; this is a hypothesis with experimental support. Cholinergic loss is prominent in Alzheimer's disease, and the system is a pharmacological target, although clinical questions lie outside this page.

## Role in Active Inference
Yu and Dayan (2005) proposed that ACh signals expected uncertainty, the unreliability of top-down predictions, while norepinephrine signals unexpected uncertainty. In the Active Inference literature, acetylcholine has been associated with the precision of sensory prediction errors, so that higher levels increase the weight of bottom-up evidence relative to priors (Moran et al., 2013; Feldman and Friston, 2010). Moran and colleagues tested this with pharmacological manipulation and modelling of cholinergic effects on responses. The assignment of neuromodulators to specific precisions is a hypothesis under active investigation; competing assignments (for example dopamine to policy precision) and alternative non-Bayesian descriptions of ACh exist, and the human evidence is limited in size.
