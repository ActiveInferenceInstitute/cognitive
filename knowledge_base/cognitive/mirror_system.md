# Mirror System

## Overview
The mirror system is a network of neurons that are active both when an individual performs an action and when the individual observes another performing a similar action. Mirror neurons were first recorded in area F5 of the premotor cortex of macaque monkeys, where a neuron that fired when the animal grasped an object also fired when it watched an experimenter grasp an object (di Pellegrino et al., 1992; Gallese et al., 1996). Similar responses were later found in parietal cortex, and the circuit is often called the parieto-frontal mirror system.

## Evidence in humans
Single-neuron recordings in humans are rare, so most evidence comes from imaging, transcranial magnetic stimulation and electrophysiology. These studies show that regions of the inferior frontal gyrus and inferior parietal lobule, together with premotor cortex, are engaged by both action execution and observation, and that observing a movement can modulate the excitability of the observer's motor system (Rizzolatti & Craighero, 2004). Such overlap is also seen for some sensations and emotions, in regions such as the insula. Because direct recordings are scarce, the idea that human responses arise from neurons with the same properties as monkey F5 neurons rests on inference.

## Proposed functions and controversy
The mirror system has been proposed to support action understanding, imitation, learning from others, empathy and even the evolution of language. These claims are disputed. Critics note that macaques show little imitation, that understanding an action can be preserved when motor areas are damaged, and that mirror responses could reflect associations learned through experience or the later activation of motor representations after the action has been recognised (Hickok, 2009). Mirror neurons are therefore best regarded as one element of how the brain processes observed action, not a complete explanation of social cognition (see [[social_cognition]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
Kilner, Friston and Frith (2007) argued that mirror responses follow from predictive coding: observing an action engages a hierarchical generative model whose levels represent kinematics, goals and intentions, and the system infers the cause of the observed movement by minimising prediction error. On this view, mirror activity reflects inference rather than a special set of neurons for matching, and action understanding is a Bayesian inversion of the same model used to generate action. This is an active research programme, with support from modelling and imaging, but it does not decisively separate the predictive account from alternatives.
