# Iconic Memory

## Overview
Iconic memory is the visual form of sensory memory: a very short-lived store that keeps a detailed, precategorical trace of what was just seen. The term was introduced by Neisser (1967). Its capacity is large relative to later stages, but its contents fade within roughly a second, and are quickly overwritten by new input. It is usually placed at the earliest stage of models of [[memory_organization]], before attention selects material for [[working_memory]].

## Sperling's experiments
Sperling (1960) briefly flashed arrays of letters, for example three rows of four, for about 50 milliseconds. In whole report, observers could name only about four or five items, which is close to the span of immediate memory. In partial report, a tone shortly after offset cued one row to be reported, and performance for that row was high, implying that most of the array was available at the time of the cue. When the cue was delayed by a second or so, performance fell towards the whole-report level. The result showed a fast-decaying store with more information than can be reported before it fades.

Later work distinguished several components. Visible persistence is the phenomenal afterimage-like continuation of a stimulus, and it shortens as stimulus duration or brightness increases, whereas informational persistence, the availability of visual information for report, does not behave in the same way (Coltheart, 1980). Masking by a following pattern erases the trace, which indicates that the store is vulnerable to interference. Whether a separate storage buffer exists, or whether the effect arises from ordinary visual processing and persistence in early visual cortex, has been debated. The auditory counterpart is called echoic memory.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no specific Active Inference theory of iconic memory, and the phenomenon was characterised well before predictive-processing frameworks. One tentative framing is that a brief store of recent sensory input provides the evidence over which fast, low levels of a hierarchical generative model operate, so that higher levels can update beliefs on a slower timescale. Models with temporal depth in perception, and the observation that cueing selects a subset of retained information, fit the picture qualitatively. The framing is an analogy, not a derivation, and it adds little predictive detail beyond classical accounts. See [[visual_perception]] and [[attention_mechanisms]].
