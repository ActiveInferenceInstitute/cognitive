# Appraisal

## Overview
Appraisal is the process of evaluating an event, object or situation with respect to its significance for the individual's goals, values and wellbeing. Appraisal theories of emotion hold that emotions are elicited and differentiated by such evaluations, not by events alone, so that the same event can lead to different emotions in different people. Arnold and Lazarus developed the idea in the mid twentieth century, and Scherer, Smith and Ellsworth, and others elaborated it.

## Main accounts
- **Lazarus.** Lazarus (1991) distinguished primary appraisal, which asks whether something is relevant to one's wellbeing and whether it is a threat, harm or challenge, from secondary appraisal, which asks about coping options (see [[primary_appraisal]] and [[secondary_appraisal]]). Stress arises when demands are judged to exceed resources (see [[stress_response]]).
- **Component process model.** Scherer proposed that appraisal proceeds through a sequence of checks, for example novelty, intrinsic pleasantness, goal relevance, coping potential and normative significance, and that the outcome patterns the response in several components (Scherer et al., 2001).
- **Dimensional theories.** Others describe appraisal along dimensions such as valence, certainty, control and agency, which map different emotions, for instance anger and sadness, to different profiles.

## Issues
Appraisal need not be conscious or deliberate, and critics note that appraisals may follow rather than cause emotion, or that the notion becomes circular when appraisals are inferred from the emotion they are meant to explain. Empirical work using self-report and manipulation of appraisals supports a causal contribution, and reinterpretation of a situation is a core emotion regulation strategy (see [[cognitive_reappraisal]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is a developing theoretical programme. Appraisal can be framed as inference about the implications of sensed events for goals encoded as prior preferences, with certainty, control and agency corresponding to beliefs about the precision of outcomes and policies (see [[precision_weighting]] and [[agency]]). Joffily and Coricelli (2013) proposed that emotional valence reflects the rate of change of free energy over time, and deep active inference models of affect extend that line (see [[hesp_2021]]). These proposals offer a formal vocabulary for appraisal dimensions but have not displaced established appraisal theories.
