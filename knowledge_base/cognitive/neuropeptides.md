# Neuropeptides

## Overview
Neuropeptides are small chains of amino acids, usually from three to a few dozen residues, that neurons release to signal to other cells. They are synthesised as larger precursors in the cell body and packaged into dense-core vesicles. In contrast to classical neurotransmitters such as glutamate or GABA, which are made locally and act quickly at the synapse, neuropeptides are released in response to more intense or sustained activity, can diffuse beyond the synaptic cleft, and act mainly on G protein-coupled receptors. Their effects therefore unfold over longer timescales (see [[neuromodulation]]). van den Pol (2012) reviews how neuropeptide transmission shapes brain circuits.

## Examples
- **Oxytocin and vasopressin.** These closely related peptides, made in the hypothalamus, regulate birth, lactation and water balance, and influence social behaviour. Comparative studies of voles, reviewed by Insel and Young (2001), linked differences in receptor distribution to differences in pair bonding.
- **Opioid peptides.** Endorphins and enkephalins modulate pain and reward.
- **Substance P** contributes to pain signalling.
- **Orexin and neuropeptide Y** regulate arousal and feeding.
- **Corticotropin-releasing hormone** coordinates stress responses (see [[stress_response]]).

## Social effects in humans
Intranasal oxytocin has been reported to affect trust, emotion recognition and attention to social cues, as in the study by Kosfeld and colleagues (2005). Subsequent work shows that effects are modest and depend on context and individual differences, that delivery to the brain through the nose is uncertain, and that several early findings have been difficult to replicate. Claims that oxytocin is a simple trust or love hormone are therefore not supported.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is a proposal under investigation. In Active Inference, neuromodulators are often interpreted as encoding the precision of beliefs or prediction errors. Quattrocki and Friston (2014) proposed that oxytocin encodes the precision of interoceptive signals and supports learning a generative model of the emotional and social self, and they applied this to autism. This mapping is theoretical, and its empirical support is limited compared with that for dopamine or acetylcholine.
