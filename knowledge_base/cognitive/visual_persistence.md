# Visual Persistence

## Overview
Visual persistence is the continuation of a visual image or of information about it after the physical stimulus has stopped. It is part of why moving pictures appear continuous and why a briefly flashed display can still be reported accurately for a fraction of a second. Work on the topic has distinguished several things that are easily confused, so the term needs to be read carefully.

## Kinds of persistence
- **Visible persistence.** The stimulus continues to look present for a short time, on the order of a tenth of a second, after it is removed. It shows an inverse duration effect: the longer a stimulus is presented, the shorter the persistence that follows its offset, and persistence also declines with stimulus intensity (Coltheart, 1980).
- **Informational persistence.** Information about the stimulus remains available to be read out even though the display no longer looks present. In the partial-report procedure, observers who were cued by a tone to report one row of a briefly shown letter array could do so with high accuracy, and accuracy fell as the cue was delayed by up to about a second. This implied a high-capacity, rapidly decaying store (Sperling, 1960).
- **Neural persistence.** Retinal and cortical responses outlast the stimulus, which is plausibly part of the basis for both kinds above.
- **Afterimages.** These come from adaptation of retinal photoreceptors and from later processes, and they differ from persistence in time course and appearance, often appearing as complementary colours.

Coltheart argued that visible persistence and the information used in partial report should be distinguished from each other, and that the term iconic memory should be reserved for the second. Later work has questioned how much of the partial-report advantage reflects a visual store as opposed to later categorical codes (see [[iconic_memory]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
Persistence can be viewed as temporal integration within perceptual inference: beliefs about a scene are updated by successive evidence and do not reset when the input ceases, so the percept decays gradually as predictions are no longer supported. This description is an analogy and does not explain the measured time courses. A predictive processing account of phenomena like the inverse duration effect has not been developed in detail.
