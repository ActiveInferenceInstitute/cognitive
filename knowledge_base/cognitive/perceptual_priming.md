# Perceptual Priming

## Overview
Perceptual priming is the improvement in the speed or accuracy of identifying a stimulus as a result of a previous encounter with the same or a similar stimulus, based on its physical form rather than its meaning. It is a form of implicit memory, because it can be shown without any deliberate attempt to recall the earlier episode. Perceptual priming is usually contrasted with conceptual priming, which depends on meaning (see [[priming_effects]] and [[implicit_memory]]).

## Measurement and properties
Typical tasks present items in a study phase and later test identification of degraded or briefly shown versions, for example word-stem completion, fragment completion, or naming of pictures at threshold. Priming is shown as better performance on studied than on new items. Several properties are commonly reported:
- **Form specificity.** Effects are often reduced when the modality, typeface or object viewpoint changes between study and test, which suggests that the stored information is tied to perceptual features.
- **Dissociation from explicit memory.** Priming can occur without recognising the item, and manipulations that improve explicit recall, such as deep semantic encoding, often leave perceptual priming unchanged (Tulving, Schacter and Stark, 1982).
- **Longevity.** Effects can last hours or longer, although their size varies across tasks.

## Neural basis
Patients with amnesia can show intact priming despite severely impaired recall and recognition (Warrington and Weiskrantz, 1970), which supports the view that priming relies on different systems from those of declarative memory ([[memory_systems]], [[amnesia]]). Neuroimaging often finds reduced activity on repeated presentation in posterior visual regions, called repetition suppression, and this reduction has been proposed as a neural correlate of priming (Grill-Spector, Henson and Martin, 2006).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an interpretation of repetition effects within predictive-processing accounts, not a settled result. Repetition suppression can be explained as the consequence of learning or sharpening of the representation of a stimulus, which reduces prediction error on its next presentation, and competing explanations such as neural fatigue and facilitation remain in the literature (Grill-Spector et al., 2006). Predictive accounts also separate effects of stimulus repetition from effects of expectation, which are tested experimentally by varying the probability of repeats.
