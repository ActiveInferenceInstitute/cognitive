# Prospective Memory

## Overview
Prospective memory is remembering to carry out an intended action at an appropriate point in the future, such as posting a letter on the way past a postbox or taking medication at a set hour. It differs from retrospective memory, which concerns past events, although the two interact. A prospective task has a retrospective component, remembering what the action is, and a prospective component, remembering that something must be done and noticing when the moment arrives.

## Types and theories
- **Event-based and time-based.** Event-based tasks are cued by an external event, such as meeting a particular person. Time-based tasks must be performed at a given time or after an interval and offer no external cue, so they usually depend more on self-initiated monitoring.
- **Monitoring accounts.** Preparatory attentional and memory processes theory holds that remembering an intention requires capacity-consuming monitoring of the environment for the target, so ongoing task performance often slows while an intention is held.
- **Spontaneous retrieval accounts.** The multiprocess framework (McDaniel & Einstein, 2000; see also McDaniel & Einstein, 2007) proposes that the target can sometimes reflexively trigger retrieval of the intention without monitoring, depending on the cue's salience, its association with the intention and the demands of the ongoing task. Both routes are thought to operate, with the balance shifting by situation.
- **Neural correlates.** Imaging and lesion work frequently implicates rostral prefrontal cortex, alongside medial temporal regions for the content of the intention.

Failures of prospective memory are common in daily life and are more consequential in older adults, in clinical populations with frontal or medial temporal dysfunction, and in safety-critical work.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no established Active Inference model of prospective memory. A conceptual mapping is available: an intention can be treated as a prior preference or policy that must be maintained over a delay, and noticing the cue as inference that the current context matches the one in which the policy applies. Deep temporal models with slowly changing higher-level contexts provide the formal machinery for holding such goals, but this is a framing, not a tested account.
