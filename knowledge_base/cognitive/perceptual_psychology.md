# Perceptual Psychology

## Overview
Perceptual psychology is the experimental study of how organisms detect, discriminate, organize and interpret sensory information, and how perception relates to the physical stimulus and to behavior. Its methods are behavioral: measure what an observer reports or does as the stimulus is varied systematically, then infer the underlying process.

## Mechanism
The founding method is psychophysics, introduced by Fechner (1860), which relates stimulus magnitude to sensation through thresholds and scaling. Weber's law, that the just-noticeable difference grows in proportion to baseline intensity, is the classic regularity. Signal detection theory (Green and Swets) separated sensitivity ($d'$) from response bias, which made it possible to ask whether an experimental manipulation changed perception or only the observer's decision criterion. Adaptive procedures such as staircases estimate thresholds efficiently.

Theoretical traditions differ about what perception is. Helmholtz described perception as unconscious inference from ambiguous sensory data. The Gestalt school emphasized organization into wholes. Gibson's ecological approach argued that the optic array specifies the environment directly, with no need for inference, and introduced affordances. Constructivist and computational approaches (Marr) treat perception as information processing with explicit levels of analysis.

Standard phenomena include illusions, adaptation and aftereffects, constancies (size, color, lightness), cue combination, and perceptual learning. Many are used as diagnostic tests of competing models, since illusions reveal the assumptions a system applies.

## Role in Active Inference
Active inference descends directly from the Helmholtzian strand: perception as inference about hidden causes under a generative model, with priors and precision determining how data and expectation are weighed. Behavioral findings such as optimal cue combination, shifts in perception toward prior expectation, and context effects in illusions are the typical evidence offered for it, and psychophysical methods are how model predictions are tested.

The relation to Gibson is contested. Some active-inference authors argue that affordances can be accommodated as expected consequences of action, while ecological psychologists dispute that inference is needed at all. This page does not resolve that disagreement.
