# Temperament

## Overview
Temperament refers to constitutionally based individual differences in emotional, motor and attentional reactivity and in the self-regulation of that reactivity. These differences are visible early in life, show moderate stability, and are thought to be shaped by genetic and biological factors in interaction with experience. Temperament is usually treated as a developmental precursor of adult [[personality]] rather than as identical to it, since personality also includes values, goals and self-concept that emerge later.

## Major models
- **Thomas and Chess.** From the New York Longitudinal Study, they described nine dimensions: activity level, rhythmicity, approach or withdrawal, adaptability, intensity, mood, persistence, distractibility and sensory threshold. They grouped children into three broad patterns, labelled easy, difficult and slow to warm up, and emphasised goodness of fit between a child's temperament and the demands of the environment (Thomas and Chess, 1977).
- **Rothbart.** Temperament is defined as individual differences in reactivity and self-regulation. Questionnaire and observational work identify broad factors of surgency or extraversion, negative affectivity and effortful control, the last of which reflects voluntary regulation of attention and behaviour and matures through childhood (Rothbart, 2011).
- **Behavioural inhibition.** Kagan and colleagues studied children who respond to novelty with wariness and withdrawal, and followed their development over time.

Twin and adoption studies indicate a substantial heritable contribution to temperament measures. Temperament is not fixed: rank-order stability is moderate and increases with age, and parenting, culture and later experiences can change how traits are expressed.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference offers a vocabulary rather than an established account of temperament. Stable individual differences could be expressed as differences in priors, for example the baseline precision assigned to sensory evidence, the strength of prior preferences over outcomes, or the tolerance for uncertainty. Reactivity to novelty would then correspond to how strongly unexpected input is weighted (see [[precision_weighting]]), and effortful control to the capacity to modulate that weighting. This mapping is a hypothesis used in computational approaches to individual differences and has not been tested against the dimensional models above.
