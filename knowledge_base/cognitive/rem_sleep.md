# REM Sleep

## Overview
Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep is a stage of sleep first described by Aserinsky and Kleitman in 1953. It is characterised by bursts of rapid eye movements, a fast, low-amplitude cortical EEG resembling wakefulness, and loss of skeletal muscle tone. It alternates with non-REM sleep in cycles of roughly 90 minutes, and REM periods tend to lengthen toward the end of the night. Reports of vivid, narrative dreams are most frequent after waking from this stage, though dreaming also occurs in non-REM sleep.

## Physiology and function
- **Neuromodulation.** Cholinergic activity is high in REM sleep, while noradrenergic and serotonergic neurons are largely silent, a profile that differs from both waking and non-REM sleep (Hobson, 2009).
- **Atonia.** Brainstem circuits inhibit spinal motor neurons, which prevents the enactment of dreams. When this mechanism fails, the result is REM sleep behaviour disorder, which is recognised as a risk marker for later synucleinopathies such as Parkinson's disease.
- **Memory and emotion.** REM sleep has been linked to the consolidation of emotional and procedural memories, but findings are mixed and the specific contribution of REM relative to non-REM sleep remains under debate.
- **Development.** REM sleep makes up a larger share of sleep in infancy than in adulthood, which has motivated proposals about a role in brain development.

## Relation to Active Inference
Hobson and Friston (2012) proposed that REM sleep and dreaming be understood as the brain running its generative model in the absence of external input, a state they described as protoconsciousness. In this view sensory precision is low and the model is exercised through internally generated activity, which could allow it to be refined and its complexity reduced. Dream phenomenology such as bizarre content and loss of insight is read as the outcome of weak sensory constraints on inference. The proposal is a coherent theoretical framework but its specific predictions have been tested only partly, and it is one of several accounts of REM function.
