# Motor Units

## Overview
A motor unit consists of a single alpha motor neuron in the spinal cord or brainstem and all the skeletal muscle fibres it innervates. The term was introduced by Sherrington. Each action potential in the motor neuron normally produces a twitch in every fibre of its unit through the [[neuromuscular_junction]], so the unit is the smallest functional element through which the nervous system controls force. The set of motor neurons supplying one muscle is called its motor neuron pool.

## Properties
- **Innervation ratio.** Muscles used for fine movements, such as those moving the eye, have few fibres per motor neuron, whereas large postural and limb muscles have many hundreds or more, so one neuron produces a larger force step.
- **Unit types.** Units are classified as slow (S), fast fatigue-resistant (FR) and fast fatigable (FF), with progressively larger force, faster contraction and lower resistance to fatigue.
- **Graded force.** Force increases through recruitment of additional units and through increases in the firing rate of active units.

## Size principle
Henneman (1957) showed that motor neurons are recruited in order of size: small neurons, which drive slow low-force units, are activated first, and larger neurons are added as demand rises. This orderly recruitment simplifies control, since a single descending drive that increases in strength recruits units in a fixed sequence. Exceptions have been reported for some rapid or specialised movements.

## Clinical relevance
Loss of motor neurons, as in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, or damage to peripheral nerves reduces the number of functioning units and causes weakness. Recording the electrical activity of motor units through electromyography is used in clinical assessment and in research (see [[movement_disorders]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
In the Active Inference account of the motor system, descending signals are interpreted as predictions about proprioceptive input rather than commands, and spinal reflex arcs fulfil these predictions by driving motor neurons (Adams and colleagues, 2013; see [[proprioception]]). The motor unit is therefore the final effector of this process. The account is a theoretical proposal, and the mapping of precision and prediction onto spinal circuitry is not fully settled.
