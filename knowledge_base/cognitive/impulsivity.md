# Impulsivity

## Overview
Impulsivity is not a single process. It refers to a cluster of tendencies to act quickly without forethought, to prefer immediate rewards, or to be unable to stop an action once started. It is a normal trait varying across people and is also a feature of several psychiatric conditions, where its role is correlational and heterogeneous.

## Dimensions and measurement
Evenden (1999) and Moeller and colleagues (2001) distinguish several components:
- **Choice impulsivity (impulsive decision-making).** A preference for smaller sooner rewards over larger later ones, measured by delay-discounting tasks.
- **Action impulsivity (impulsive action).** Failure to withhold or cancel a response, measured by go/no-go and stop-signal tasks.
- **Reflection impulsivity.** Deciding with too little information gathered.
- **Trait impulsivity.** Self-report scales such as the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale. Scores from tasks and questionnaires correlate only weakly, which supports the view that these are distinct constructs.

**Delay discounting.** A common description is hyperbolic: the present value of an amount $A$ at delay $D$ is $V = A/(1+kD)$, with discount rate $k$. Higher $k$ means steeper discounting. Hyperbolic discounting predicts preference reversals. With $k=0.2$ per day, 50 now ($V=50$) beats 100 in 10 days ($V=33.3$), but when both options are pushed 20 days into the future, 50 in 20 days ($V=10$) is worth less than 100 in 30 days ($V=14.3$), so the later-larger option is chosen.

**Neural correlates.** Ventromedial prefrontal cortex and ventral striatum are implicated in valuation, right inferior frontal cortex in stopping, and dopaminergic and serotonergic systems in modulating both. Causal claims about specific neurotransmitters are uncertain.

## Role in Active Inference
Dopamine has been proposed to encode the precision of beliefs about policies (Friston et al., 2014), so that altered precision could produce either indecision or premature commitment to a policy. Delay-related behaviour could arise from the form of the prior preferences and the planning horizon, and from how much future outcomes weigh in expected free energy. These are hypotheses within computational psychiatry, tested mainly in simulation and small samples. Competing explanations, such as the reinforcement-learning account of steeper discounting, explain much of the same data. Nothing here supports diagnostic or treatment conclusions.
