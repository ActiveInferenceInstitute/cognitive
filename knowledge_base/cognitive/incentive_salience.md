# Incentive Salience

## Overview
Incentive salience is the motivational property that turns the perception of a reward-associated cue into something attractive and capable of provoking approach and effort. In the account developed by Berridge and Robinson, it is the psychological basis of "wanting", which is separable from "liking", the hedonic pleasure produced when a reward is consumed. A cue that has acquired incentive salience stands out in attention, draws behaviour towards itself, and can cause craving, without necessarily being associated with greater enjoyment.

## Wanting and liking
Berridge and Robinson (1998) reviewed evidence that dopamine is not the mediator of hedonic impact. Rats with up to 99% depletion of dopamine in the nucleus accumbens and neostriatum still showed normal pleasure-related facial reactions to sweet tastes, yet fail to initiate eating and seeking. They concluded that dopamine systems contribute mainly to incentive salience, with hedonic impact depending on other systems. Later work from Berridge's group points to opioid and related signals in small hedonic hotspots of the nucleus accumbens and ventral pallidum. Alternative accounts emphasise dopamine in reward prediction errors and learning (see [[reward_prediction_error]]), and the relative roles are still debated.

## Sensitisation and addiction
In the incentive-sensitisation theory of addiction (Robinson and Berridge, 1993), repeated drug exposure sensitises mesolimbic systems in susceptible individuals, so that drug cues become pathologically attractive, even when pleasure from the drug itself does not increase or declines. Experimental support includes work on sign-tracking, in which some animals approach and interact with a cue predicting reward, and not the reward location, and these animals appear to be more susceptible to cue-triggered relapse in some models. Evidence in humans is more limited and the theory is one among several accounts of compulsive use.

## Relation to Active Inference
Dopamine is proposed in Active Inference process theories to encode the precision of beliefs about policies, which influences how strongly cues drive action selection. This offers a parallel to incentive salience, since both concern the cue-driven motivation to act, but the mapping is a proposal and not an established equivalence, and wanting-versus-liking is not a standard distinction in the framework (see [[dopamine_system]]).
