# Protocol Analysis

## Overview
Protocol analysis is a family of methods in which participants speak aloud while they perform a task, and the resulting transcript, the protocol, is segmented, coded and analysed to infer the cognitive processes involved. It was developed in the study of human problem solving by Allen Newell and Herbert Simon and given its methodological foundation by K. Anders Ericsson and Simon, who argued that verbal reports are legitimate data when collected and interpreted under the right conditions. It is used to study expert performance, reasoning, reading, design, and the strategies people choose when solving problems (see [[reasoning_problem_solving]]).

## The Ericsson and Simon model
Ericsson and Simon (1980, 1984) proposed an information-processing account of what a verbal report can contain. Their central distinction is about the relation between the report and the contents of short-term memory at the time the report is made.
- **Concurrent think-aloud.** The person verbalises the thoughts that are already in the focus of attention in a form that can be spoken. This is expected to reproduce the sequence of heeded information without changing it much, although it may slow performance when the verbalisation requires translating non-verbal content.
- **Retrospective reports.** Reports made after the task depend on retrieval from long-term memory, are more open to reconstruction and inference, and are less reliable the longer the delay.
- **Requests to explain.** Asking participants to give reasons, or to describe processes they did not attend to, can change the thinking itself and yields inferred accounts, not records.

## Procedure
Typical steps are instructions and practice with a neutral task, collecting the protocol while minimising prompts, transcribing, segmenting into units that correspond to single operations, coding against a scheme derived from a task analysis, and comparing the coded sequence with predictions from a model such as a production system or search model. Inter-rater agreement on the coding is usually reported.

## Criticism
Nisbett and Wilson (1977) reviewed studies indicating that people often cannot report the causes of their own judgments and instead offer plausible theories about them. Ericsson and Simon's reply is that this critique applies to reports that ask for explanations of processes that were not attended to, and not to the reporting of attended content. Verbal reports remain a partial source: they say little about automatic processes and may under-report non-verbal imagery.

## Relation to Active Inference
Protocol analysis is a measurement method and has no direct connection to the free energy principle. Think-aloud data could be used to constrain models of planning in which the agent evaluates sequences of actions (see [[hierarchical_active_inference]]), for instance by comparing reported subgoals with the policies a model prefers. Such use is a modelling choice.
