# Perseveration

## Overview
Perseveration is the continuation or repetition of a response, thought or behaviour beyond the point at which it is appropriate, especially after the task rules or context have changed. It appears in speech, motor behaviour, thought and test performance. It is a descriptive term for a behavioural pattern and is not a diagnosis, and it is seen in a range of neurological and psychiatric conditions as well as, occasionally, in healthy people under fatigue or high load.

## Forms
Sandson and Albert (1984) distinguished three types in neurological patients:
- **Recurrent perseveration**: the return of an earlier response to a later, different stimulus, such as naming a new picture with the name of a previous one.
- **Continuous perseveration**: the uninterrupted repetition of an ongoing act, for example continued drawing of the same line.
- **Stuck-in-set perseveration**: the maintenance of a previously correct category or rule after a switch is required.

In language, perseveration may occur as repeated words or phrases in aphasia ([[aphasia]]). In motor tasks, it may appear as repeated movements, and in thought as repetitive ideas.

## Assessment and associations
Perseverative errors are a standard output of card-sorting tasks. In the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, a patient continues to sort by a previous principle after feedback indicates it is no longer correct. Milner (1963) reported that patients with frontal lobe excisions made more perseverative errors than those with other lesions, and this became an influential link between perseveration and prefrontal function ([[prefrontal]], [[executive_dysfunction]]). Later work shows that the association is not exclusive to the frontal lobe, that perseveration also occurs after damage elsewhere, and that it is reported in conditions such as schizophrenia, obsessive-compulsive disorder and autism. A deficit in [[cognitive_flexibility]] and [[set_shifting]] is one common description.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is interpretive. In a predictive account, perseveration can be read as a failure to update beliefs or policies when evidence changes: very precise priors over a rule or over a habitual policy give prediction errors from negative feedback too little weight, so the previous response continues to be selected. Computational psychiatry studies of reversal learning use related ideas, but the mapping of specific parameters to clinical perseveration is still tentative and varies across models and conditions ([[decision_making]]).
