# Syllable Structure

## Overview
A syllable is a unit of speech organisation larger than a single sound and smaller than a word. In most phonological analyses it has a hierarchical internal structure. The **nucleus**, usually a vowel, forms the peak. Material before it is the **onset**, and material after it is the **coda**. The nucleus and coda together form the **rime**, which is the domain of rhyme and, in many languages, of stress assignment. Syllables with no coda are open, and those with a coda are closed. In most analyses only the nucleus is obligatory, although some languages also require an onset.

## Principles
- **Sonority.** Sounds can be ranked by sonority, roughly from stops through fricatives, nasals and liquids to glides and vowels. Within a syllable, sonority tends to rise from the onset to the nucleus and fall towards the coda. English /plank/ conforms to this, while /s/ before a stop, as in "spin", is a well-known exception.
- **Maximal onset.** When consonants occur between two vowels, speakers and analyses tend to assign as many as the language allows to the following syllable's onset.
- **Phonotactics.** Each language permits a specific range of syllable shapes. Hawaiian allows only open syllables with at most one onset consonant, many varieties of Japanese restrict codas mainly to nasals and the first half of geminates, and English allows up to three consonants in onset and several in coda, as in "strengths".

Syllable weight, determined by the rime, affects stress placement in many languages. Languages with moraic structure, such as Japanese, use the mora rather than the syllable as the timing unit.

## Cognitive role and Active Inference
Psycholinguistic work suggests that frequently used syllables may be stored as articulatory units that are retrieved whole, an idea known as the mental syllabary (Levelt and Wheeldon, 1994). Cortical oscillations in the range of the syllable rate have been proposed as a mechanism that segments the speech signal (Giraud and Poeppel, 2012). In Active Inference, speech perception is modelled by hierarchical generative models whose levels operate on different timescales, so that slower levels constrain faster ones. Kiebel and colleagues (2009) used such models for recognising sequences of sequences, with birdsong as the example. The syllable is a natural intermediate level in this picture, but the correspondence is an analogy rather than a tested claim about neural implementation.
