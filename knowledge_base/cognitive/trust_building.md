# Trust Building

## Overview
Trust is a willingness to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations about another party's behaviour. Trust building is the process by which such expectations are formed and revised through interaction, communication, and information about reputation. It is studied in social psychology, organisational research, and behavioural economics, where it is operationalised as a costly choice that pays off only if the partner reciprocates.

## Components and findings
- **Trustworthiness.** A widely used organisational model identifies ability, benevolence, and integrity as the attributes of a trustee that a trustor evaluates (Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman).
- **Trust game.** In the investment game, a trustor sends part of an endowment, it is multiplied, and the trustee decides how much to return. Amounts sent are measures of trust and amounts returned of reciprocity.
- **Repeated play.** In repeated games, trust develops as partners reciprocate. Brain imaging of repeated trust games reports signals in the caudate that shift from reflecting the partner's reciprocation to anticipating it as reputation forms (King-Casas and colleagues).
- **Asymmetry.** Violations tend to damage trust more than equal-sized cooperative acts repair it, a pattern reported often but varying with context and violation type.
- **Contextual levers.** Reputation information, communication, commitment devices, and shared group membership all shift initial trust.
- **Oxytocin.** Early reports that intranasal oxytocin increases trust have not held up well in replications and reviews, so a specific causal role is uncertain ([[oxytocin]]).

## Role in Active Inference
Trust can be formalised as a belief about the partner's hidden type: a categorical or continuous variable describing their disposition to cooperate, updated from observed actions ([[belief_updating]]). Choosing to trust has pragmatic value (expected payoff) and epistemic value, because it reveals the partner's type ([[epistemic_value]], [[expected_free_energy]]). Multi-agent formulations in which agents model each other extend the idea ([[multi_agent_active_inference]]). These constitute a modelling approach and have been applied to specific economic games; wider claims about trust in real settings remain hypotheses.
