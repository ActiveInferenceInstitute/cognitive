# Ambiguous Figures

## Overview
An ambiguous figure is a stimulus whose sensory data are compatible with more than one stable interpretation. Observers typically see one interpretation at a time, and the percept alternates between them without any change in the image. Well-known examples include the Necker cube, whose depth orientation reverses, the Rubin vase and faces, in which figure and ground exchange roles, and the duck-rabbit figure used by Jastrow and later discussed by Wittgenstein. The phenomenon of alternation is treated more generally under [[bistable_perception]].

## Properties
- **One percept at a time.** Observers do not usually see a blend of the interpretations, which indicates that perception commits to a single coherent explanation of the input.
- **Reversals.** Spontaneous switching occurs after seconds, and the rate varies between people and with conditions. Reversal dynamics are often described as a stochastic process with a gamma-like distribution of dwell times.
- **Voluntary influence.** Observers can often bias which interpretation is seen or speed up reversals through attention or eye position, but cannot fully control it.
- **Context and expectation.** Prior exposure, surrounding context and instructions shift the interpretation seen first, which shows that experience contributes to perception (see [[context_effects]] and [[expectation_effects]]).
- **Neural correlates.** Imaging studies report that switches involve changes in activity in visual areas and in frontal and parietal regions. Debate continues about which of these causes switching and which reflects it.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection to predictive processing is an active research area. A hierarchical generative model that has two hypotheses with similar posterior probability cannot settle on both, so perception selects one and explains away the data under it. As the evidence for the current hypothesis fails to account for part of the input, the accumulating prediction error is thought to favour the competing hypothesis, producing a switch (see [[prediction_error]]). Hohwy, Roepstorff and Friston (2008) developed this argument for binocular rivalry, and similar reasoning has been applied to ambiguous figures. Alternative explanations, such as neural adaptation and noise, can also produce reversals and the accounts are not mutually exclusive.
