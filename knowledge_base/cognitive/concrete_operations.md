# Concrete Operations

## Overview
The concrete operational stage is the third of Jean Piaget's four stages of cognitive development, following the sensorimotor and preoperational stages and preceding formal operations. It is usually placed between about seven and eleven years of age. Piaget described its defining achievement as the acquisition of mental operations: internalised, reversible actions that can be coordinated into organised systems. These operations are called concrete because they apply to objects and situations that are present or readily imagined, not to purely abstract or hypothetical propositions.

## Characteristic abilities
- **Conservation.** The child understands that quantity, such as number, liquid volume or mass, is unchanged by a change in appearance. Pouring water from a short wide glass into a tall narrow one does not alter the amount.
- **Reversibility and decentration.** The child can mentally undo a transformation and attend to several dimensions at once, such as height and width, instead of fixating on one.
- **Classification.** Objects can be sorted into nested hierarchies, so that the child grasps that a class includes its subclasses.
- **Seriation and transitivity.** Items can be ordered along a dimension such as length, and the child can infer that if A exceeds B and B exceeds C, then A exceeds C, at least when the items can be handled or visualised.

## Critiques
Later work challenged the idea of sharp, domain-general stages. Performance on conservation and related tasks depends on task wording, familiarity and training, and different conservation concepts are often mastered at different ages, a pattern Piaget called horizontal decalage. Many contemporary researchers favour more gradual, domain-specific accounts of development, while keeping Piaget's descriptions of the tasks as a standard reference point.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no derivation of Piagetian stages from the free energy principle. The link is an analogy: development can be described as the learning of richer generative models, with the stage transitions corresponding to the acquisition of structure that supports counterfactual, reversible reasoning about transformations. Work on structure learning and hierarchical models explores such ideas, but it is a research direction and not an established account of the concrete operational stage.
