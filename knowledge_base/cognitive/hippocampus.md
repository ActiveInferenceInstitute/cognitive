# Hippocampus

## Overview
The hippocampus is a seahorse-shaped structure in the medial temporal lobe, part of the hippocampal formation together with the dentate gyrus and subiculum, and it is closely connected with the entorhinal cortex. It is required for forming new declarative memories, especially episodic memories, and for spatial navigation, and it contributes to imagination and planning.

## Anatomy and function
Cortical input arrives through the entorhinal cortex to the dentate gyrus, then CA3, then CA1 and the subiculum, in the so-called trisynaptic circuit, with additional direct pathways. Key findings:
- **Memory.** After bilateral medial temporal lobe resection the patient H.M. lost the ability to form new declarative memories while retaining skills and short-term memory (Scoville and Milner, 1957). Older memories are less affected, consistent with systems consolidation.
- **Space.** Place cells fire when an animal occupies a particular location (O'Keefe and Dostrovsky, 1971). In the neighbouring entorhinal cortex, grid cells fire at the vertices of a hexagonal lattice (Hafting et al., 2005).
- **Computation.** Marr (1971) proposed that recurrent connections in CA3 support autoassociative storage and completion of patterns from partial cues, and the dentate gyrus is proposed to orthogonalise similar inputs (pattern separation). Complementary learning systems theory (McClelland, McNaughton and O'Reilly, 1995) proposes that the hippocampus rapidly learns specific episodes while neocortex gradually extracts structure, with replay during sleep transferring memories between them.
- **Dynamics.** Theta oscillations and sharp-wave ripples organise sequences of place-cell firing. During rest and sleep, sequences are replayed, which supports consolidation and may support planning.

Whether the hippocampus is a pure memory structure or a general relational or predictive map is debated, as is the extent to which it is needed for older memories.

## Role in Active Inference
Hippocampal sequences can be seen as the inference of trajectories under a generative model of space and time. Penny, Zeidman and Burgess (2013) developed a probabilistic model of forward and backward inference in spatial cognition, and Friston and Buzsaki (2016) related hippocampal sequences to the representation of what and when within a hierarchical model. Scene construction, imagining coherent scenes, has been proposed as a hippocampal function that supports evaluating policies in Active Inference planning. These accounts are active research programmes that map known physiology onto model components and do not follow from the free-energy principle alone.
