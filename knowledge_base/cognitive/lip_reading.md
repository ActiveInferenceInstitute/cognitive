# Lip Reading

## Overview
Lip reading, also called speechreading, is the recognition of spoken language from the visible movements of the lips, jaw, tongue tip and face. It is used deliberately by many deaf and hard-of-hearing people, and it is also used automatically by hearing listeners, who understand speech better in noise when they can see the speaker. Visible speech is therefore a normal part of [[speech_perception]], not only a compensatory skill.

## Information available
Many speech sounds are produced by articulators that are not visible, and several are visually indistinguishable. For example, the consonants /p/, /b/ and /m/ look alike on the lips. Groups of sounds that share a visual appearance are called visemes. For this reason, speech cannot be fully recovered from vision alone, and skilled lip readers rely on context, knowledge of the language and expectations about the topic. Performance differs widely between individuals, and it is not simply predicted by hearing status.

## Integration with hearing
Sumby and Pollack (1954) showed that seeing the talker improves the intelligibility of speech in noise by an amount comparable to a substantial increase in signal-to-noise ratio. The McGurk effect, in which a visual syllable combined with a different auditory syllable gives a fused percept, demonstrates that vision alters what is heard (McGurk and MacDonald, 1976; see [[multisensory_illusions]]). Silent lip reading activates regions of auditory cortex, including superior temporal areas, in hearing people (Calvert and colleagues, 1997; see [[superior_temporal]] and [[auditory_cortex]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
Audio-visual speech is a natural case for Bayesian cue combination, in which visual and auditory evidence are weighted by their reliability (see [[cue_integration]]). Predictive accounts add that the visible articulation often precedes the sound and can serve as a prior for upcoming auditory input, although the strength of this effect varies. Active Inference offers a general language for such precision-weighted combination (see [[precision_weighting]]), but specific models of lip reading are not a settled part of the framework.
