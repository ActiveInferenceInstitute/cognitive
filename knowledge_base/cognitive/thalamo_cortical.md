# Thalamo-Cortical Circuits

## Overview
Thalamo-cortical circuits consist of the reciprocal pathways between the thalamus and the neocortex. Almost every cortical area sends projections to the thalamus and receives projections from it. These loops are central to sensory processing, to the generation of rhythms during sleep and wakefulness, and to theories of how cortical areas communicate.

## Organisation
Sherman and Guillery distinguish two classes of thalamic relay nucleus. **First-order** nuclei, such as the lateral geniculate nucleus for vision, receive driving input from the periphery and relay it to primary sensory cortex. **Higher-order** nuclei, such as the pulvinar, receive their main driving input from layer V of the cortex and relay information to other cortical areas, forming a route for cortico-thalamo-cortical communication in parallel with direct corticocortical connections. Both classes also receive feedback from layer VI of the cortex, and the number of such corticothalamic synapses onto relay cells is large compared with the number of driving synapses from the periphery, suggesting a strong modulatory role (Sherman and Guillery, 2002).

The thalamic reticular nucleus, a shell of inhibitory neurons, receives collaterals from both thalamocortical and corticothalamic axons and inhibits relay cells. This arrangement allows gating of thalamic transmission and is involved in generating sleep spindles. Steriade, McCormick and Sejnowski (1993) reviewed how intrinsic properties of thalamic neurons and their network interactions produce oscillations that change between sleep and waking states.

## Role in attention and predictive processing
Thalamo-cortical loops are widely proposed to contribute to attention, since thalamic nuclei can modulate the gain and synchrony of cortical activity. In Adaptive Resonance Theory, matched bottom-up and top-down signals produce resonant states, and thalamo-cortical feedback has been proposed to support the top-down expectation component (see [[adaptive_resonance_theory]]). In predictive processing, Kanai and colleagues (2015) suggested that the pulvinar helps regulate precision across a cortical hierarchy, so that it modulates the weighting of prediction errors (see [[precision_weighting]]). These are theoretical proposals that guide experiments; anatomical connectivity is well established, but the computational functions attributed to the loops remain under investigation.
