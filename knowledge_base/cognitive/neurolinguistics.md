# Neurolinguistics

## Overview
Neurolinguistics investigates how language is implemented in the brain. It combines evidence from patients with brain damage, from neuroimaging and electrophysiology in healthy people, and from computational models. The field aims to relate components of linguistic theory, such as phonology, the lexicon, syntax and semantics, to neural systems and their dynamics.

## Classical and modern models
The classical view arose from nineteenth-century lesion studies. Broca linked damage to a left frontal region with impaired speech production, and Wernicke linked damage to a left posterior temporal region with impaired comprehension (see [[brocas_area]], [[wernickes_area]]). The resulting Wernicke-Lichtheim model has been revised, since aphasia syndromes do not map neatly onto single regions and many areas contribute to each function. Contemporary accounts emphasise networks of regions connected by white matter pathways. A prominent example is the dual-stream model of Hickok and Poeppel (2007), in which a ventral stream maps sound onto meaning and a dorsal stream maps sound onto articulatory representations. Friederici (2011) describes a frontotemporal network with distinct pathways for syntactic and semantic processing.

## Methods
Lesion-symptom mapping relates damaged tissue to deficits. Functional magnetic resonance imaging localises activity during language tasks, while electroencephalography and magnetoencephalography track the timing of processes, for example the N400 response associated with semantic integration and the P600 associated with syntactic reanalysis. Brain stimulation can test whether a region is necessary for a task. Language is lateralised to the left hemisphere in most right-handed people, although the right hemisphere contributes, notably to prosody and discourse.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is moderately developed. Predictive coding models propose that language areas form a hierarchy in which top-down predictions about upcoming words or phonemes are compared with bottom-up signals. Event-related responses such as the N400 have been interpreted in terms of prediction error, though alternative interpretations exist. Computational work by Friston and colleagues (2020) applies Active Inference to simulated linguistic communication, but a decisive empirical test of the framework against competing accounts is still lacking.
