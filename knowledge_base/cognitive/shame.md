# Shame

## Overview
Shame is a negative self-conscious emotion in which a person judges the self, as a whole, to be inadequate, defective or exposed to the disapproval of others. Self-conscious emotions depend on the capacity to represent oneself and to imagine how one is seen, and so they develop later in childhood than basic emotions such as fear (Tracy & Robins, 2004). Shame can be triggered by actual public exposure or by private reflection on a perceived failure of the self with respect to a standard.

## Features and functions
- **Distinction from guilt.** Following Lewis (1971), shame is generally contrasted with guilt: shame centres on the global self ("I am bad"), whereas guilt centres on a specific behaviour ("I did something bad"). Shame is associated with the urge to hide, withdraw or disappear, whereas guilt is more associated with the urge to repair or apologise (Tangney & Dearing, 2002). Studies often find the distinction clearer in ratings than in everyday experience, since the emotions frequently occur together.
- **Display.** Characteristic nonverbal behaviour includes lowered gaze, slumped posture and head down. Tracy and Matsumoto (2008) found similar displays in congenitally blind and sighted athletes after losing, which suggests a partly innate component.
- **Social function.** It has been proposed to serve as an appeasement signal and to help to preserve standing within a group by motivating conformity with norms. This idea is plausible but difficult to test directly.
- **Clinical relevance.** Chronic or intense shame is associated with depression, social anxiety and some trauma-related conditions, though the direction of causation is not settled and is often debated.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no established Active Inference model of shame. A tentative interpretation links self-conscious emotions to inference within a social generative model, where norms act as shared priors about acceptable conduct and the agent's own standing is a state to be estimated. Shame would then reflect a large mismatch between observed or imagined social feedback and the expectations of the self-model, with withdrawal and concealment as policies that limit further surprise. This reading is conceptual and unvalidated.
