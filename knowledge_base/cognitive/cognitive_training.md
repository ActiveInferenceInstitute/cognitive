# Cognitive Training

## Overview
Cognitive training is structured, repeated practice on tasks intended to improve a cognitive ability, such as working memory, attention, processing speed or executive control, so that benefits extend beyond the trained task. It includes computerised "brain training" programmes and adaptive tasks whose difficulty tracks performance. Related approaches targeting attention are covered in [[attention_training]].

## Transfer
Transfer is the key question. Studies distinguish:
- **Trained-task gains**: large and reliable, reflecting strategy and stimulus-specific learning, as in [[perceptual_learning]] and [[skill_acquisition]].
- **Near transfer**: improvement on tasks that share processes with the trained task, often modest.
- **Far transfer**: improvement on distinct abilities, such as fluid reasoning or everyday functioning, much harder to demonstrate.

The idea that practice generalises only to the extent that tasks share elements goes back to Thorndike and Woodworth's work at the start of the twentieth century. Jaeggi and colleagues (2008) reported that working-memory training improved fluid intelligence, which prompted much follow-up. Subsequent meta-analyses, such as Melby-Lervåg and Hulme (2013), found short-term gains on similar working-memory measures but little evidence of durable far transfer, and Simons and colleagues (2016) concluded that evidence for generalised benefits of commercial brain-training programmes was limited, noting methodological problems including lack of active controls and expectancy effects. Results vary with age, population and design, and the field continues to debate effect sizes.

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference contributes an explanatory framing, not a prediction of efficacy. If training improves the priors and precision settings used for a given task structure, then gains should extend only to tasks whose generative structure is shared, consistent with the observed pattern of strong specificity and weak far transfer (see [[precision_weighting]], [[bayesian_learning]], [[structure_learning]]). Broad transfer would require learning of structure common across tasks, as in [[meta_learning]]. This is a post hoc fit to existing findings and has not been used to predict which programmes should work, so it should be treated as an interpretation.
