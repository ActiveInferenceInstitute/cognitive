# Schemas

## Overview
A schema is an organised structure of prior knowledge about a type of situation, object or event, which supplies expectations and fills in missing detail. Examples include the sequence of events in a restaurant visit, the layout of a typical kitchen, or a stereotype about a social group. The concept was introduced into psychology by Bartlett (1932), who showed that people reconstruct stories in line with their existing knowledge, and into developmental theory by Piaget.

## Properties and findings
- **Schema-driven memory.** Material that fits a schema is usually learned faster and recalled more reliably, whereas missing details tend to be filled in from expectation, which produces systematic distortions in recall (Bartlett, 1932).
- **Scripts.** Schank and Abelson (1977) described scripts as schemas for stereotyped sequences of actions, used to understand narratives and anticipate what happens next.
- **Assimilation and accommodation.** Piaget distinguished fitting new experience into an existing schema from modifying the schema when experience does not fit, which is a basic account of how knowledge changes with development.
- **Neural basis.** Work on the medial prefrontal cortex and hippocampus indicates that information consistent with an established schema can be integrated into neocortex more quickly, while novel information relies more heavily on the hippocampus (van Kesteren et al., 2012). Schema theory has been criticised for vagueness, and its claims are best treated as a family of related ideas.

## Relation to Active Inference
A schema can be read as a hierarchical generative model: a structured prior over what states and observations typically occur together and in what order. Applying a schema corresponds to inference within an existing model, and assimilation fits that description. Accommodation corresponds to changing the model, which in the framework is the problem of structure learning (see [[structure_learning]]). Surprise relative to the schema's predictions drives revision. The mapping is a conceptual correspondence that makes schema theory formally tractable, not a result that schemas have been demonstrated to be generative models in the brain.
