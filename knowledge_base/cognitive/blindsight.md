# Blindsight

## Overview
Blindsight is the finding that some patients with damage to primary visual cortex (V1) can respond appropriately to visual stimuli in the part of the visual field that has become blind, while denying that they see anything there. The term was introduced following work by Weiskrantz and colleagues in the 1970s (Weiskrantz et al., 1974). When forced to guess, patients may point to, localise or discriminate stimuli above chance, and some show pupillary responses or detect motion and orientation.

## Characteristics and mechanisms
Performance is usually best for simple attributes such as location, motion, orientation and sometimes wavelength, and poor for form and object identity. Weiskrantz distinguished a type in which there is no awareness at all from a type in which the patient senses that something happened without any visual quality. The preserved abilities are generally attributed to pathways that bypass V1, including the retinotectal route to the superior colliculus and projections from the thalamus, including the lateral geniculate nucleus and pulvinar, to extrastriate areas (Cowey, 2010). The size and location of the lesion, and the amount of spared tissue, vary between patients and bear on interpretation.

## Interpretation
The central dispute is whether blindsight shows unconscious visual processing or merely degraded conscious vision that patients report conservatively. Differences in response criterion can make weak percepts look like no percepts, so studies compare awareness reports with performance using signal-detection methods. A second dispute concerns what the dissociation says about the neural basis of awareness: it is cited in discussions of [[global_workspace]] theories, which tie awareness to broadcasting of information, and of higher-order theories.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. Predictive-processing accounts of consciousness tie awareness to hierarchical inference with sufficient precision and depth, so a dissociation of discrimination from report is compatible with processing that influences behaviour without engaging the inference that supports reportable experience. No quantitative Active Inference model of blindsight is established, and the case is better seen as a constraint that any theory of awareness must accommodate.
