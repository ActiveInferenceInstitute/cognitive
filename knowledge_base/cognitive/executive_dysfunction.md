# Executive Dysfunction

## Overview
Executive dysfunction is a collective term for difficulties in the control processes that organise behaviour towards goals: planning, initiating and sequencing actions, holding goals in mind, inhibiting inappropriate responses, shifting between tasks and monitoring errors. It is a description of a pattern of impairment, not a diagnosis. It appears after frontal or frontostriatal damage and in many neurological and psychiatric conditions, including traumatic brain injury, Parkinson's disease, schizophrenia and attention-deficit disorders, and it varies widely in severity.

## Clinical features
Typical presentations include perseveration on a failed strategy, impulsive or disinhibited responding, poor organisation of multi-step tasks, difficulty with novel situations, and reduced awareness of one's own difficulties. Stuss and Benson (1986) described the frontal lobes as exerting executive control over other brain functions, and Baddeley's concept of a dysexecutive syndrome grew from the same clinical observations. Performance may be preserved on routine, well-practised activities and fail when a task requires new rules or the overriding of a habit (see [[automaticity]]).

## Assessment
Neuropsychological tests target separate components. Miyake et al. (2000) found, in healthy adults, that updating, shifting and inhibition are related but separable factors, which suggests that executive dysfunction is not unitary. Laboratory measures include the Stroop, card-sorting and tower-planning tasks (see [[set_shifting]] and [[perseveration]]). Lezak and colleagues note that scores on these tests can be normal in patients whose everyday organisation is clearly impaired, so test results and daily-life observation are usually considered together.

## Relation to Active Inference
Computational psychiatry reads control deficits as altered inference about policies, for instance imprecise priors, mis-set precision of beliefs about action outcomes, or reduced ability to hold goals in a hierarchy of beliefs. These proposals are active research programmes, with simulations of specific tasks, and not settled explanations of any single condition. A link is also drawn to dopaminergic modulation of precision in frontostriatal loops (see [[frontostriatal]]).
