# Tremor

## Overview
Tremor is an involuntary, rhythmic, oscillatory movement of a body part. It is the most common movement disorder, and it ranges from the small tremor present in all healthy people to a disabling condition that interferes with writing, eating and speech. The Movement Disorder Society consensus classifies tremor by its activation condition and its distribution in the body (Deuschl, Bain and Brin, 1998). The description below is general and is not guidance on diagnosis or treatment.

## Classification
- **Rest tremor** occurs when the affected part is relaxed and supported against gravity. The classic example is the four to six hertz rest tremor of Parkinson's disease (see [[parkinsons]]), often described as pill-rolling in the hand.
- **Action tremor** occurs during voluntary contraction. It includes postural tremor while a position is held, kinetic tremor during movement, and intention tremor, which grows in amplitude as the limb approaches a target.
- **Physiological tremor** is a low-amplitude, high-frequency oscillation present in healthy people. It is thought to reflect mechanical resonance of the limb together with reflex and central contributions, and it can be enhanced by fatigue, anxiety, caffeine or certain drugs.
- **Essential tremor** is a common action tremor, usually of the hands and often with a family history, for which the cause is not fully understood.
- **Cerebellar tremor** is a typically slower intention tremor associated with damage to cerebellar pathways (see [[cerebellar_ataxia]]).

## Mechanisms
Tremors arise from different mechanisms. Parkinsonian rest tremor has been linked to pathological oscillations in basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical circuits, essential tremor to a network involving the cerebellum, thalamus and cortex, and physiological tremor in part to peripheral mechanics and reflex loops. Any feedback loop with delay and sufficient gain can oscillate, and this principle is used in models of both normal and pathological tremor.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no standard Active Inference account of tremor. Active Inference treats movement as the fulfilment of proprioceptive predictions, with precision determining how strongly sensory feedback drives corrections (Adams, Shipp and Friston, 2013). Oscillation could arise in such a scheme if feedback gain or delays were mis-set, and dopamine has been associated with the precision of motor policies (see [[dopamine_system]]). These connections are conceptual, and no model has been validated against clinical tremor data.
