# Vicarious Reinforcement

## Overview
Vicarious reinforcement is the strengthening or weakening of an observer's tendency to perform a behaviour as a result of seeing another individual, the model, receive reward or punishment for it. The observer does not receive the consequence directly. The concept was developed in Albert Bandura's social learning theory as a corrective to accounts in which learning depended on the learner's own experienced outcomes. When observed consequences discourage the behaviour, the effect is sometimes called vicarious punishment.

## Evidence
In the classic experiments of Bandura, Ross and Ross (1963) and Bandura (1965), children watched a model behave aggressively and saw the model rewarded, punished or given no consequence. Children who saw the model punished later imitated the behaviour less than those who saw it rewarded or ignored. When the children were later offered incentives to reproduce the behaviour, the differences largely disappeared, indicating that the observed consequences influenced performance rather than acquisition of the knowledge. This performance and acquisition distinction is central to the theory, which also names attention, retention and motivation as processes governing observational learning (Bandura, 1977).

More recent work asks how the brain represents others' outcomes. Burke et al. (2010) reported that observational learning engaged separate prediction error signals related to the outcomes of another person's choices (ventromedial prefrontal cortex) and to the choices themselves (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex). This suggests that reinforcement learning computations can be applied to observed outcomes, as well as experienced ones. Individual differences, similarity of the model to the observer, and the observer's own prior experience are thought to modulate the effect.

## Relation to Active Inference
Vicarious reinforcement can be given an Active Inference reading in which observed outcomes of other agents update the observer's beliefs about which actions lead to preferred states, through a shared generative model of the environment. This extends ordinary belief updating, with other agents' observations serving as evidence, and is related to work on social learning and imitation. The connection is conceptual, since specific models fitting the classic findings in Active Inference terms have not been established, and standard reinforcement learning accounts remain the most commonly used computational description (see [[reinforcement_learning]]).
