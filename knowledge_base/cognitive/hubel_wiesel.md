# Hubel and Wiesel

## Overview
David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel carried out single-neuron recordings in the visual cortex of cats and later macaque monkeys from the late 1950s. Their work established that cells in primary visual cortex respond selectively to oriented edges and bars, and that these cells are organised in an orderly anatomical arrangement. They shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work.

## Main findings
- **Orientation selectivity.** Hubel and Wiesel (1959) reported that cells in cat striate cortex respond to a line or edge at a particular orientation in a particular part of the visual field, though they respond poorly to diffuse light, unlike retinal and thalamic cells with circular centre-surround fields (see [[orientation_tuning]]).
- **Simple and complex cells.** In 1962 they distinguished simple cells, with separate excitatory and inhibitory subregions, from complex cells, which respond to an oriented stimulus anywhere within a larger field and often prefer a direction of motion. They proposed a hierarchy in which simple cells are built from aligned inputs from thalamic cells and complex cells from converging simple cells.
- **Columnar organisation.** Cells with similar orientation preference are grouped in columns running through the cortical layers, and neighbouring columns shift gradually in preferred orientation. Eye dominance is also organised in columns (see [[cortical_columns]]).
- **Deprivation.** Later experiments by Wiesel and Hubel showed that closing one eye in young kittens shifted cortical responsiveness to the open eye, and that this sensitivity depends on age, which led to the concept of critical periods (see [[critical_periods]]).

## Influence and qualifications
The feedforward hierarchy became a template for models of object recognition in which selectivity is built stage by stage. Subsequent research has shown that responses of cortical neurons are shaped by recurrent circuits, feedback and context, and that neurons respond to natural scenes in ways not predicted by simple oriented-filter models. Hubel and Wiesel also described cells showing end-stopping, a reduced response when a stimulus extends beyond the classical receptive field.

## Relation to Active Inference
Their findings are explained by predictive-coding models, which is a modelling result and not a proof. Rao and Ballard (1999) showed in a hierarchical predictive-coding network trained on natural images that units develop oriented receptive fields and that end-stopping arises as a consequence of feedback predictions (see [[predictive_coding]]). This indicates that the observations are compatible with, but not unique to, prediction-based accounts.
