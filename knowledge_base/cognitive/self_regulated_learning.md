# Self-Regulated Learning

## Overview
Self-regulated learning (SRL) refers to learners' active control of their own learning: setting goals, selecting strategies, monitoring progress and adjusting when results fall short. Zimmerman (1989) described self-regulated learners as metacognitively, motivationally and behaviourally active participants in their own learning. The concept developed from social cognitive theory and from research on how students differ in the strategies they use.

## Phases and components
Zimmerman (2002) described a cyclical model with three phases. In forethought, the learner analyses the task, sets goals and holds beliefs about self-efficacy. In performance, the learner applies strategies and monitors progress, using self-instruction, attention focusing and self-recording. In self-reflection, the learner judges results, attributes causes for success or failure and reacts, which feeds back into later forethought. Winne and Hadwin (1998) offered an information-processing model in which learners monitor and control their work through repeated cycles of task definition, goal setting, tactics and adaptation.

The core components are:
- **Cognitive strategies**: rehearsal, elaboration, organisation and practising retrieval.
- **Metacognitive monitoring**: judging what is known, as in [[judgment_of_learning]].
- **Motivation and affect**: self-efficacy, interest and the management of frustration.
- **Resource management**: time, effort and help-seeking.

Research has linked these behaviours to academic achievement, although the strength of association depends on the measure used, and self-report instruments are limited by learners' own misjudgments. Interventions that teach strategies and monitoring appear to help, particularly when practised in the subject domain.

## Relation to Active Inference
The monitor-and-adjust cycle can be compared with hierarchical inference, in which a higher level estimates the reliability of lower-level performance and adjusts allocation of effort or choice of strategy. This is an analogy and not an established Active Inference model of education. Formal work on metacognition as inference about one's own confidence is the nearest related literature (see [[metacognition]]).
