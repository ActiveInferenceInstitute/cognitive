# Visual Neuroscience

## Overview
Visual neuroscience studies how the eye and brain turn light into perception and visually guided action. It spans retinal physiology, the thalamic and cortical visual pathways, and the computations they perform. Its findings supply most of the empirical constraints on models of hierarchical perception, including predictive-coding accounts.

## Mechanism
Photoreceptors (rods and cones) transduce light; retinal circuits compute center-surround contrasts and send signals through retinal ganglion cells to the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) and on to primary visual cortex (V1). Parallel channels exist from the retina onward, commonly grouped as magnocellular, parvocellular and koniocellular pathways, with different sensitivities to motion, fine detail and color.

Hubel and Wiesel's recordings in cat V1 described simple cells tuned to oriented edges and complex cells that tolerate small position shifts, and showed an orderly arrangement of orientation and ocular dominance columns. Later work found that V1 responses are strongly modulated by context, stimulus history and attention, so the early "feature detector" picture is incomplete.

Beyond V1, extrastriate cortex is organized in a hierarchy with extensive reciprocal connections (Felleman and Van Essen). Ungerleider and Mishkin proposed a ventral "what" stream and a dorsal "where" stream; Goodale and Milner reframed the split as perception versus action. The reciprocal wiring matters: feedback connections in visual cortex are anatomically as prominent as feedforward ones.

Receptive fields are the basic descriptive unit: the region of visual space and the stimulus features that drive a neuron. Models of the response range from linear-nonlinear filters to normalization models ([[gain_control]]).

## Role in Active Inference
The anatomy of reciprocal, hierarchical connections and the existence of extra-classical receptive-field effects (for example end-stopping and surround suppression) are among the findings cited in support of predictive-coding accounts of cortex. Mapping specific cell classes and laminar structure onto the message passing of [[predictive_processing]] is an active hypothesis, not an established identification. Many visual phenomena also have standard accounts that do not invoke prediction, so the visual-neuroscience literature underdetermines the choice between them.
