# Embodied Simulation

## Overview
Embodied simulation is the idea that cognition draws on partial reenactments of sensory, motor and affective states. To understand a described action, to imagine a scene or to interpret another person's movement, the brain is proposed to reuse the neural systems that would be active in doing, perceiving or feeling the same thing. Barsalou (2008) developed this view as grounded cognition, and Gallese has used the term embodied simulation for accounts of understanding others that rest on shared bodily formats.

## Evidence
- **Language.** Reading action words such as "lick", "pick" or "kick" activates motor and premotor regions in a roughly somatotopic way.
- **Action observation.** Premotor and parietal areas with mirror properties respond both when an action is performed and when it is seen, and they are often cited as a substrate for simulation in action understanding (Gallese & Goldman, 1998).
- **Imagery.** Mentally rehearsing a movement engages many of the circuits used in executing it.

## Critiques
Mahon and Caramazza (2008) argued that sensorimotor activation during conceptual tasks may follow, instead of constitute, the retrieval of abstract concepts, and that patients with severe motor deficits can still understand actions. Debate concerns whether simulation is necessary or only a by-product, how it differs from inference about others' mental states in theory-based accounts (see [[simulation_theory]]), and how it extends to abstract concepts.

## Role in Active Inference
The fit with predictive processing is natural. In Active Inference, a generative model can be run without sensory input to produce imagined or counterfactual sensory and proprioceptive consequences, so simulation is a use of the model. Kilner, Friston and Frith (2007) proposed that the responses of the mirror neuron system can be understood as predictive coding of observed action under the observer's own motor model. This is an active research programme, but the specific neural predictions are not settled, and the claim that cognition is simulation across all domains is contested.
