# Rhythm Perception

## Overview
Rhythm perception is the ability to perceive patterns in the timing of events, most often sounds, and to group them into units. Listeners commonly extract a beat, a regular pulse to which they can tap or move, and a metre, a hierarchy of strong and weak beats. Rhythm is a structure over time intervals, not a single interval, and the same pattern can be perceived with different beats depending on accent and context.

## Components
- **Grouping.** Events are clustered into phrases by their spacing and accents.
- **Beat induction.** A periodic pulse is inferred from the pattern and can persist across silence or syncopation, where notes do not fall on the beat.
- **Metre.** Beats are organised into nested levels, for example duple or triple divisions.
- **Entrainment.** Attention and movement synchronise with the pulse. Large and Jones (1999) described this with internal oscillators whose phases lock to external events, so that attention peaks at expected times.

## Neural basis
Beat perception engages motor structures even without movement. Imaging work showed activity in the basal ganglia and supplementary motor area during beat-based rhythms (Grahn & Brett, 2007), and the cerebellum contributes to timing of short intervals. Neural oscillations in auditory and motor cortex have been proposed to track the beat, though how causal this tracking is remains debated. Impairments of rhythm perception occur in some neurological conditions, including Parkinson's disease, and have been used to motivate rhythmic cueing in rehabilitation.

## Relation to Active Inference
Rhythm provides a clear case of prediction: a metre sets up expectations about when events will occur, and syncopation can be seen as violation of those expectations. Predictive-coding accounts of rhythm and metre have been developed in the music-cognition literature (Vuust & Witek, 2014), which makes this an active research programme rather than a formal derivation within Active Inference.
