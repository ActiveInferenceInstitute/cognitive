# Trust Repair

## Overview
Trust repair is the process by which trust is restored after one party has violated the other's expectations. Research on the topic comes mainly from organisational and social psychology, behavioural economics and the study of close relationships. It starts from the observation that a violation lowers the trustor's willingness to be vulnerable, and asks which actions by the violator, the trustor or third parties can raise it again.

## Mechanisms and findings
Studies distinguish violations by what they signal about the violator. A **competence** violation indicates lack of skill, while an **integrity** violation indicates a failure to follow acceptable principles. Kim, Ferrin, Cooper and Dirks (2004) found in experimental studies that an apology was more effective than a denial for repairing trust after a competence violation, whereas denial was more effective after an integrity violation. A proposed reason is that an admission of an integrity failure suggests a stable negative trait, whereas an admission of an honest mistake does not.

Other repair actions include explanations and accounts, compensation, promises about future conduct, and structural measures such as monitoring or contracts, which substitute control for trust. Schweitzer, Hershey and Bradlow (2006) studied repeated trust games in which a partner broke trust, and examined how promises, apologies and subsequent trustworthy behaviour affected recovery; trust tended to recover only partially, and not necessarily to its earlier level. The effects also depend on timing, on the severity of the harm, on whether the violation was intentional, and on the history of the relationship. Dirks, Lewicki and Zaheer (2009) discuss repair at the level of dyads and organisations, noting that repair needs both parties' participation.

Results from short laboratory scenarios with students may not carry over to long-lived relationships, and measures of trust differ between studies.

## Relation to Active Inference
Trust can be modelled as a belief about another agent's reliability. A violation then produces a large prediction error that drives revision of the generative model, and repair corresponds to later evidence that restores confidence, with slower recovery when the violation is judged to reflect a stable trait. This is an interpretive account in line with general belief-updating models of social learning, not an empirically specific model of repair (see [[prediction_error]], [[trust_games]]).
