# Situation Modification

## Overview
Situation modification is an emotion regulation strategy in which a person changes features of an external situation so that its emotional impact changes. Examples include asking a noisy neighbour to turn the music down, rearranging a workspace, or moving a difficult conversation to a calmer setting. In James Gross's process model of emotion regulation it is the second of five families of strategy, placed early in the emotion-generative sequence because it acts on the situation itself rather than on later stages of the response.

## Place in the process model
The process model orders regulation strategies by the point at which they intervene in the unfolding of an emotion:
- **Situation selection**: approaching or avoiding situations likely to evoke a given emotion.
- **Situation modification**: changing a situation one is already in.
- **Attentional deployment**: directing attention within a situation.
- **Cognitive change**: altering the meaning assigned to a situation (see [[cognitive_reappraisal]]).
- **Response modulation**: influencing physiological, experiential or behavioural responses once they have arisen.

The first two families are antecedent-focused and act on the external environment. Situation modification overlaps with problem-focused coping in the stress and coping literature, where the person tries to change the troubling transaction between self and environment instead of managing the emotion it produces (Lazarus and Folkman, 1984). The boundary with other strategies is not sharp, since a modified situation is also appraised differently.

## Relation to Active Inference
In Active Inference, action is the process by which an agent changes sensory input so that it conforms to predictions or preferred outcomes. Situation modification can be read as an instance of this: an agent that expects distress at a given state of affairs may act to bring the world back toward preferred states, instead of revising its beliefs. Cognitive reappraisal then corresponds more closely to changing beliefs or their precision. This mapping is an interpretive analogy rather than a worked-out model, though Active Inference treatments of emotion and affective valence (see [[affective_neuroscience]]) provide a vocabulary in which it could be formalised.
