# Language Lateralization

## Overview
Language lateralization is the asymmetric organisation of language function across the two cerebral hemispheres. In most people the left hemisphere is dominant for core linguistic operations such as producing and comprehending words and sentences. The pattern was first inferred in the nineteenth century from aphasia following left-hemisphere lesions (see [[brocas_area]] and [[wernickes_area]]) and has since been measured with several methods.

## Evidence
- **Lesion studies.** Left-hemisphere damage in right-handed adults produces aphasia far more often than right-hemisphere damage (see [[aphasia]]).
- **Wada testing.** Temporary anaesthesia of one hemisphere shows which side supports speech, and is used before neurosurgery.
- **Split-brain studies.** Patients with a sectioned corpus callosum, studied by Sperry and Gazzaniga, can name stimuli presented to the left hemisphere but not to the right.
- **Neuroimaging and dichotic listening.** Functional imaging shows left-biased activation in language tasks, and dichotic listening typically yields a right-ear advantage for speech.

## Variation
Dominance is a graded property. The large majority of right-handers are left-dominant, while left-handers show a higher proportion of right or bilateral organisation, though most are still left-dominant. Different components lateralize to different degrees: syntax and phonological processing tend to be strongly left-biased, whereas prosody, some aspects of pragmatics and discourse-level processing recruit the right hemisphere more (see [[prosody_processing]]). After early left-hemisphere injury, language can develop in the right hemisphere, a sign of developmental plasticity.

## Relation to Active Inference
No Active Inference derivation of hemispheric asymmetry for language is established. The topic connects to hierarchical models of speech perception and production, in which different cortical levels encode different timescales of linguistic structure, and one proposal is that hemispheres differ in temporal integration windows. This remains a hypothesis in the speech neuroscience literature rather than a result specific to Active Inference.
