# Choice Paralysis

## Overview
Choice paralysis describes a failure or delay in choosing, or reduced satisfaction with a choice, that is attributed to the number or similarity of the available options. It is discussed under several overlapping names, including choice overload, the paradox of choice and analysis paralysis. The concept links consumer research, cognitive psychology and clinical observation of indecision, but it is not a diagnosis, and the term is used loosely.

## Evidence
- **Original studies.** Iyengar and Lepper (2000) reported that shoppers faced with a large display of jams were less likely to buy than those faced with a small one, and that students given many essay topics were less likely to complete the optional assignment. These findings were influential and are widely cited.
- **Meta-analysis.** A meta-analysis of choice overload studies found a mean effect close to zero with high variability across studies, which indicates that overload occurs under some conditions and not others (Scheibehenne et al., 2010). Proposed moderators include choice set complexity, how well the options can be compared, the decision maker's preferences and expertise, and the time pressure.
- **Popular accounts.** Schwartz (2004) argued that abundant choice can increase regret and decrease satisfaction, particularly for people who try to find the best option (maximisers), though the evidence for these individual differences is mixed.

## Proposed mechanisms
Larger sets raise the effort of comparison, the chance of conflicting attributes and the expected regret of missing a better alternative, any of which may lead people to defer, to choose a default or to choose with less satisfaction (see [[regret_minimization]] and [[heuristics]]). Having no dominant option, as when alternatives are nearly equal in value, can produce long deliberation, although classical decision theory treats near ties as having low cost.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is a model-based interpretation. In Active Inference the choice of a policy follows from a posterior over policies determined by their expected free energy. When several policies have similar expected free energy, or when the agent has low confidence about outcomes, the posterior is flat and no policy is selected with high precision (see [[expected_free_energy]] and [[policy_selection]]). Time spent gathering information to sharpen the distribution can be viewed as epistemic value. This offers a formal vocabulary for indecision but has not been validated against the choice overload literature.
