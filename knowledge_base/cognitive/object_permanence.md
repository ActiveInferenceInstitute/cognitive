# Object Permanence

## Overview
Object permanence is the understanding that objects continue to exist and keep their properties when they cannot be seen, heard or touched. It was described by Jean Piaget (1954) as a major achievement of the sensorimotor period of infancy (see [[sensorimotor_development]]). Without it, objects that leave the field of view would have no continued identity, and goal-directed search for them would not make sense.

## Piaget's account
Piaget observed infants' reactions when an attractive object is hidden. Very young infants stop attending when an object disappears. Around eight to twelve months, infants begin to search for a hidden object, but often make the A-not-B error: after finding an object repeatedly at location A, they search at A again even when they have seen it hidden at B. Piaget proposed that full object permanence, including tracking of invisible displacements, emerges by about eighteen to twenty-four months, and interpreted the errors as evidence that the infant's concept of the object is still tied to action.

## Later research
Looking-time studies using violation-of-expectation methods suggested that infants are sensitive to the continued existence of occluded objects much earlier. Baillargeon, Spelke and Wasserman (1985) found that five-month-old infants looked longer at an event in which a screen appeared to rotate through the location of a hidden box than at a possible event. Such results have been taken to support early core knowledge of objects, but interpretations are disputed. Alternative views stress that looking time may reflect perceptual novelty, and that failures in manual search depend on limits of memory, inhibition and motor planning and not only on concepts. The age at which a robust concept appears therefore depends on the measure used.

## Relation to Active Inference
Object permanence fits naturally with a generative model that includes hidden states representing objects that persist and move even when no sensory evidence is available. When an object is occluded, the model continues to predict its existence and location, and belief updating returns to sensory evidence when the object reappears (see [[hidden_states]] and [[belief_updating]]). Developmental change can be described as acquiring such latent structure through learning. This formulation matches the logic of the phenomenon, but it has not been shown to account for the details of infant search errors or looking times.
