# Korsakoff Syndrome

## Overview
Korsakoff syndrome is a chronic neuropsychiatric condition caused by severe deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1). It is most often associated with long-term heavy alcohol use, although other causes of malnutrition can produce it. It frequently follows an acute episode of Wernicke encephalopathy, characterised by confusion, abnormal eye movements and impaired gait, and the combined picture is called the Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome. The disorder is named after Sergei Korsakoff, a nineteenth-century Russian psychiatrist.

## Clinical features
- **Anterograde amnesia.** New episodic information is acquired very poorly, so events are forgotten within minutes.
- **Retrograde amnesia.** Loss of memories from before onset is variable and often graded, with remote memories better preserved than recent ones.
- **Preserved abilities.** Immediate span, general knowledge and many aspects of language and reasoning are comparatively intact, and procedural and other implicit learning is relatively spared.
- **Confabulation and limited insight.** Some patients produce inaccurate accounts of past events without intent to deceive and may under-recognise their deficit.

## Neuropathology
Damage is typically reported in the mammillary bodies, anterior and mediodorsal thalamic nuclei and connected diencephalic structures, with frequent additional changes in frontal regions. These structures belong to circuits linked to the hippocampal formation, which fits the view that diencephalic and medial temporal lesions can produce related amnesic syndromes (see [[medial_temporal_lobe]]).

## Relevance to memory theory
The syndrome is a standard source of evidence for dissociations between declarative and non-declarative memory: patients can improve on skill tasks and show [[implicit_memory]] effects while being unable to recall the practice sessions. This supports a multiple-systems account of memory.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no dedicated Active Inference model of the disorder. Confabulation can be described informally as the generation of plausible beliefs from a generative model when sensory or mnemonic evidence is weak, so that priors dominate; this is an interpretive analogy in the predictive processing literature, not an established derivation. Treatment and diagnosis belong to clinical care and are outside this page.
