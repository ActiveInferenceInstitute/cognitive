# Peripersonal Space

## Overview
Peripersonal space is the zone around the body within which objects can be touched, grasped or avoided. It is distinguished from extrapersonal space, which lies beyond reach, and it is often treated as the space in which the body interacts with the environment. The concept arose from neurophysiological findings and is now studied in humans with behavioural, lesion and imaging methods (Rizzolatti, Fadiga, Fogassi and Gallese, 1997).

## Neural basis
In macaques, neurons in the ventral premotor cortex, the ventral intraparietal area and parts of the putamen respond both to tactile stimulation of a body part and to visual or auditory stimuli presented near that body part. Their visual receptive fields extend a limited distance out from the skin and often move with the body part rather than with the eyes. This arrangement allows signals in different sensory frames to be combined in coordinates anchored to the body (see [[multisensory_integration]], [[premotor_cortex]], [[posterior_parietal]]). Graziano and Cooke (2006) linked such neurons to defensive and avoidance behaviour. In humans, comparable effects are inferred from crossmodal interference, where a visual stimulus near the hand speeds or biases the detection of touch on that hand.

## Plasticity and tool use
The boundary is not fixed. Training with a tool such as a rake can extend the visual receptive fields of bimodal neurons along the tool in monkeys, and behavioural studies in humans report analogous changes in crossmodal effects (Maravita and Iriki, 2004). Whether this reflects incorporation of the tool into the body schema or a change in attention is debated ([[body_schema]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is a conceptual one. Bufacchi and Iannetti (2018) argued that the field is better described as a map of the value of actions directed at or away from nearby objects than as a fixed region, and this fits the view that perception is organised around prediction of the consequences of action. In Active Inference terms, inference about nearby objects and the body's own state are tied together, and self-recognition and body ownership have been analysed in predictive-coding terms as inference about the causes of multisensory signals (Apps and Tsakiris, 2014). Whether peripersonal neurons implement quantities from the framework has not been directly tested.
