# Perisylvian Network

## Overview
The perisylvian network is the set of cortical regions surrounding the Sylvian (lateral) fissure, together with the white matter tracts that connect them, which is most consistently damaged in language disorders. In the left hemisphere it includes the inferior frontal gyrus ([[brocas_area]]), the posterior superior temporal and adjacent parietal cortex ([[wernickes_area]], supramarginal and angular gyri), and the arcuate fasciculus linking frontal and temporal regions. Damage in this territory produces the classical aphasias (see [[aphasia]]).

## Anatomy and connectivity
Diffusion tractography has shown that the arcuate pathway is not a single bundle. Catani, Jones and ffytche (2005) described a direct segment connecting Broca's and Wernicke's territories and an indirect pathway running through the inferior parietal cortex, via anterior and posterior segments. These perisylvian tracts are typically larger on the left than the right in right-handed people, a difference related to [[language_lateralization]], though the degree of asymmetry varies between individuals. Perisylvian regions on both sides participate in speech perception, repetition and the sequencing of sounds.

## Revisions to the classical model
In the nineteenth-century model, later developed by Wernicke and Geschwind, language is organised as a chain from a comprehension centre to a production centre. Modern work finds this account incomplete. Hickok and Poeppel (2007) proposed a dual-stream organisation, with a ventral stream mapping sound to meaning and a dorsal stream mapping sound to articulation, in which parts of the perisylvian network have sensorimotor roles. Tremblay and Dick (2016) argue that the classical model should be set aside in favour of distributed networks that also extend well beyond the perisylvian cortex, including anterior temporal and frontal regions. Lesion studies likewise show that classical syndromes correspond only loosely to damage in any single region, and that connectivity matters. The term remains useful as a descriptive label for the region most often affected, but it is not a complete map of language function.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is indirect. Dual-stream models are compatible with hierarchical generative models of speech with sensorimotor prediction, and Active Inference accounts of speech production have been proposed, but identification of specific perisylvian regions with specific computational roles remains hypothetical.
