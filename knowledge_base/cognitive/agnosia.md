# Agnosia

## Overview
Agnosia, literally "not knowing", is a neurological condition in which a person fails to recognise stimuli through one sensory modality even though basic sensory functions such as acuity, visual fields or hearing thresholds are largely intact, and language and general intellect are not sufficient to explain the failure. It usually follows focal brain damage, for example from stroke, anoxia or trauma, or neurodegeneration. Heinrich Lissauer's 1890 clinical report distinguished two levels of failure, and Sigmund Freud introduced the term "agnosia" in 1891.

## Forms
- **Apperceptive agnosia.** Perceptual processing is disrupted so that the parts of a stimulus cannot be assembled into a coherent whole. Patients may be unable to copy a drawing or match objects by shape, though they can see local features (see [[perceptual_organization]]).
- **Associative agnosia.** Perception is adequate, with copying and matching preserved, but the percept cannot be linked to stored knowledge about meaning or use.
- **Modality- and category-specific forms.** These include visual object agnosia, prosopagnosia for faces (see [[face_processing]]), auditory agnosia for environmental sounds, and tactile agnosia. Some patients show recognition failures that depend on category.

Damage to the ventral occipitotemporal visual pathway is the typical substrate of visual forms. Studies of the patient D.F., who could not report the orientation of a slot but could post a card through it, led Goodale and Milner to propose a dissociation between ventral perception and dorsal action systems (see [[ventral_stream]]). Diagnosis requires excluding sensory loss, aphasia and dementia.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is interpretive. Recognition is treated in predictive-processing accounts as inference over causes with a hierarchical generative model, so agnosia can be described as a breakdown in inverting that model: either the bottom-up integration of features, as in apperceptive cases, or the access to learned higher-level priors, as in associative cases. This offers a vocabulary for classifying the deficits and for linking them to disconnection, but it restates the neuropsychological findings in new terms and has not led to a distinct, tested account of agnosia.
