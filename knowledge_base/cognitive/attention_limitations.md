# Attention Limitations

## Overview
Attention limitations are the constraints on how much information can be selected, processed to awareness or acted on at one time. They show up as failures to notice or report stimuli that were physically available, and as performance costs when tasks are combined. They are among the most replicable findings in cognitive psychology.

## Principal phenomena
- **Attentional blink**: after a first target is identified in a rapid serial stream, a second target arriving roughly 200-500 ms later is often missed (Raymond, Shapiro and Arnell, 1992; see [[attentional_blink]]).
- **Inattentional blindness**: unexpected, visible objects go unreported when attention is engaged elsewhere, as in the invisible-gorilla task of Simons and Chabris (1999; see [[inattentional_blindness]]).
- **Change blindness**: large changes across a brief interruption go undetected unless attention is directed to them (see [[change_blindness]]).
- **Dual-task interference**: central decision and response-selection stages show a processing bottleneck, so concurrent tasks delay one another (Pashler, 1994; see [[divided_attention]]).

## Competing explanations
Early-selection accounts (Broadbent, 1958) placed a filter early in processing. Late-selection and load-based accounts argue that selection depends on perceptual load, with high load reducing processing of irrelevant stimuli (Lavie, 1995). Resource accounts treat capacity as a graded pool; bottleneck accounts treat one stage as strictly serial. Explanations also differ on whether failures reflect lack of perception, lack of memory for what was perceived, or lack of report. These are not mutually exclusive, and different phenomena may have different loci.

## Role in Active Inference
In predictive-processing terms, unattended or unexpected inputs are those with low assigned precision, so their prediction errors have little influence on beliefs. Under this reading blindness phenomena are consequences of precision allocation, not of a hard bottleneck (see [[attention_allocation]] and [[precision_weighting]]). The account fits the qualitative pattern, but alternative accounts also fit it and quantitative model comparisons are limited. Finite capacity enters more directly through the complexity cost of belief updating (see [[model_complexity]]), though that link is a modelling argument, not an established derivation.
