# Dual Tasks

## Overview
In a dual-task paradigm, participants perform two tasks concurrently or with overlapping timing, and performance is compared with each task done alone. The decline in speed or accuracy is the dual-task cost. The method is used to infer whether two tasks draw on shared mental resources, to estimate the demands of a task, and to study how attention is divided. In ordinary language it is the laboratory counterpart of multitasking.

## Theoretical accounts
- **Capacity or resource theories.** Kahneman (1973) described attention as a limited pool of effort that can be allocated across activities. Wickens' multiple resource model refined this by proposing separate resources defined by dimensions such as processing stage, input modality and response type, so interference should be greater between tasks that compete for the same resource.
- **Bottleneck theories.** Pashler (1994) reviewed evidence that a central stage, particularly response selection, can handle only one task at a time. In the psychological refractory period paradigm two stimuli are presented close together, and responses to the second slow down as the interval between them shrinks, consistent with the second task waiting for the central stage.
- **Executive control accounts.** Costs may arise from scheduling and coordination processes or from crosstalk between similar task representations.

## Findings
Costs are smaller when tasks are dissimilar, well practised or automatic, and larger when both require central decision-making or working memory. Extended practice can reduce or, in some studies, nearly remove costs, which has been attributed to automatisation or to more efficient scheduling. In applied settings, dual-task methods are used to study driving while using a phone and in clinical work on gait while performing a cognitive task.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference does not offer a settled account of dual-task interference. A natural reading is that attention corresponds to the allocation of precision, and that competing tasks cannot both be assigned high precision at once, which would produce costs. This idea is an interpretation, not an experimentally confirmed result of the framework.
