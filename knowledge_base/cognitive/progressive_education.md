# Progressive Education

## Overview
Progressive education refers to a loosely connected body of educational reforms, mainly from the late nineteenth and the twentieth century, that opposed schooling built chiefly on recitation, fixed curricula and external discipline. Its proponents argued that education should start from the learner's experience and interests, develop practical and social capacities along with knowledge, and prepare students for participation in a democratic society. The movement was never a single programme, and schools that called themselves progressive differed widely in practice (Cremin, 1961).

## Dewey's formulation
The philosopher John Dewey is the figure most closely associated with the theory, and it is linked to his pragmatism ([[pragmatism]]). In *Democracy and Education* (1916) he described education as the reconstruction of experience and treated the school as a community in which democratic habits are practised. In *Experience and Education* (1938) he held that experience is educative only when it is continuous with later experience and carefully organised, and he criticised both traditional instruction and progressive schools that treated freedom of the child as a replacement for structure. His wider psychology rejected a sharp split between sensation and action, a view first stated in his paper on the reflex arc (Dewey, 1896).

## Characteristic practices
Practices often associated with the tradition include:
- **Learning by doing**, in which projects, workshops and inquiry provide the context for acquiring skills ([[project_based_learning]], [[experiential_learning]]).
- **Attention to the learner's interests** as a starting point for curriculum.
- **Collaboration and shared governance** in the classroom.

## Evaluation and criticism
Critics argue that instruction with minimal guidance is less effective for novices than explicit instruction, citing limits of working memory (Kirschner, Sweller and Clark, 2006). Supporters respond that progressive aims need not exclude guidance and that Dewey himself called for structure. Research on specific methods has mixed results that depend on the age of learners, the subject and the quality of implementation, so general claims in either direction are rarely well supported.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is a loose analogy. Dewey's emphasis on action as the basis of knowing, and learning by exploration, resemble the Active Inference view that organisms acquire models of the world by acting and sampling it. This resemblance is thematic and does not amount to an educational theory derived from the framework. See [[constructivist_learning_theory]] for a closer cognitive counterpart.
