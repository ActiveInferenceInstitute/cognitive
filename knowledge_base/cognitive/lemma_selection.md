# Lemma Selection

## Overview
In models of speech production, a lemma is the abstract part of a lexical entry that specifies a word's syntactic properties, such as word class and grammatical gender, and links it to a concept. It is distinct from the word form, which specifies the phonological segments. Lemma selection is the step in which, given a concept to be expressed, one lemma is chosen from among the active candidates. [[lemma_retrieval]] covers access to the entry; this page concerns the competition and choice among candidates.

## The standard model
Levelt (1989) and Levelt, Roelofs and Meyer (1999) describe a staged process: conceptual preparation, then lemma selection, then retrieval of the word form, then syllabification and articulation. Activating a concept spreads activation to semantically related lemmas, so that "dog" also partly activates "cat". Selection is competitive, with the chosen lemma being the one whose activation is highest relative to its rivals. Evidence cited for this comes from picture-word interference, in which a semantically related distractor word slows picture naming, and from tip-of-the-tongue states, in which speakers retrieve syntactic properties such as gender without the sound form.

## Contested points
The most debated questions are whether lemma and form stages are strictly serial and whether lemmas exist as a separate level. Dell (1986) proposed an interactive account in which activation flows both forward and back between levels, which better accommodates some speech-error patterns such as mixed errors. Caramazza (1997) argued that the data are compatible with models without a lemma level, in which syntactic information is accessed from word-form nodes. Proponents of the staged account respond with the tip-of-the-tongue findings and with patterns from aphasia. The debate has been conducted largely on reaction-time and error data, and both families of models continue to be developed.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is analogical. Choosing a word could be cast as inference over lexical states given a communicative intention, with competition resolved by posterior probability, and this is consistent with hierarchical generative models of language. No tested Active Inference model of lemma selection is established.
