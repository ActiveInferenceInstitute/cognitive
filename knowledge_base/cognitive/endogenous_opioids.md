# Endogenous Opioids

## Overview
Endogenous opioids are peptides made by the body that bind opioid receptors, the same receptors on which morphine and other opiate drugs act. The main families are the enkephalins, the endorphins (notably beta-endorphin) and the dynorphins, with endomorphins as a further group. Their discovery in the mid-1970s, including the isolation of two enkephalins from brain tissue (Hughes and colleagues, 1975), explained how opiate drugs could act on the nervous system.

## Receptors and functions
The peptides act mainly on three G-protein-coupled receptor types, mu, delta and kappa, which generally reduce neuronal excitability and neurotransmitter release. Established roles include modulation of pain, in circuits that run through regions such as the periaqueductal gray and spinal cord, regulation of stress responses, effects on mood and on reward and hedonic processing, and modulation of autonomic and endocrine function. Mu receptors are the primary target for opiate analgesics, and kappa receptor activation is often associated with dysphoric effects. Opioid peptide systems also take part in the regulation of food intake and are implicated in addiction.

## Social bonding hypothesis
Panksepp and colleagues (1978) observed that low doses of opiates reduced separation distress calls in young animals, and proposed that opioids underlie social attachment. The brain opioid theory of social attachment, reviewed by Machin and Dunbar (2011), extends this idea: social contact, such as grooming in primates, releases endorphins that produce a rewarding calm, which reinforces bonds. Human studies using pharmacological blockade or neuroimaging of opioid receptors have reported links between opioid signalling and social contact or laughter, but sample sizes are often small and interpretation is debated. The theory is a hypothesis with supporting evidence and some conflicting findings, not an established account.

## Relation to Active Inference
No settled account relates endogenous opioids to Active Inference. Some authors suggest that opioid and dopaminergic systems may be involved in signalling hedonic value, the precision of affective predictions, or the resolution of uncertainty, but these proposals are speculative and tentative. Within the vault, the topic bears on how social reward and cooperation are implemented biologically.
