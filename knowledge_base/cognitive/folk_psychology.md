# Folk Psychology

## Overview
Folk psychology is the ordinary framework by which people explain and predict behaviour in terms of mental states, chiefly beliefs, desires, intentions and emotions. We say that someone took an umbrella because they wanted to stay dry and believed it would rain. The term names both the practice and, in some philosophical accounts, a body of tacit knowledge or theory that underlies it. It is also called commonsense psychology, belief-desire psychology and, in developmental work, theory of mind.

## Accounts of how it works
- **Theory-theory.** Mindreading relies on an implicit theory with laws linking mental states to inputs and actions. Developmental versions hold that children revise this theory as evidence accumulates, with the false-belief task a standard test of progress.
- **Simulation theory.** People use their own cognitive resources as a model of others, running their decision-making processes offline with imagined beliefs and desires. Mirror-neuron research is sometimes linked to this view, though the connection is debated.
- **Interaction and narrative accounts.** Some authors argue that much social understanding comes from direct perception of intentions in interaction and from narratives about reasons, and not from theorising or simulation.
Premack and Woodruff (1978) introduced the question of whether non-human animals have a theory of mind, and Dennett (1987) described the "intentional stance", in which attributing beliefs and desires is a useful predictive strategy whether or not they are inner states.

## Philosophical status
Eliminative materialists such as Paul Churchland (1981) argue that folk psychology is a false empirical theory that mature neuroscience will replace, much as folk physics was replaced. Defenders reply that belief-desire explanation is indispensable, that it is not a theory in the relevant sense, or that its categories could be vindicated at a higher level of description, as functionalist views suggest. Davies and Stone (1995) collect the main positions of the theory-simulation debate.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an active research programme. Active Inference models social understanding as inference about another agent's hidden states and policies, which resembles the inverse planning accounts of mindreading. In this reading, beliefs and desires attributed to others correspond to beliefs and preferences in their generative model. Whether the framework supports a theory-based, simulation-based or other account of folk psychology remains open, as does its relation to the eliminativist question.
