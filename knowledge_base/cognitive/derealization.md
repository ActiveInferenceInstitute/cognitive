# Derealization

## Overview
Derealization is the feeling that the external world is unreal, strange, distant or lifeless. Surroundings may seem like a film set, appear flat or foggy, or seem separated from the observer by a glass wall. Perception itself is intact and the person recognises that the feeling is subjective. It is usually discussed together with depersonalization, which concerns the self instead of the world, and the two co-occur often.

## Occurrence and classification
Short episodes are common, especially during fatigue, acute stress, panic or after intense experiences, and may also follow some substances or accompany migraine and seizure conditions. The DSM-5-TR groups persistent or recurrent, distressing derealization with depersonalization in a single dissociative disorder, provided that it is not better explained by another condition. This entry is descriptive and does not offer diagnosis or treatment guidance.

## Proposed mechanisms
Accounts emphasise altered emotional and attentional processing of sensory input. Reported findings include reduced emotional responses to aversive stimuli and differences in activity in visual association, prefrontal and limbic regions, but results are modest and not uniform across studies. Reviews such as Sierra and Berrios (1998) treated depersonalization and derealization as related syndromes with a shared neurobiological hypothesis, while noting differences in their emphasis.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive-processing proposals suggest that the sense of a vivid, real world depends on successful prediction of the sensory and bodily consequences of interacting with it. Seth and colleagues' interoceptive model of presence applies this idea to loss of the sense of reality: if affective and interoceptive predictions are not confirmed, or are held with low precision, the world may feel as though it lacks significance. The proposal is a hypothesis under investigation, supported by indirect evidence, and not an established explanation.
