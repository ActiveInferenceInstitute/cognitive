# Construct Validity

## Overview
Construct validity concerns whether a test, task or manipulation measures the theoretical construct it claims to measure. Many central terms in psychology and cognitive science, such as working memory, anxiety or attention, are not directly observable, so they are studied through operational procedures (see [[operationalism]]). Construct validity asks whether inferences from the procedure to the construct are justified. The concept was formalised by Cronbach and Meehl (1955) in the context of psychological testing.

## Sources of evidence
Cronbach and Meehl argued that validity is established by embedding a construct in a network of lawful relations, called the nomological network, and checking that the measure behaves as the network predicts. Evidence is usually accumulated from several directions:
- **Convergent validity**: measures of the same construct correlate.
- **Discriminant validity**: measures of distinct constructs do not correlate more than expected.
- **Content validity**: the items sample the domain of the construct adequately.
- **Predictive and criterion validity**: scores forecast relevant outcomes.
- **Internal structure**: factor structure or dimensionality matches the theory.

Validity is therefore not a property that a test either has or lacks. It is a matter of degree, and it applies to interpretations of scores, not to instruments alone. Borsboom, Mellenbergh and van Heerden (2004) offered a contrasting view in which a test is valid if the attribute exists and variations in it causally produce variations in the outcome.

## Threats
Common threats include under-representation, where the measure omits part of the construct, and construct-irrelevant variance, where it also captures other abilities, strategies or motivation. In experiments, participants may respond to perceived demand, and a single task may confound the process of interest with task-specific demands. Shadish, Cook and Campbell (2002) treat construct validity of causes and effects as one of four validity types, alongside statistical conclusion, internal and external validity (see [[ecological_validity]]).

## Relevance to computational modelling
Model parameters raise the same issue. When a fitted parameter such as a precision or learning rate is interpreted as a cognitive or clinical construct, evidence is needed that it is identifiable, reliable across tasks and related to external criteria. In Active Inference modelling this is an active methodological concern, not a settled one, and recovery studies and cross-task comparisons are the usual checks.
