# Metalinguistic Awareness

## Overview
Metalinguistic awareness is the ability to reflect on language itself rather than simply using it to communicate. A speaker who judges that a sentence is ungrammatical, notices that two words rhyme, or explains that a word has more than one meaning is drawing on this ability. It is a specialised kind of [[metacognition]] concerned with linguistic form. Most children use language fluently well before they can analyse it.

## Components
- **Phonological awareness.** Noticing and manipulating sounds, for example segmenting a word into phonemes or judging rhymes (see [[phonology]]).
- **Word awareness.** Recognising words as units distinct from their referents, and that a long word need not name a large object.
- **Syntactic awareness.** Judging and correcting grammaticality and recognising structural ambiguity (see [[grammar]]).
- **Pragmatic awareness.** Reflecting on the intended meaning of utterances, irony and appropriateness in context.

## Development and literacy
Awareness of sound structure usually grows during the preschool and early school years. Phonological awareness is among the best early predictors of success in learning to read, and training studies support a causal contribution (Bradley and Bryant, 1983). The relation is reciprocal, since learning an alphabetic script also sharpens awareness of phonemes. Difficulties in phonological awareness are characteristic of many people with [[dyslexia]].

## Bilingualism
Bialystok proposed that bilingual children often show advantages on tasks requiring control of attention to language form, especially when the task includes misleading cues, which she attributed to experience in selecting between two languages (Bialystok, 2001). The generality of bilingual advantages in cognitive control is contested in the wider literature.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy. Explicit reflection on language involves holding linguistic representations as objects of higher-order beliefs, which fits hierarchical models in which upper levels represent properties of lower-level processing. No established Active Inference model of metalinguistic tasks exists.
