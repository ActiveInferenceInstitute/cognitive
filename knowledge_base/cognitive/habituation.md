# Habituation

## Overview
Habituation is a decline in response to a stimulus that is presented repeatedly and turns out to be unimportant. It is often called the simplest form of learning and is found across species from single-celled organisms to humans, for instance when a startle response to a sound diminishes over repetitions. It lets an organism stop spending resources on inputs that carry no useful consequences and keeps responsiveness for new events.

## Properties
Thompson and Spencer (1966) listed characteristic features, later revised by Rankin and colleagues (2009).
- Response decrement typically follows a negative exponential-like course and is stronger with weaker or more frequent stimuli.
- It shows spontaneous recovery after a rest interval.
- A different, strong stimulus can restore the response, which is called dishabituation.
- Habituation can be stimulus specific, so a change in a feature of the stimulus renews responding.
- Further training after the response has reached floor can lead to slower recovery.

It is distinguished from sensory fatigue and from [[sensory_adaptation]] at receptors, because the response to a different stimulus remains intact, which implicates changes in central processing. Mechanistically, the best-studied case is the gill-withdrawal reflex in the sea slug Aplysia, where habituation involves reduced transmitter release at a synapse. In humans, habituation is measured through orienting responses, [[skin_conductance]], startle and looking time in infants, which is used to test whether infants discriminate stimuli. Sokolov (1963) proposed that the nervous system builds a neuronal model of a repeated stimulus and responds when input mismatches the model. See [[learning_theory]].

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is close and conceptually natural. Sokolov's model-comparison idea anticipates predictive coding: as a stimulus becomes predictable, prediction error shrinks and the response declines, while a deviant stimulus produces mismatch and renewed responding (see [[prediction_error]] and [[predictive_coding]]). Repetition suppression and mismatch responses in neuroimaging are often interpreted this way, although alternative accounts based on neural fatigue or sharpening of representations make overlapping predictions, so the data do not uniquely favour predictive coding. In Active Inference terms, a stable generative model leaves little to be learned, and attention and exploration shift to sources of higher expected information gain. See [[emotional_processing]] for affective habituation.
