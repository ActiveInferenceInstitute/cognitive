# Economic Games

## Overview
Economic games are laboratory tasks in which participants make choices with real or hypothetical monetary consequences that depend on the choices of others. They turn social behaviour such as trust, fairness, reciprocity and cooperation into measurable quantities, and are used in behavioural economics, social psychology and neuroscience. Their predictions are derived from game theory, and deviations from the self-interested equilibrium are the main object of study.

## Common games
- **Prisoner's dilemma.** Two players each choose to cooperate or defect. With payoffs T (temptation), R (reward), P (punishment) and S (sucker) ordered $T > R > P > S$, defection is the dominant strategy in a single round although mutual cooperation pays more. Repetition allows reciprocal strategies.
- **Ultimatum game.** A proposer offers a split of a sum and a responder accepts or rejects; rejection gives both nothing. A payoff-maximising responder would accept any positive offer, but low offers are commonly rejected (Guth, Schmittberger and Schwarze, 1982).
- **Dictator game.** The proposer decides the split alone, which separates generosity from strategic fear of rejection.
- **Trust game.** An investor sends money that is multiplied, and a trustee decides how much to return, measuring trust and trustworthiness (Berg, Dickhaut and McCabe, 1995).
- **Public goods game.** Players contribute to a shared pool that is multiplied and divided equally, so free-riding is individually tempting; costly punishment of free riders is often examined.

Results vary with stakes, anonymity, culture and framing, and behaviour in a game does not always predict behaviour outside the laboratory.

## Role in Active Inference
Economic games supply a test bed for models of other minds. In an Active Inference account, a player maintains beliefs about the partner's type or intentions and selects actions that minimise expected free energy under those beliefs, so reputation and trust emerge from belief updating about an interlocutor. Related work uses recursive reasoning about the opponent's model of oneself. Such models fit behaviour in iterated games, but the framework is one of several competing formalisms (including inequity aversion and reinforcement learning), and its distinctive advantage here remains under evaluation.
