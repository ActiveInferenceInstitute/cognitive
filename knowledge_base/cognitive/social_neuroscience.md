# Social Neuroscience

## Overview
Social neuroscience is an interdisciplinary field that studies the neural, hormonal, and physiological mechanisms underlying social behaviour and the way social context shapes brain and body. The term and programme were articulated by Cacioppo and Berntson in 1992, bringing together social psychology and neuroscience. It builds on [[social_cognition]] and [[cognitive_neuroscience]].

## Mechanism
Topics include perceiving other people (faces, bodies, gaze, [[emotion_recognition]]), understanding their minds ([[theory_of_mind]], [[perspective_taking]]), understanding their actions ([[imitation]]), empathy and shared affect, social reward and pain, group membership and [[social_norms]], and cooperation and competition ([[cooperation]]). Reported circuitry includes the mentalizing network ([[medial_prefrontal]], temporoparietal junction, [[inferior_parietal]]), the amygdala, the insula, the [[anterior_cingulate]], and the reward system. Hormonal work includes [[oxytocin]].

Methods include fMRI, EEG and MEG, lesion studies, transcranial stimulation, pharmacological manipulation, and single-neuron recording in animals. Hyperscanning records brain activity from two or more interacting people at once, enabling study of inter-brain synchrony ([[neural_synchrony]]), though causal interpretation of synchrony is debated. The social brain hypothesis (Dunbar) links primate neocortex size to social group size, although its generality and the direction of causation are contested. Methodological concerns include small samples, reverse inference from region to function, and the artificiality of laboratory social situations, which have driven work using real interaction and naturalistic stimuli.

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference treats social interaction as inference in a shared environment populated by other agents with their own generative models. Two agents who each predict the other can coordinate, converge on shared beliefs, and achieve synchrony; Friston and Frith's account of communication is the best-known statement. Norms, culture, and roles are cast as shared priors that make others' behaviour predictable (see [[social_norms]]). Hyperscanning findings of inter-brain synchrony are sometimes interpreted this way. The framework offers a unifying vocabulary and generates formal models ([[multi_agent_active_inference]]), but much of its social application is theoretical and simulation-based; direct tests that discriminate it from alternative accounts remain rare.
