# Auditory Streaming

## Overview
The sound reaching the ears is a sum of waveforms from all active sources, and the auditory system must separate it into the contributions of individual sources. Auditory streaming is the perceptual result: components of the mixture are grouped into streams, each experienced as a coherent sound source such as one voice, an instrument or a footstep sequence. Bregman (1990) named the broader problem auditory scene analysis and framed it by analogy with visual grouping (see [[gestalt_principles]]).

## Grouping cues
- **Simultaneous grouping.** Components that start together, share a harmonic series or have common amplitude modulation tend to fuse into one sound. A component that starts earlier or is mistuned can be heard separately.
- **Sequential grouping.** Tones close in frequency, timbre and location tend to form one stream. In the classic alternating-tone paradigm, two tones A and B played in a repeating pattern are heard as one galloping sequence when the frequency difference is small and as two separate streams when it is large and the presentation is fast.
- **Bistability.** For intermediate settings, listeners experience alternation between one-stream and two-stream percepts, comparable to [[bistable_perception]] in vision.
- **Spatial and attentional factors.** Location cues help, and attention can select a stream and affect the build-up of segregation, although some streaming occurs without attention (see [[selective_attention]]).
- **Familiarity.** Schemas for speech and music allow listeners to hear sources they know in noise.

## The cocktail party problem
Cherry (1953) posed the problem of following one talker among several. Behavioural work shows that listeners use spatial separation, voice pitch and timing differences, as well as lip movements, to follow the target (see [[lip_reading]] and [[speech_perception]]). Neural recordings show that cortical activity tracks the attended speech stream more strongly than the ignored one.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an active research area. Winkler, Denham and Nelken (2009) proposed that auditory scene analysis operates by forming and testing predictive regularity representations, in which the brain maintains competing hypotheses about the sources in the scene and selects those that best predict incoming sounds (see [[predictive_processing]] and [[prediction_error]]). Competing stream hypotheses and switching between them resemble the inference accounts of visual bistability. The proposal has support from deviance-detection studies but is one of several accounts, and it is not a derivation from the free energy principle.
