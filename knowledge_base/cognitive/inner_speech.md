# Inner Speech

## Overview
Inner speech is the experience of silently talking to oneself in one's own voice, sometimes called verbal thought or the inner voice. It is a common component of everyday mental life, used for planning, reasoning, remembering, self-regulation and rehearsal. People differ widely in how much they report it and in its form, which ranges from fully worded sentences to condensed fragments of meaning.

## Development and functions
Vygotsky (1934/1986) proposed that inner speech develops from the egocentric speech that children speak aloud during tasks. It becomes internalised and abbreviated, and takes on a role in guiding thought and behaviour. Alderson-Day and Fernyhough (2015) review the evidence for several functions, including self-regulation, working memory and rehearsal, task switching, and self-reflection, and emphasise varied phenomenology across individuals. Experience-sampling studies find that its frequency differs substantially between people and situations. Rehearsal of verbal material in short-term memory is described by Baddeley's phonological loop (see [[phonological_loop]]).

## Neural basis
Inner speech engages parts of the left inferior frontal gyrus and related language and motor-planning regions, and it activates auditory areas to a lesser degree than overt speech. Its relation to the motor system is shown by evidence that silent speech can produce small muscle activity and that interfering with articulation affects some verbal tasks, but inner speech can occur without detectable articulation. Whether it should be thought of as speech production without output or as a distinct form of representation is debated.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive accounts treat inner speech as covert speech action: the motor and auditory predictions of a spoken utterance are generated without the utterance being executed. A theory of auditory verbal hallucinations in schizophrenia proposes that inner speech is misattributed to an external source when the usual prediction of its sensory consequences fails (Frith, 1992), a mechanism related to [[efference_copy]] and [[corollary_discharge]]. Predictive-processing models of hallucinations reformulate this in terms of unusually strong priors or altered precision (Corlett et al., 2019). These are active research programmes with partial support, and the origin of verbal hallucinations is not settled.
