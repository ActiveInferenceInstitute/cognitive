# Theory of Mind

## Overview
Theory of mind (ToM), also called mentalizing, is the capacity to attribute mental states such as beliefs, desires, intentions, and emotions to oneself and others, and to use those attributions to explain and predict behaviour. The term comes from Premack and Woodruff's 1978 question of whether chimpanzees have a theory of mind.

## Mechanism
The classic test is the false-belief task: an agent holds a belief that the observer knows to be false (in the Sally-Anne paradigm, a character searches for an object where she left it, not where it now is), and the participant must predict her behaviour from her belief rather than from reality. In standard verbal versions, children typically pass at around four to five years; whether infants show implicit forms of belief tracking in looking-time and anticipatory-looking paradigms remains debated. Later-developing abilities include second-order beliefs ("she thinks that he thinks"), irony, and faux pas.

Neuroimaging repeatedly implicates a mentalizing network: the temporoparietal junction (overlapping [[inferior_parietal]] cortex), the [[medial_prefrontal]] cortex, the posterior cingulate and precuneus, and the superior temporal sulcus and anterior temporal poles. Distinctions are often made between cognitive ToM (inferring beliefs and intentions) and affective ToM (inferring feelings), the latter overlapping with [[emotion_recognition]].

## Role in Active Inference
In Active Inference, mentalizing is treated as inference about the hidden causes of another agent's behaviour: the observer holds a generative model of the other agent, itself a model that generates actions from beliefs and preferences, and inverts it from observed actions. Recursive ("I think that you think") reasoning is a nested model of this kind. Moutoussis and colleagues formalised interpersonal inference in this way, and Friston and Frith argued that communication works when two agents share a generative model, so each can predict the other. These are formal proposals supported mainly by simulation. The link between Active Inference and the empirical ToM literature is plausible rather than demonstrated, and other computational accounts (inverse planning, Bayesian ToM, simulation theory) address the same phenomena. See [[multi_agent_active_inference]] for the multi-agent modelling context.
