# Stress Hormones

## Overview
Stress hormones are the chemical messengers that mobilise the body to deal with real or anticipated challenges. Two systems are involved. The sympathetic-adrenal-medullary system releases adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline within seconds, raising heart rate, blood pressure and energy availability. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis acts over minutes to hours: the hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone, the pituitary releases adrenocorticotropic hormone, and the adrenal cortex releases glucocorticoids, principally cortisol in humans and corticosterone in rodents (see [[cortisol]]).

## Actions
Selye (1936) described a general response to diverse noxious agents, which led to the modern concept of stress. Sapolsky, Romero and Munck (2000) argued that glucocorticoids have actions that are permissive, suppressive, stimulatory and preparative, and not simply suppressive: they restrain some stress-activated defence systems while priming others. Their release follows a daily rhythm, with a peak shortly after waking, and is limited by negative feedback through glucocorticoid receptors in the brain and pituitary.

## Effects on cognition
Stress hormones act on brain regions with high receptor densities, including the hippocampus, amygdala and prefrontal cortex. Moderate, brief release tends to improve alertness and the consolidation of emotionally arousing events, a process dependent on the amygdala (see [[emotional_memory]]), whereas strong or prolonged exposure can impair working memory and memory retrieval and, in animals, produce changes in hippocampal and prefrontal structure. Effects depend on timing, dose, sex and individual history, and human results are variable.

## Allostasis
McEwen (2007) reviewed allostasis, stability through change, as a framework: the hormones that help an organism adapt in the short term cause wear, termed allostatic load, when chronically activated. Control of the stress system therefore involves a trade-off between short-term benefit and long-term cost.

## Relation to Active Inference
In Active Inference, the brain regulates internal states by predicting them and acting to keep them within expected bounds, which is allostasis expressed as interoceptive inference. Peters, McEwen and Friston (2017) proposed that uncertainty, especially about whether the organism can control its situation, drives stress responses, and that chronic uncertainty could contribute to disease. This is a theoretical proposal that organises existing findings; it does not replace the endocrine description above.
