# Fluid Intelligence

## Overview
Fluid intelligence is the ability to reason, detect patterns and solve unfamiliar problems without depending on previously learned knowledge or skills. The distinction between fluid and crystallized abilities was proposed by Raymond Cattell and developed with John Horn, and it remains a core component of the Cattell-Horn-Carroll model of cognitive abilities. In that model fluid reasoning is a broad ability sitting below general intelligence.

## Measurement
Fluid intelligence is assessed with tasks designed to minimise cultural and educational content, such as matrix reasoning problems (for example Raven's Progressive Matrices), series completion, and classification of abstract figures. Performance is timed or untimed depending on the instrument. In factor-analytic work, fluid reasoning correlates very highly with general intelligence, and some researchers regard the two as nearly equivalent, though this is disputed.

## Development and correlates
Average performance on fluid tasks rises through childhood and adolescence, peaks in early adulthood, and declines gradually in later life, whereas crystallized abilities tend to be maintained longer. Fluid reasoning is strongly related to working memory capacity and to executive attention, and measures of the two share much of their variance. Whether the relation reflects a common mechanism, such as the ability to maintain goals in the face of interference, remains debated. Evidence that training on working memory tasks transfers to fluid reasoning is mixed, and meta-analyses generally find small or inconsistent transfer.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference offers no established account of fluid intelligence. A speculative mapping treats fluid reasoning as the capacity to infer the structure of a new situation quickly, which in generative-model terms resembles structure learning and flexible belief updating, while crystallized knowledge corresponds to the content of a model already learned. This is an analogy that suggests hypotheses, and it has not been tested as an account of psychometric data.
