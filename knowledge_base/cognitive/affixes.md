# Affixes

## Overview
An affix is a bound morpheme, a meaningful element that cannot stand alone as a word and that attaches to a root or stem to change its meaning or grammatical function. Affixes are the main device of affixal morphology. English *un-* in *unlock*, *-er* in *teacher* and *-s* in *dogs* are familiar examples. The study of word structure and formation is called morphology, and affixes are one of its central objects.

## Types
- **By position.** Prefixes precede the base (*re-* in *rewrite*), suffixes follow it (*-ness* in *kindness*), infixes occur inside it (as in some Austronesian languages), and circumfixes surround it (as in German participles such as *ge-...-t*). Languages differ widely in which positions they use.
- **By function.** Inflectional affixes mark grammatical categories such as number, tense or case, and do not usually create a new lexeme (*walk, walked*). Derivational affixes create new words and often change the word class (*happy, happiness*).
- **Productivity.** Some affixes combine freely with new bases, for example English *-ness*, while others are restricted to a few words. Productivity is graded and depends on phonological, semantic and frequency factors.
- **Allomorphy.** One affix may take different forms depending on its context, as the English plural is pronounced differently in *cats*, *dogs* and *horses* (see [[phonology]]).

## Processing and acquisition
Psycholinguistic research asks whether complex words are stored whole, decomposed into stem and affix, or both. Evidence from priming and eye movements suggests that decomposition plays a role in recognising many transparent derived words, with whole-word storage for frequent or opaque forms, and models differ on the details (see [[lexical_access]] and [[psycholinguistics]]). Children acquire inflectional affixes gradually, and morphological awareness is associated with reading development (see [[metalinguistic_awareness]] and [[language_learning]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no established Active Inference account specific to affixes. Language is modelled in this framework as hierarchical generative processing, in which higher levels predict lower-level units such as words and sounds, and recognition of a stem plausibly constrains the affixes that follow. That is an analogy with predictive accounts of [[language_comprehension]] and has not been tested for morphology in particular.
