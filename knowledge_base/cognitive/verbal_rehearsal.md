# Verbal Rehearsal

## Overview
Verbal rehearsal is the silent or spoken repetition of words, digits or other verbal items so that they remain available for a short time. A familiar example is repeating a telephone number between hearing it and dialling. In the multicomponent model of working memory it is the function of the articulatory control process of the [[phonological_loop]], which refreshes the decaying traces held in a phonological store (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974). The general topic of [[rehearsal]] is wider and includes non-verbal forms.

## Evidence
- **Word-length effect.** Immediate serial recall is better for lists of short words than for lists of long words. Baddeley, Thomson and Buchanan (1975) found that recall span was related to speech rate and approximated the number of words that could be said in a fixed short time, which was consistent with a time-limited rehearsal loop.
- **Articulatory suppression.** Repeating an irrelevant word aloud ("the, the, the") during presentation prevents rehearsal. It reduces recall and abolishes the word-length effect for visually presented lists, and it removes the effect of phonological similarity for visual input.
- **Maintenance and elaboration.** Maintenance rehearsal, which merely repeats, differs from elaborative processing, which relates items to meaning. According to the levels-of-processing framework, long-term retention depends more on the depth of processing than on the amount of repetition (Craik and Lockhart, 1972).

Verbal rehearsal is contested as the only maintenance mechanism. Alternative accounts hold that attention can refresh memory traces without articulation, and that decay is not the main source of forgetting.

## Neural basis
Imaging studies of verbal working memory implicate left inferior frontal regions including [[brocas_area]], premotor cortex, the inferior parietal cortex ([[supramarginal]]) and the cerebellum. The frontal and premotor activity is usually associated with the articulatory process and the parietal activity with the store (Paulesu et al., 1993).

## Relation to Active Inference
Rehearsal can be described as self-generated speech whose predicted sensory consequences keep a belief about a word sequence active. Under this description, covert rehearsal is a form of action, in the Active Inference sense, directed at the agent's own beliefs. This is an interpretation and not a proposed model with distinguishing predictions. The established explanations of rehearsal remain those of the working memory literature.
