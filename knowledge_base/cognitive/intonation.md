# Intonation

## Overview
Intonation is the use of variation in pitch across phrases and sentences, produced mainly by changes in the fundamental frequency of the voice. It is one component of prosody, together with duration, loudness and rhythm (see [[prosody_processing]]). Intonation operates above the level of the individual word, and in languages that use pitch to distinguish word meanings, such as Mandarin, intonation operates alongside lexical tone.

## Functions
Intonation contributes to meaning in several ways that are partly independent of the words:
- **Sentence type and attitude.** A rising contour is common in questions in many languages, and a falling contour in statements, although the pattern varies across languages and dialects.
- **Focus and information structure.** Prominent pitch movement marks the word or phrase that is new or contrasted: in "Mary bought a BOOK" the accent signals what is at issue.
- **Phrasing.** Pitch resets and boundary tones mark the grouping of words into phrases, which can resolve syntactic ambiguity.
- **Affect and stance.** Pitch range and shape convey emotion, politeness, certainty and speaker attitude (see [[pragmatics]]).

## Linguistic analysis
In the autosegmental-metrical approach, associated with Pierrehumbert and described comprehensively by Ladd (2008), a contour is analysed as a sequence of discrete tones, mainly pitch accents attached to stressed syllables and boundary tones at phrase edges. This approach underlies annotation systems such as ToBI. It treats the contour as built from a small set of categorical elements, whose realisation is then scaled by pitch range and speaking rate, and not as a continuous curve alone. The inventory of tones and their meanings is language specific.

## Processing and development
Listeners use intonation online, for example to anticipate where a phrase will end or which word will carry focus. Cutler, Dahan and van Donselaar (1997) reviewed evidence that prosody influences the segmentation and interpretation of spoken language. Sensitivity to the intonation of the ambient language begins early: infants respond to prosodic patterns of their native language, and prosody is thought to support the discovery of phrase structure.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is general and not specific. Predictive-processing accounts of speech treat prosodic cues as information that sets expectations about upcoming syntax, stress and meaning, and violations of expected prosody as sources of prediction error. Specific Active Inference models of intonation have not been developed.
