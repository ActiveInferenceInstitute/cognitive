# Assistive Technology

## Overview
Assistive technology is any item, piece of equipment or system used to maintain, increase or improve the functional capabilities of people with disabilities, whether acquired through injury and disease or present from birth. It ranges from low-technology aids such as canes and pencil grips to complex electronic systems. Provision usually involves assessment, selection, training and follow-up, with attention to the person's goals and environment.

## Categories
- **Mobility.** Wheelchairs, walkers, orthoses and powered exoskeletons support movement and posture (see [[orthoses]] and [[exoskeletons]]).
- **Vision.** Screen readers, magnifiers, braille displays and, experimentally, [[retinal_implants]].
- **Hearing.** Hearing aids, captioning and [[cochlear_implants]].
- **Communication.** Augmentative and alternative communication devices, including symbol boards, speech-generating devices and eye-gaze systems, for people with limited speech.
- **Cognition and learning.** Reminders, scheduling tools, text-to-speech and speech recognition, which support memory, attention or reading difficulties (see [[dyslexia]] and [[executive_dysfunction]]).
- **Access to computers.** Switches, alternative keyboards, voice control and brain-computer interfaces.

## Design and outcomes
Good assistive technology is matched to the person, the task and the context, and device abandonment is a recognised problem when this fit is poor, when training is inadequate or when devices are stigmatising. Common principles include simplicity, customisation and compatibility with existing tools. Evaluation considers function, participation and quality of life, not only technical performance (see [[occupational_therapy]]).

## Neural interfaces
Neuroprosthetics read signals from the nervous system or write signals into it. Brain-computer interfaces allow communication or device control from neural activity in people with severe paralysis (Wolpaw et al., 2002). These systems pose learning problems for both the user and the decoding algorithm (see [[neuroprosthetics]] and [[neural_decoding]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy. Using a tool or prosthesis can be described as the brain extending its generative model to include a new part of the body or world, so that sensory feedback from the device is explained and action is selected to realise intended outcomes. Active Inference formulations of motor control, in which movement results from proprioceptive predictions, have been proposed as a way to think about prosthetic and interface control (see [[motor_control]] and [[extended_mind]]). They have not become standard engineering practice for assistive devices.
