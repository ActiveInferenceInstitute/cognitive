# Phonology

## Overview
Phonology is the study of how sounds are organised in a language. It concerns the inventory of contrastive sound categories, called phonemes, the permitted ways of combining them into syllables and words, and the rules by which sounds change in context. Phonology differs from phonetics, which describes the physical production and acoustics of speech, in that it concerns the abstract sound patterns that speakers know. Learning the sound system is one of the earliest tasks of language acquisition.

## Acquisition
Young infants can discriminate many speech contrasts, including some that do not occur in their ambient language. Werker and Tees (1984) showed that, over the first year of life, this ability declines for non-native contrasts while sensitivity to native ones is maintained. This process is called perceptual attunement or narrowing. Infants also become sensitive to which sound sequences are permitted in their language and to the stress patterns of words, which helps them find word boundaries in continuous speech. Kuhl and colleagues have proposed that early exposure and social interaction shape the neural representation of native sound categories. Production follows perception: babbling gradually takes on the sounds of the ambient language, and children pass through predictable patterns of simplification, such as replacing difficult sounds with easier ones, before reaching adult-like pronunciation.

## Theoretical approaches
Classical generative phonology represented sounds as bundles of distinctive features and described alternations with ordered rules (Chomsky and Halle, 1968). Later theories, including optimality theory, express patterns as interacting constraints of differing priority. Usage-based approaches emphasise that stored detail about specific words and speakers also shapes sound knowledge.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive accounts treat speech perception as inference in a hierarchical generative model in which higher-level expectations about words and syllables constrain the interpretation of acoustic input. Perceptual attunement fits this picture in a loose way, since learning native categories amounts to acquiring priors about which sounds occur. This is an interpretation that motivates modelling work, not an established derivation.
