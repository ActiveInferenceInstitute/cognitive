# Direct Instruction

## Overview
Direct instruction is a family of teaching approaches in which the teacher explicitly presents content, demonstrates skills, checks understanding and organises practice with feedback. The lowercase term describes this general style of explicit teaching. The capitalised Direct Instruction names specific programmes developed by Siegfried Engelmann and colleagues, which use carefully sequenced, scripted lessons and frequent student responses.

## Key features
- **Clear presentation.** New material is introduced in small steps, with worked examples and explicit explanation.
- **Modelling and guided practice.** The teacher demonstrates, then students practise with support that is gradually withdrawn.
- **Active checking.** Frequent questions reveal misunderstandings, and corrections are immediate.
- **Independent practice and review.** Skills are rehearsed until fluent, and earlier material is revisited.

Rosenshine's review of research on effective teachers identified several such functions, including daily review, presenting in small steps, asking many questions, and providing models and scaffolds.

## Evidence and debate
Evaluations of Engelmann's programmes, including the large US Project Follow Through study, are frequently cited as showing favourable results on basic-skill outcomes, though interpretations of that evidence differ. Cognitive load theory, developed by Sweller, supplies a theoretical rationale: working memory is limited, so novices benefit from explicit guidance and worked examples, whereas unguided problem solving imposes a heavy load. Critics argue that explicit instruction can emphasise recall over deeper understanding and motivation, and constructivist educators propose guided inquiry as a compromise. The balance appears to depend on learner expertise and the kind of goal being pursued.

## Relation to Active Inference
No direct link exists. Active Inference does not prescribe an instructional method, and its constructs of epistemic value and curiosity are more often cited in connection with exploratory learning than with explicit teaching. Any application to classroom practice would be a speculative extension.
