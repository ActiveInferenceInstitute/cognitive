# Social Intelligence

## Overview
Social intelligence is the ability to understand other people and to behave wisely in relations with them. The concept covers perceiving the intentions, emotions and dispositions of others, predicting their behaviour, and choosing actions that achieve goals while maintaining relationships. It is studied as an individual difference in psychology, as an adaptation in evolutionary biology and anthropology, and as a design target in artificial intelligence.

## Definitions and debates
Edward Thorndike (1920) described social intelligence as the ability to understand and manage men and women and to act wisely in human relations, and listed it alongside abstract and mechanical intelligence. Later attempts to measure it as a separate factor, using tests of social judgement or knowledge, found it hard to distinguish empirically from general verbal ability, which led many researchers to fold it into broader constructs. Newer treatments emphasise component skills such as emotion recognition, perspective taking and social problem solving, and Goleman (2006) popularised the term for the neural and interpersonal bases of relating to others. It overlaps with [[emotional_intelligence]] without being identical to it.

In evolutionary work, Humphrey (1976) argued that the demands of living in groups, rather than ecological problems alone, may have driven the evolution of primate intellect. Dunbar's social brain hypothesis later proposed that primate neocortex size correlates with social group size, a pattern since debated with respect to its causal interpretation and its generality across taxa.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference offers a framework for part of this capacity. An agent with a generative model that includes other agents can infer their hidden states, such as goals and beliefs, and select actions that account for their likely responses; see [[theory_of_mind]] and [[social_cognition]]. The link is an active research programme concerned with multi-agent and communicative models, not a settled account of social intelligence as measured in psychology, and the empirical predictions that distinguish it from other computational theories of social inference remain under investigation.
