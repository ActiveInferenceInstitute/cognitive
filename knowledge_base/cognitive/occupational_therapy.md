# Occupational Therapy

## Overview
Occupational therapy is a health profession concerned with enabling people to take part in the activities that make up daily life, such as self-care, work, study, play and social participation. These activities are called occupations. Practitioners work with people whose participation is limited by injury, illness, developmental conditions, ageing or environmental barriers. The central aim is functional participation rather than the restoration of an isolated impairment.

## Process
Practice is usually described as a cycle of evaluation, intervention and outcome review. The evaluation looks at the person, the occupation and the environment together, asking what the person needs or wants to do and what stands in the way. Interventions then act on one or more of these three elements:
- **The person**: graded practice of movement, perception or cognitive strategies, and training in compensatory techniques.
- **The occupation**: changing how a task is performed, for example breaking it into steps, simplifying it or changing its sequence.
- **The environment**: modifying physical layouts, supplying tools such as [[assistive_technology]] or splints and [[orthoses]], and involving carers and workplaces.

Common settings include stroke and hand rehabilitation, paediatric development, mental health, and support for older adults who wish to remain at home. Cognitive aspects, including planning, attention and self-monitoring, are treated alongside motor ones (see [[executive_functions]]).

## Scientific background
Much intervention rests on findings from motor learning: practice that is repeated, task-specific, varied and supplied with informative feedback tends to produce more durable gains than passive movement (see [[motor_learning]] and [[skill_acquisition]]). The reasoning is linked to experience-dependent change in the nervous system ([[neural_plasticity]]). Evidence quality differs across conditions and interventions, and effect sizes are best taken from systematic reviews for each specific condition rather than generalised across the field.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an interpretive one rather than a derivation. In an Active Inference reading, a person's habitual routines are generative models of how actions produce sensory consequences. Illness, injury or altered body mechanics change those consequences, so the model must be updated through repeated action. Task adaptation and environmental modification can be described as reshaping the sensory outcomes and affordances that the model encounters, and graded practice as supplying the experience needed to update it. This framing has been explored mainly for motor learning and rehabilitation in general, not as a specific research programme for occupational therapy.
