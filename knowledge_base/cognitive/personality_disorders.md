# Personality Disorders

## Overview
Personality disorders are conditions in which characteristic ways of thinking, feeling, relating to others and controlling impulses are markedly different from the expectations of a person's culture, are pervasive and inflexible across situations, begin by adolescence or early adulthood, are stable over time, and lead to distress or impairment. They are defined at the level of long-standing traits rather than episodes, and they commonly co-occur with mood, anxiety and substance-use disorders (American Psychiatric Association, 2013).

## Classification
- **Categorical scheme.** The fifth edition of the DSM lists ten specific disorders in three clusters. Cluster A comprises paranoid, schizoid and schizotypal; cluster B comprises antisocial, borderline, histrionic and narcissistic; and cluster C comprises avoidant, dependent and obsessive-compulsive personality disorders.
- **Dimensional approaches.** The categories overlap heavily, and many individuals meet criteria for several. DSM-5 includes an alternative model in its third section that combines an assessment of impairment in self and interpersonal functioning with pathological trait domains. The eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases abandons the specific types in favour of a rating of severity with optional trait qualifiers. Whether categorical or dimensional systems are more valid remains debated (Livesley, 2003).
- **Course and care.** Features may soften with age, and psychological therapies are the main form of treatment, with the evidence base strongest for borderline personality disorder. Diagnosis requires care because of cultural variation, stigma and overlap with other conditions.

## Relation to Active Inference
No established Active Inference account of personality disorders exists. Work in computational psychiatry has begun to treat some patterns, particularly those marked by unstable relationships and difficulty in understanding others, as problems of inference about self and others, for example rigid or poorly updated priors about how other people will respond, or atypical precision on interpersonal cues (see [[theory_of_mind]] and [[social_cognition]]). These ideas are conceptual and not tested against clinical data, and the heterogeneity of the diagnostic category makes a unified account unlikely.
