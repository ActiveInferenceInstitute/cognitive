# Insula

## Overview
The insula, or insular cortex, lies buried within the lateral sulcus, covered by the frontal, parietal and temporal opercula. It receives visceral, gustatory, thermal, painful and other bodily signals through the thalamus and is densely connected with the amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex and orbitofrontal cortex. It is therefore usually described as a hub linking bodily state to affect and cognition.

## Organisation and functions
Craig (2002) proposed a posterior-to-anterior progression: the posterior insula contains a primary representation of the physiological condition of the body, and successive re-representation toward the anterior insula integrates this with context, motivation and emotion, giving rise to subjective feeling. The anterior insula, together with the anterior cingulate, forms a core of the salience network.

Reported functions include:
- **Interoceptive awareness.** Activity and grey-matter measures in the right anterior insula correlate with accuracy in detecting one's own heartbeat (Critchley et al., 2004; see [[interoception]]).
- **Pain and disgust.** The insula responds to noxious stimuli and to disgust-related stimuli, including observing disgust in others.
- **Empathy.** The anterior insula is active both when people experience pain and when they know a loved one is in pain, which has been taken as evidence that it supports affective sharing (Singer et al., 2004; see [[empathy]]).
- **Taste and autonomic control.** The insula contains primary gustatory cortex and influences cardiovascular regulation.

Because it is engaged by many tasks, activation of the insula alone does not identify a specific mental state.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an active research programme. In interoceptive inference, feelings and emotional states are the brain's best explanations of its own bodily signals, generated by hierarchical models that predict visceral input and are corrected by interoceptive prediction errors (Seth, 2013; Barrett & Simmons, 2015). The anterior insula is a proposed locus for these high-level predictions and for the precision assigned to bodily signals, and failures of such precision have been proposed in accounts of anxiety and alexithymia. Evidence is largely indirect, from imaging and computational modelling (see [[homeostasis]]).
