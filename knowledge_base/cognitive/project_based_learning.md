# Project-Based Learning

## Overview
Project-based learning is an approach to teaching in which students acquire knowledge and skills by working, over an extended time, on an investigation of a complex and meaningful question or problem. The investigation leads to a product, such as a report, model, performance or design, that is shared with an audience beyond the teacher. The approach is close to, but distinct from, problem-based learning, which originated in medical education and usually centres on case problems without a required product.

## Features
Blumenfeld and colleagues (1991) identified recurring features of the approach:
- **A driving question** that organises activities and connects them to real problems.
- **Investigations** in which students ask questions, plan, gather information and draw conclusions.
- **Artefacts** that represent students' developing understanding and can be evaluated.
- **Collaboration** among students, teachers and sometimes community members.
- **Use of tools**, including technological ones, that support the inquiry.

Teachers act as facilitators who scaffold the work, set expectations and assess understanding, so the role is not passive.

## Origins
The roots lie in the progressive tradition ([[progressive_education]]). Dewey's emphasis on learning through purposeful activity influenced the project method described by Kilpatrick (1918), who proposed that wholehearted purposeful activity in a social setting is the natural unit of education. Contemporary versions are also informed by [[constructivist_learning_theory]], which holds that learners build understanding through activity, and by [[experiential_learning]].

## Evidence and criticism
Studies report gains in motivation and in application of knowledge in some settings, but findings on achievement are mixed and depend on the design, the teacher's skill, the age of the students and the outcome measured. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) argued that learning approaches giving little guidance to novices are less effective than explicit instruction, which has led to a debate about how much scaffolding projects should include. Many practitioners respond that well-structured projects supply this guidance and are best placed after foundational instruction.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is a loose thematic analogy and the framework has not been used to derive or test project-based methods. A project can be described as an extended cycle of acting, observing the outcome and updating a model of the problem, with the product and its audience supplying feedback. That is a redescription of familiar ideas in active learning ([[active_learning]]), not a prediction that distinguishes the method from alternatives.
