# Magical Number Seven

## Overview
The magical number seven refers to George Miller's (1956) observation that people can hold roughly seven items, plus or minus two, in immediate memory, and that a similar limit appears in absolute judgment of unidimensional stimuli. Miller used the phrase half playfully, noting that the coincidence of the number across different tasks might be only that. The paper became one of the most cited in psychology and helped establish the view of cognition as limited-capacity information processing.

## Chunks and information
Miller drew on information theory. In absolute judgment tasks, such as identifying tones of different pitch, listeners could reliably distinguish only a handful of categories, corresponding to a channel capacity of a few bits. Memory span, in contrast, is measured in items. Miller proposed that memory span is limited by the number of chunks, not by the number of bits, because an item can be recoded into a larger meaningful unit. A person who groups binary digits into triples, for example, can remember a much longer sequence. This idea led to the concept of chunking as a mechanism of expertise, as in the chess studies of Chase and Simon.

## Later revisions
Subsequent work argued that seven overestimates the capacity of focused attention, since span tasks allow rehearsal and the use of long-term knowledge. Cowan (2001) reviewed evidence from tasks in which rehearsal and chunking are controlled and concluded that capacity is about four chunks. Baddeley and Hitch's working memory model and later resource models also treat capacity as depending on the type of material and on the precision of representation. The exact limit, and whether it is a fixed number of slots or a flexible resource, remain debated.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is indirect. Limited capacity is not predicted by the free energy principle itself, but hierarchical generative models can be described as compressing sequences into higher-level states, which parallels chunking. In this loose sense Miller's recoding is an analogy for message compression in a hierarchical model.
