# Sensorimotor Contingencies

## Overview
Sensorimotor contingencies are the regular ways in which sensory input changes as a function of the perceiver's own actions: how a retinal image shifts with an eye movement, how a surface's texture changes as a finger slides across it, how a sound changes as the head turns. In the sensorimotor theory of O'Regan and Noë, seeing consists in the exercise of knowledge of such contingencies, rather than in building an internal picture.

## Core claims
- **Two kinds.** Modality-specific laws (vision changes with blinks and eye movement; touch does not) distinguish senses. Object-related laws (how this cup's appearance changes as I walk around it) distinguish objects.
- **Mastery.** Perception depends on implicit mastery of these laws. This accounts for perceptual presence, the sense that unseen sides of an object are still there, because the agent knows how the input would change if it acted.
- **Evidence.** Adaptation to inverted vision or to sensory substitution devices, where users gain perceptual abilities only through active exploration, is often cited in support. Interpretation of such results is debated, and the theory's account of conscious experience is contested.
- **Link to embodiment.** The theory is a central strand of [[enactive_cognition]] and [[embodied_cognition]], with [[ecological_psychology]] as a related tradition built around affordances.

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference supplies a formal counterpart. A generative model with action-conditioned transitions encodes what would be observed under each action, so mastery of contingencies becomes learned structure in the transition and likelihood mappings ([[transition_model]], [[observation_model]]). Seth proposed that perceptual presence arises from generative models that are counterfactually rich, representing how sensory data would change under actions not taken. This predictive-processing reconstruction is a proposal rather than an established result, and enactivist authors often resist its use of internal models.
