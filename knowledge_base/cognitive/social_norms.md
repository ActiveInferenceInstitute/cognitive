# Social Norms

## Overview
Social norms are shared expectations about how members of a group behave or ought to behave in a given situation, backed by informal approval or sanction. They coordinate behaviour, support [[cooperation]], and are acquired largely through observation and [[social_learning]].

## Mechanism
Cialdini and colleagues' focus theory separates descriptive norms (what most people actually do) from injunctive norms (what people approve of), which can diverge and which influence behaviour when made salient. Bicchieri's account treats a norm as a behavioural rule that an individual follows conditionally on empirical expectations (others follow it) and normative expectations (others think one should follow it, and may sanction deviation). On this view compliance depends on beliefs rather than fixed preferences, and norms can persist even when most people privately disagree.

Fehr and Fischbacher argued that humans enforce norms, including at personal cost through third-party punishment of unfair behaviour, which is central to the explanation of large-scale human cooperation. Developmentally, young children infer norms from demonstration and protest at violations. Neuroimaging studies of norm violation and conformity implicate the [[anterior_cingulate]] and insula (signalling deviation and its aversiveness), lateral prefrontal cortex ([[dorsolateral_prefrontal]]) in norm-guided control, and the ventral striatum and [[medial_prefrontal]] cortex in valuation of social conformity, though these are correlational and broadly not norm-specific. Norms vary across cultures and are studied in [[cultural_cognition]].

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference accounts treat norms as shared prior beliefs over how agents in a context behave, learned from observed behaviour. Constant and colleagues' model of "regimes of expectations" casts conformity as agents minimising surprise about their social environment: acting as others do keeps outcomes predictable, and a norm violation produces prediction error that is aversive, or informative, depending on its precision ([[prediction_error]], [[precision_weighting]]). Veissiere and colleagues extended the account to culture, proposing that cultural practices are shared generative models that coordinate expectations. These are formal and conceptual models, validated mostly by simulation. They fit the observations on conformity and sanctioning, but they do not yet discriminate between norm theories, and their treatment of enforcement and punishment is thin.
