# Cued Recall

## Overview
In cued recall, participants are given a cue related to a studied item and asked to produce the item. A typical paradigm presents word pairs at study, such as "river - candle", and tests with the first word. Other cues include a category name, a word stem, or a semantic associate. Cued recall lies between free recall, which gives no item-specific cue, and recognition, in which the item itself is shown.

## Key findings
- **Availability versus accessibility.** Tulving and Pearlstone (1966) showed that participants who failed to recall words freely could recall many of them when given category names. Information can be stored yet not accessible without a suitable cue.
- **Encoding specificity.** Tulving and Thomson (1973) proposed that a cue is effective to the extent that it matches information encoded with the target. A weak associate present at study can outperform a strong associate that was not, and recognition can fail for items that cued recall retrieves, a result known as recognition failure of recallable words.
- **Cue overload.** The more items associated with a single cue, the less effective the cue is for any one of them.
- **Retrieval practice.** Attempting cued recall strengthens later memory more than restudying does, which makes it a useful learning technique.

## Mechanisms
Retrieval cues are thought to reinstate part of the original encoding context, and the remainder of the trace is then completed. Computational models of the hippocampus describe this as pattern completion in recurrent networks, in which a partial input evokes a stored pattern. Cued recall requires generating the item as well as judging its familiarity, and so depends more on recollection than recognition does.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference treats recall as inference in a generative model: a partial observation serves as evidence and the model infers the full event that most probably produced it. This corresponds closely to pattern completion and to the role of cues, but the framework has been used for memory mainly through scene-construction and hippocampal simulations. It is not a distinct empirical account of cued recall.
