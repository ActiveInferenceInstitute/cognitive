# Emotional Awareness

## Overview
Emotional awareness is the ability to attend to, identify and verbally differentiate emotional states in oneself and in others. It is a form of metacognition applied to affect, and it varies widely between individuals. It is distinct from the intensity of emotion itself: a person can feel strongly yet describe the feeling only vaguely.

## Levels and measurement
Lane and Schwartz (1987) proposed a developmental model with five levels of emotional awareness, each building on the one before:
1. Awareness of bodily sensations.
2. Action tendencies, where feeling is expressed as an urge to act.
3. Single emotions, such as being sad or angry.
4. Blends of emotions, with more than one feeling distinguished at once.
5. Blends of blends, a differentiated understanding of the emotional experience of self and others.

The Levels of Emotional Awareness Scale scores free-text descriptions of imagined scenes against these levels. A related notion is emotional granularity, the degree to which a person distinguishes between similar affective states. Low awareness is studied as alexithymia, a trait of difficulty identifying and describing feelings that is associated with several psychiatric and medical conditions. Alexithymia is a dimensional trait, not a diagnosis, and its relation to those conditions is correlational.

Neuroimaging links emotional awareness to anterior cingulate and medial prefrontal cortex, insula, and regions involved in interoception, but findings are not specific to a single region.

## Role in Active Inference
Smith, Lane, Parr and Friston (2019) proposed a deep active inference model in which emotion concepts are higher-level hypotheses that explain lower-level patterns of bodily and exteroceptive evidence. In this account, greater emotional awareness corresponds to a richer set of high-level states and more precise inference over them, while low awareness corresponds to coarse or imprecise higher-level beliefs. It also suggests why awareness is tied to interoceptive precision. This is a computational hypothesis with some simulation support and limited direct empirical testing, and it is one of several competing explanations of alexithymia. No clinical claim follows from it.
