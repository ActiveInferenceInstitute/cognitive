# Bradykinesia

## Overview
Bradykinesia means slowness of movement. In clinical use the term refers to a syndrome of slowed initiation and execution of voluntary movement, accompanied by a reduction in amplitude (hypokinesia) and a progressive decline in speed or size during repetitive movement, sometimes called the sequence effect. It is the core motor feature required for a diagnosis of parkinsonism, together with rest tremor or rigidity (Postuma et al., 2015). It also occurs in other conditions involving the basal ganglia. This page describes the sign neutrally and does not offer medical guidance.

## Clinical features
- **Slow and small movements.** Finger tapping, hand opening and closing, and foot tapping become slower and smaller, with hesitations or arrests.
- **Reduced spontaneous movement.** Facial expression, blinking, arm swing and gesture decrease, and speech may become quiet and monotone.
- **Difficulty with sequences and simultaneous tasks.** Performing two movements together or in sequence is impaired more than a single movement.
- **Context dependence.** Movements can be improved by strong external cues, such as visual markers on the floor for walking, a phenomenon called kinesia paradoxa in extreme cases.

## Mechanisms
In Parkinson's disease, loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra reduces dopamine in the striatum and alters activity in the basal ganglia output nuclei, which are thought to modulate cortical motor areas (see [[parkinsons]] and [[basal_ganglia]]). Berardelli and colleagues (2001) reviewed physiological studies suggesting that the problem includes difficulty in scaling the size of the initial muscle command and in sustaining motor output, as well as impaired sequencing and use of sensory feedback. Theories that stress reduced vigour, and the cost-benefit weighting of effort, relate the slowness to the role of dopamine in motivation (see [[motivation]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is a proposed theory, and its testing is ongoing. Friston and colleagues (2012) suggested that dopamine encodes the precision of beliefs about action, in particular policies, so that reduced dopaminergic precision would flatten the posterior over actions and make it hard to commit to movement (see [[precision_weighting]] and [[policy_selection]]). Under this view, bradykinesia and akinesia reflect a failure to select a prior that favours action, and cues act by supplying precise sensory evidence. Other theories, such as those based on energetics or on reinforcement learning, offer competing accounts.
