# Premotor Cortex

## Overview
The premotor cortex is the part of the lateral frontal lobe, within Brodmann area 6, that lies immediately anterior to primary motor cortex ([[primary_motor]]). It contributes to preparing and selecting movements, especially movements guided by external sensory cues such as vision, and it sends projections both to primary motor cortex and to the spinal cord. The supplementary and pre-supplementary motor areas on the medial surface are usually treated separately ([[supplementary_motor]]).

## Organisation
The lateral premotor cortex is divided into dorsal and ventral parts, each of which has further subdivisions, and each is linked to parietal regions in parallel fronto-parietal circuits (Rizzolatti and Luppino, 2001).
- **Dorsal premotor cortex** is implicated in reaching and in selecting movements according to arbitrary rules, and neurons there show activity during an instructed delay before movement begins.
- **Ventral premotor cortex**, including area F5 in the macaque, is involved in grasping and in hand-object interactions. It is connected with the anterior intraparietal area.

Wise (1985) summarised early evidence that premotor neurons respond to cues that specify an upcoming movement and fire during a preparatory period. Cisek and Kalaska (2010) argued that the premotor and parietal cortex simultaneously represent several possible actions, with a selection process favouring one.

## Mirror neurons
Area F5 contains neurons that discharge both when the monkey performs a particular goal-directed action, such as grasping, and when it observes another individual performing a similar action (di Pellegrino et al., 1992). These mirror neurons have been proposed to contribute to the understanding of actions, though the extent of their role in humans and in imitation or social cognition is debated ([[mirror_system]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is a developed hypothesis. In the Active Inference account of the motor system, descending signals from motor and premotor cortex are not commands but predictions of proprioceptive input, and spinal reflex arcs fulfil these predictions by minimising prediction error (Adams, Shipp and Friston, 2013). Premotor areas would then encode more abstract, hierarchically higher predictions about the goals and sequences of action. The account is consistent with preparatory activity and with the importance of sensory cues, but alternative control theories explain much of the same physiology, and the detailed anatomical mapping is still being tested.
