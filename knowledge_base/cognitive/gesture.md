# Gesture

## Overview
Gestures are movements of the hands, arms, head or body that carry communicative meaning. They accompany speech in all human cultures studied, and they also occur without speech, from pointing and waving to the full sign languages of deaf communities. The study of gesture asks how such movements relate to language, thought and social interaction.

## Kinds of gesture
Kendon (2004) and McNeill (1992) described a range sometimes called Kendon's continuum, from gesticulation that accompanies speech, through pantomime and conventional emblems such as the thumbs-up, to sign languages with full grammar. McNeill classified co-speech gestures as:
- **iconic**, depicting a feature of an object or action;
- **metaphoric**, depicting an abstract idea in terms of a concrete form;
- **deictic**, pointing to a location, object or person;
- **beat**, small rhythmic movements aligned with the stress pattern of speech.

## Gesture and speech
McNeill argued that gestures and speech arise from a single process of thought and are tightly integrated in timing and meaning, with the gesture often conveying imagistic information that is not in the words. Gestures commonly begin shortly before the related word or phrase. Listeners integrate gestural information when it is available, and speakers often gesture while talking even when listeners cannot see them, which suggests that gestures also serve the speaker, for example by helping to organise spatial content. Pointing appears early in infancy, before the first words, and is considered a prelinguistic foundation of joint attention and communication (see [[joint_attention]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
Gesture fits accounts of communication as interacting agents inferring each other's intentions. Friston and Frith (2015) modelled communication between two agents who each predict the other's actions, so that producing a signal is an action that makes one's own states inferable by a partner. A gesture is an obvious example of a signal that is both an action and a source of evidence. Links to the [[mirror_system]] use similar ideas about predicting observed actions. These are theoretical models, based mainly on simulations, and not accounts of the structure of natural gesture.
