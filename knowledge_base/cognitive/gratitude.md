# Gratitude

## Overview
Gratitude is the emotion, and the more enduring disposition, of appreciating a benefit that one has received, typically from another person but also from fortune or circumstance. Psychologists separate state gratitude, a momentary response to a particular gift or favour, from trait gratitude, a general tendency to notice and respond to benefits. The topic has a long history in moral and religious thought, where it is often treated as a virtue, and entered mainstream empirical psychology in the early 2000s.

## Measurement and findings
Trait gratitude is usually measured by self-report, for example with the six-item Gratitude Questionnaire developed by McCullough and colleagues. Correlational studies associate higher trait gratitude with greater subjective well-being and with more satisfying social relationships. Intervention studies, such as the one by Emmons and McCullough (2003), asked participants to list things they were grateful for over a period of days or weeks and compared them with groups listing neutral events or hassles. Such studies reported higher well-being in the gratitude condition. Later work finds smaller and more variable effects, and the size and durability of intervention benefits remain debated, partly because of reliance on self-report and non-blind designs.

## Social function
Functional accounts propose that gratitude supports reciprocal cooperation. It signals that a benefit was noticed, motivates the recipient to repay or pass on help, and strengthens the bond with the benefactor. On this view it belongs with other social emotions such as guilt and empathy that regulate conduct within relationships. Neuroimaging studies report engagement of medial prefrontal and reward-related regions during gratitude tasks, but findings differ across paradigms.

## Relation to Active Inference
Gratitude has no established formulation in Active Inference. A loose analogy is available: appreciating a benefit involves attributing an outcome to a source and revising beliefs about that source and about the world, and reappraisal of this kind could alter the priors that shape later emotional responses. Active Inference treatments of emotion that stress interoceptive inference and the regulation of affective states, discussed under [[emotional_processing]] and [[emotion_regulation]], would be the natural place to develop this, but that work has not been done specifically for gratitude and the link should be read as speculative.
