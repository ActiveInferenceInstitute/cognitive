# Amnesia

## Overview
Amnesia is a disproportionate loss of memory relative to other cognitive abilities. Perception, language, reasoning and immediate attention are typically spared, so the person is alert and communicates normally but cannot retain or retrieve certain kinds of information. Amnesia can follow damage to the medial temporal lobe, diencephalon or basal forebrain, as in encephalitis, stroke, head injury, oxygen deprivation and chronic alcohol use with thiamine deficiency (Korsakoff syndrome). Transient forms occur, for example transient global amnesia, and psychogenic amnesia is attributed to psychological rather than structural causes.

## Forms
Two temporal patterns are distinguished, and they often coexist.
- **Anterograde amnesia** is the inability to form new lasting memories after the onset of the condition (see [[anterograde_amnesia]]).
- **Retrograde amnesia** is loss of memories acquired before onset, often with greater loss of recent than remote memories (see [[retrograde_amnesia]]).

## What amnesia revealed about memory
The case of the patient H.M., who in 1953 underwent bilateral removal of medial temporal tissue to treat epilepsy, was reported by Scoville and Milner in 1957. He had severe loss of new declarative memories, yet could hold a conversation, had normal immediate span and could learn motor skills. This demonstrated that the medial temporal lobe is critical for forming long-term memories, that short-term and long-term memory depend on partly separate processes, and that skill learning does not require the same structures. Later work by Squire and others developed the distinction between declarative memory, impaired in amnesia, and nondeclarative memory such as skills and priming, which are preserved (see [[memory_systems]] and [[procedural_memory]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. In Active Inference, memory is not stored as a separate archive but is expressed in the parameters of a generative model, and the hippocampal system is proposed to support construction of scenes and sequences. Patients with hippocampal amnesia are impaired not only at remembering the past but at imagining new experiences, as shown by Hassabis and colleagues, which is consistent with a shared constructive mechanism. Describing amnesia as a failure to update or draw on such a model is a reframing and has not been shown to explain more than established neuropsychological accounts.
