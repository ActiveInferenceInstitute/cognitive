# Emotional Intelligence

## Overview
Emotional intelligence is the capacity to perceive, understand, use and manage emotions, both one's own and those of other people. The term was introduced into the scientific literature by Salovey and Mayer (1990) and popularised in the 1990s by Goleman's book of the same name. The construct is contested: researchers disagree about whether it is a genuine cognitive ability, a cluster of personality traits, or a loose collection of social skills.

## Models
- **Ability model.** Mayer and Salovey describe four branches: perceiving emotions, using emotions to facilitate thought, understanding emotions, and managing emotions. Ability is measured by performance tests in which answers are scored against expert or consensus criteria, as in the MSCEIT.
- **Trait model.** Trait emotional intelligence, associated with Petrides and colleagues, concerns self-perceived emotional dispositions and is measured by self-report questionnaires. It overlaps substantially with established personality dimensions.
- **Mixed models.** Popular accounts combine abilities with motivation, empathy and social skills. Critics argue that such broad definitions make the construct hard to separate from personality and general intelligence.

## Findings and criticism
Ability-based scores correlate moderately with general intelligence and with some measures of social functioning, while self-report measures correlate more with personality. Critics question whether emotional intelligence adds predictive value beyond these established constructs, and whether consensus scoring really identifies correct answers. Claims in popular and commercial settings are typically stronger than the evidence supports.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy with partial support. In interoceptive-inference accounts, emotion arises from inferences about the causes of bodily signals, and differences in how precisely those signals are weighted could plausibly affect emotional awareness and its regulation. Alexithymia, a difficulty identifying and describing feelings, has been discussed in this framework. Perceiving others' emotions can likewise be framed as inference over hidden mental states. Active Inference does not currently provide an agreed model of emotional intelligence as a measurable ability.
