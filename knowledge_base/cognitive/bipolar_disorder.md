# Bipolar Disorder

## Overview
Bipolar disorder is a psychiatric condition characterised by recurrent episodes of abnormally elevated or irritable mood and energy, alternating with periods of depression or of relatively normal mood. In the classification of the American Psychiatric Association (2013), bipolar I disorder requires at least one manic episode, whereas bipolar II disorder requires at least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode. Cyclothymic disorder involves chronic, milder fluctuations. The condition was historically called manic-depressive illness (Goodwin & Jamison, 2007).

## Features and course
- **Mania and hypomania.** Elevated mood, decreased need for sleep, increased activity, racing thoughts and risk-taking, which in mania cause marked impairment (see [[mania]]).
- **Depressive episodes.** Low mood, loss of interest, fatigue and altered sleep and appetite, which often make up much of the time ill (see [[depression]]).
- **Course and treatment.** The illness is usually recurrent and begins in adolescence or early adulthood. Mood-stabilising drugs, notably lithium, together with other medications and psychological treatment, are used, and treatment of depressive phases is difficult. Family studies show a strong inherited contribution, involving many genes of small effect and environmental influences.
- **Risk.** The disorder carries elevated risk of suicide, and also high rates of co-occurring anxiety and substance-use disorders.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no accepted Active Inference model of bipolar disorder. Clark, Watson and Friston (2018) proposed a computational perspective on mood in which mood reflects an agent's higher-level estimate of how well it is doing at reducing prediction error, and that this estimate in turn adjusts the precision it assigns to its own predictions and the confidence of its policies. In this framing, depression would correspond to a persistently low estimate and mania to an inflated one, which then biases action toward excessive risk-taking. This is a speculative interpretation that links the symptom pattern to the framework, and it has not been validated against clinical data. Related work in [[computational_psychiatry]] considers abnormalities in reward and learning rates.
