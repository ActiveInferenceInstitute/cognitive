# Iconic Gesture

## Overview
An iconic gesture is a movement of the hands, usually produced together with speech, whose form resembles a property of the referent: tracing a spiral while describing a staircase, or miming a swing while describing a bat. McNeill (1992) distinguished iconic gestures from metaphoric gestures, which depict abstract ideas as if they were objects, from deictic (pointing) gestures and from beats, which mark rhythm and emphasis. Iconic gestures are generally unplanned, are not drawn from a conventional lexicon, and differ from the codified signs of a sign language.

## Timing and content
Iconic gestures are tightly timed with speech: the stroke, the effortful part of the movement, typically coincides with or slightly precedes the related word or phrase. They often carry information not in the words, for example the manner of a motion or the relative position of objects, and speakers gesture even when the listener cannot see them, as on the telephone. Kendon (2004) treats gesture and speech as parts of a single utterance, produced by a shared process.

## Function: contested accounts
Several accounts compete. The lexical retrieval hypothesis proposes that gestures help speakers find words by activating spatial and motoric features. The information packaging hypothesis proposes that gestures help organise spatial and motor content into units suited to speech. Communicative accounts propose that gestures are produced for listeners. A meta-analysis of experiments found a significant, moderate overall benefit of gestures for comprehension, larger for gestures depicting motor actions and for gestures not fully redundant with the speech (Hostetter, 2011). These accounts are not mutually exclusive, and the evidence does not yet single out one.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is analogical. Gesture can be regarded as an action that makes a speaker's intended meaning more inferable by a partner, in line with treatments of communication as shared generative models and generalised synchrony (Friston & Frith, 2015). Embodied simulation accounts also suggest that watching a gesture engages motor representations of the depicted action. Neither claim yields specific, tested predictions about iconic gesture.
