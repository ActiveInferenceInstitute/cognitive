# Psycholinguistics

## Overview
Psycholinguistics is the study of the mental processes involved in using and acquiring language. It asks how listeners and readers turn sounds or print into meaning, how speakers turn intentions into utterances, how children learn a language, and how language is represented in memory. It emerged in the mid-twentieth century at the intersection of linguistics and experimental psychology, and it draws on linguistic theory to define the structures whose use it investigates.

## Research areas
- **Comprehension.** Word recognition, lexical access, parsing of sentence structure and the building of discourse meaning (see [[language_comprehension]] and [[sentence_processing]]).
- **Production.** Levelt's (1989) account describes speaking as a sequence from message planning through lexical selection and grammatical encoding to phonological encoding and articulation.
- **Acquisition.** How infants and children learn sounds, words and grammar.
- **Language and thought.** Bilingualism, reading, and the relation between linguistic and non-linguistic cognition.

## Methods
Typical methods include reaction-time tasks such as lexical decision and priming, eye tracking during reading or in the visual world paradigm, self-paced reading, event-related potentials such as the N400, corpus analysis, and computational modelling. Behavioural measures are used to compare predictions of competing models, for example whether syntactic and semantic information are used in sequence or together.

## Current themes
Much current work stresses that processing is incremental and probabilistic: comprehenders make use of prior experience to anticipate upcoming words, and difficulty tracks how surprising a word is in context. Another theme is the extent to which production and comprehension share representations and mechanisms.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an active research programme. Prediction-based accounts of language treat comprehension as inference over a hierarchy of linguistic and situational causes, with prediction errors signalling unexpected input. Friston and colleagues (2020) extend Active Inference to communication between simulated agents. Evidence for prediction in language is substantial, but the evidence does not discriminate between Active Inference and other probabilistic models.
