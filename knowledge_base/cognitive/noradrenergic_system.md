# Noradrenergic System

## Overview
The noradrenergic system comprises the neurons that synthesise and release norepinephrine, also called noradrenaline. In the brain, the main source is the [[locus_coeruleus]], with additional cell groups in the lateral tegmental area of the medulla and pons. Outside the brain, norepinephrine is the main transmitter released by sympathetic postganglionic neurons, and the adrenal medulla secretes mainly epinephrine together with some norepinephrine as a hormone.

## Biochemistry and receptors
Norepinephrine is a catecholamine made from tyrosine, which is converted to L-DOPA, then to dopamine, and finally to norepinephrine by dopamine beta-hydroxylase. Its action is ended mainly by reuptake through the norepinephrine transporter and by enzymatic breakdown. It acts on G-protein-coupled receptors of three main families: alpha-1, alpha-2 and beta. Alpha-2 receptors also act as autoreceptors that limit release, and receptor subtypes differ in their affinity, so that low and high concentrations can recruit different receptor populations with opposing effects.

## Functions
- **Arousal and wakefulness.** Noradrenergic activity is high during waking, lower in slow-wave sleep and lowest in REM sleep (see [[vigilance]]).
- **Attention and executive function.** In prefrontal cortex, moderate levels support working memory through alpha-2A receptors, whereas high levels during stress impair it, a dependence often described as an inverted-U relationship.
- **Stress and memory.** Emotionally arousing events release norepinephrine that strengthens consolidation of memories, in part through actions in the amygdala.
- **Autonomic regulation.** Peripheral release produces the sympathetic responses of increased heart rate and blood pressure.

Drugs acting on this system include atomoxetine, a reuptake inhibitor used in attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, clonidine, an alpha-2 agonist, and propranolol, a beta-blocker.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relationship is an analogy based on neuromodulator theories. Active Inference casts neuromodulators as setting precision, the weight given to prediction errors or beliefs, and associates dopamine and acetylcholine with specific precisions (see [[dopamine_system]], [[cholinergic_system]]). Norepinephrine has been linked in Bayesian models to unexpected uncertainty and to adaptive gain (Yu & Dayan, 2005; Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005), but there is no consensus about its formal role in Active Inference, and the evidence is mainly pharmacological and computational.
