# Thalamic Relay

## Overview
The thalamus is a group of nuclei in the diencephalon through which almost all sensory information, except olfaction, reaches the cerebral cortex. Thalamic relay neurons receive afferent input and project to cortex. Early views treated this as passive transmission, but evidence shows that relay nuclei modify the signals, control access to cortex, and form loops with it.

## Organisation and gating
- **First-order relays.** Nuclei such as the lateral geniculate nucleus for vision, the medial geniculate nucleus for hearing, and the ventral posterior nuclei for somatosensation receive driving input from peripheral sensory pathways and relay it to primary sensory cortex (Sherman & Guillery, 1996).
- **Higher-order relays.** Nuclei such as the pulvinar and mediodorsal nucleus receive their driving input from layer 5 of cortex, and so provide a route for communication between cortical areas that runs through the thalamus as well as through direct connections (Sherman, 2016).
- **Modulation.** Corticothalamic feedback from layer 6 is far more numerous than the feedforward input and modulates relay cells, as do inhibition from the thalamic reticular nucleus and cholinergic and noradrenergic input from the brainstem.
- **Firing modes.** Relay cells fire either in a tonic mode, which transmits input approximately linearly, or in a burst mode associated with low-threshold calcium channels, which is more prominent in sleep and drowsiness and may signal a change in input. Rhythms of thalamocortical circuits also underlie sleep spindles and are linked to levels of arousal (see [[reticular_activation]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
Within predictive processing, the thalamus is often proposed to act as a gain controller. Kanai and colleagues (2015) suggested that the pulvinar implements precision-weighting of prediction errors, coordinating the cortical hierarchy by regulating which areas communicate and how strongly. This is consistent with the anatomy of higher-order relays and corticothalamic feedback, but it is a proposal supported mainly by indirect evidence and has not been tested decisively. The Active Inference literature has not produced a unique account of relay function.
