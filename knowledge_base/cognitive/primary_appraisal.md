# Primary Appraisal

## Overview
Primary appraisal is a concept from Richard Lazarus's cognitive-motivational-relational theory of emotion and stress. It is the evaluation of whether an encounter matters for one's well-being and goals, made before and alongside the assessment of what can be done about it. The related page terms "relevance" and "impact" match this sense. Other appraisal theorists, such as Scherer, use different stage structures, so the term is specific to the Lazarus tradition.

## Core claims
In Lazarus and Folkman's formulation (1984), a person appraises an encounter as:
- **Irrelevant**: no implication for well-being.
- **Benign-positive**: preserves or enhances well-being.
- **Stressful**, with three sub-types: *harm/loss* (damage already done), *threat* (anticipated harm), and *challenge* (potential for gain or growth, with opportunity to master the demand).

Primary appraisal is paired with **secondary appraisal**, the evaluation of coping resources and options, and the two interact and are revised through reappraisal as the situation evolves. The emotions that result depend on the pattern of appraisal: for example, threat appraisals with low perceived coping potential tend toward anxiety, and goal obstruction toward anger. Appraisal need not be deliberate; Lazarus held that it can be fast and automatic, a position debated by theorists who argue emotions can arise without cognitive evaluation. Evidence for the theory comes from self-report studies, manipulations of how events are described, and physiological stress responses that vary with threat versus challenge appraisal. Cognitive reappraisal is a well-studied emotion regulation strategy that changes this evaluation ([[emotion_regulation]]).

## Role in Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. Appraisal can be recast as inference about the causes and expected consequences of interoceptive and exteroceptive signals: relevance corresponds to the degree to which an event bears on preferred (homeostatically viable) states, and threat to predicted deviation from them. Seth (2013) proposed that emotion arises from interoceptive inference, and Joffily and Coricelli (2013) linked valence to the rate of change of free energy over time. Secondary appraisal resembles evaluating the expected free energy of available policies, including controllability. These are conceptual proposals within a developing literature, and no direct empirical test maps Lazarus's categories onto model quantities.
