# Visual Agnosia

## Overview
Visual agnosia is a failure to recognise visual objects that cannot be explained by loss of elementary vision, language disorder or general intellectual decline. A patient may see that an object is present, describe its colour and edges, and yet not say what it is, but then identify the same object at once by touch, sound or hearing its name. Cases usually follow damage to occipital and occipitotemporal cortex, for instance from stroke, carbon monoxide poisoning or head injury.

## Types
A distinction proposed by Lissauer in 1890 remains influential.
- **Apperceptive agnosia**: perceptual processing is disrupted so that shapes cannot be assembled into a coherent form. Patients may fail to copy or match drawings, though they can draw from memory in some cases.
- **Associative agnosia**: perception of form is adequate, since patients can copy drawings accurately, but the percept does not access stored meaning. It is related to the problem of connecting perception to [[semantic_memory]].
- **Category-specific forms**: prosopagnosia is the selective loss of face recognition (see [[face_processing]]), and other cases involve difficulty with words or places.

The distinction is a continuum in practice, and many patients show a mixture. The condition is also tied to the two-streams view of visual cortex, in which a ventral pathway supports recognition and a dorsal pathway supports visually guided action. A well-known patient with ventral damage could not report the orientation of a slot but could post a card through it accurately, which illustrates that action-related vision can survive without conscious recognition.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive-processing accounts treat [[object_recognition]] as inference over a hierarchical generative model that explains sensory input through hidden causes. On this view, agnosia could be read as damage at different levels of the hierarchy: low and intermediate levels that bind features into forms for apperceptive cases, and higher levels linking forms to meaning for associative ones. This is an interpretive analogy. It has not yet been developed into a quantitative model of specific patients, and the classical lesion-based literature stands independently. See [[perceptual_inference]] and [[visual_perception]].
