# Focused Attention

## Overview
In the study of meditation, focused attention (FA) denotes practices in which attention is directed to a single chosen object, such as the sensations of breathing, a repeated word or an image, and is brought back to that object whenever it drifts. It is one of two broad families of practice distinguished in the contemplative-science literature; the other, [[open_monitoring]], keeps attention receptive to whatever arises. The term is used here in the sense of a meditation style, and not the general cognitive-psychology sense of selective attention (see [[selective_attention]]).

## The cycle of practice
Lutz, Slagter, Dunne and Davidson (2008) and, using fMRI in experienced meditators, Hasenkamp and colleagues (2012) describe a cycle that is often summarised in four steps:
1. attention rests on the object;
2. attention wanders, often unnoticed;
3. the lapse is noticed (monitoring);
4. attention is disengaged from the distractor and redirected to the object.

The skill therefore combines sustained attention, meta-awareness of the current state, and the ability to disengage and shift. With practice, the proposal is that lapses become shorter and less frequent and that less effort is required to maintain the focus.

## Findings
Studies comparing experienced practitioners with novices have reported differences in activity of frontal and parietal attention regions and in the default mode network, with some work suggesting reduced default-mode activity during practice in experts (see [[default_mode]]). Training studies suggest improvements in some tests of sustained attention. These findings come from relatively small samples, with variable control conditions and mixed replication, and effects specific to focused attention can be hard to separate from non-specific effects of practice and expectation.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference accounts treat attention as the optimisation of precision, and meditation has been analysed in these terms. In focused attention, the intended object is given high precision while competing signals, including mind-wandering, are given low precision; noticing a lapse is a change in beliefs about one's own attentional state (see [[precision_weighting]]). Such accounts are theoretical proposals, with some simulation work, and remain to be tested against experimental data.
