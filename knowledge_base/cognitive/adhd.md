# ADHD

## Overview
Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental condition defined in the DSM-5 by a persistent pattern of inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity that interferes with functioning or development. Symptoms must begin in childhood, appear in more than one setting and be inconsistent with the developmental level. Presentations are classified as predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, or combined, and the presentation can change over time. Symptoms often persist into adulthood. Family and twin studies indicate substantial heritability, and environmental factors also contribute.

## Cognitive and neural accounts
No single deficit explains all cases, and current views allow multiple pathways.
- **Executive dysfunction.** Barkley proposed that a core deficit in behavioural inhibition disrupts working memory, self-regulation and sustained attention (see [[executive_functions]] and [[inhibitory_control]]).
- **Delay aversion and motivation.** Sonuga-Barke's dual pathway model adds a motivational route, in which individuals find waiting aversive and prefer immediate rewards (see [[temporal_discounting]]).
- **Neurochemistry and circuits.** Dopaminergic and noradrenergic systems are implicated, consistent with the effect of stimulant and noradrenergic medications. Imaging studies report differences in frontostriatal and default-mode network function, although effects are small and vary across studies.
- **Attention.** Difficulties in sustaining attention, resisting distraction and shifting under demand are reported (see [[sustained_attention]]).

Assessment relies on clinical interviews and rating scales, and management combines behavioural approaches, educational support and sometimes medication under clinical supervision.

## Relation to Active Inference
Application to ADHD belongs to computational psychiatry and is at an early stage. Because dopamine is proposed to encode the precision of beliefs about policies and attention is described as precision weighting, atypical dopaminergic function could in principle alter how strongly competing sensory evidence and action plans are weighted, producing distractibility and impulsive switching. Some studies have also modelled reward learning and temporal discounting with Bayesian models. These are hypotheses, and none has been shown to account for the heterogeneity of the condition.
