# Babbling

## Overview
Babbling is the production by infants of speech-like sounds that lack identifiable words. It follows earlier cooing and vocal play and precedes the first words. Its most characteristic form is canonical babbling, repeated consonant-vowel syllables such as "bababa" or "dadada", which typically emerges in the second half of the first year. Babbling is regarded as an early stage in learning to coordinate the vocal tract and as a bridge from perception of speech to production of it.

## Stages and properties
- **Early vocalisation.** Oller (2000) describes a progression from phonation and cooing, through expansion stages with squeals and growls, to canonical babbling, followed by variegated babbling in which consonants and vowels vary within a sequence.
- **Hearing matters.** Deaf infants often start canonical babbling later, or produce less varied babble, than hearing infants, which suggests that auditory feedback contributes. Cochlear implantation is followed by changes in vocal development (see [[cochlear_implants]]).
- **Language-specific influences.** Over the first year, infants' perception becomes tuned to the sounds of their native language, and some studies report that babbling comes to reflect features of the ambient language, although the size of this effect is debated (Kuhl, 2004).
- **Modality-independent.** Deaf infants exposed to sign language produce rhythmic manual activity with syllable-like organisation, in addition to vocal babble, which Petitto and Marentette (1991) interpreted as evidence that babbling reflects a general capacity for language structure and not speech alone.
- **Motor development.** The onset of canonical babbling coincides with rhythmic movements of the limbs and has been linked to the maturation of motor control (see [[motor_learning]]).

## Functions
Babbling provides practice that links articulatory movements with their acoustic consequences, supplying a mapping used later in imitation and word production. Adult responses that are contingent on infant vocalisations are associated with more advanced vocal development, which shows a social influence (see [[imitation]] and [[statistical_learning]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy supported by modelling. Babbling can be framed as exploratory action in which an infant samples the consequences of its vocal movements. Such epistemic action reduces uncertainty about the mapping between motor commands and sensory outcomes, in the manner of Active Inference treatments of motor babbling and [[epistemic_foraging]]. Learned forward models then support later imitation and prediction (see [[forward_models]]). Computational models of this kind exist, but they are simplifications of infant vocal development.
