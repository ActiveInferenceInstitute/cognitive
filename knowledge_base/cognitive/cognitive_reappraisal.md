# Cognitive Reappraisal

## Overview
Cognitive reappraisal is the deliberate reinterpretation of a situation so as to change its emotional meaning. A person facing an upcoming examination might reconsider it as a chance to show what they have learned, or a person watching a distressing medical scene might adopt a detached, professional view. Reappraisal is among the most studied strategies of [[emotion_regulation]], and it draws directly on the idea that emotions depend on how events are appraised (see [[appraisal]]).

## Process model
Gross (1998) organised regulation strategies by when they act in the emotion-generating process. Reappraisal is antecedent-focused, because it acts early, before a full emotional response has developed, by changing the meaning attached to the situation. It is contrasted with [[expressive_suppression]], a response-focused strategy that modifies outward expression after the emotion has arisen. In experiments, reappraisal reduces self-reported negative emotion and physiological responses without the memory costs reported for suppression. In questionnaire studies, habitual use of reappraisal is associated with better wellbeing and social functioning (Gross and John, 2003). These associations are correlational, and benefits depend on context, such as the controllability of the situation.

## Neural correlates
Neuroimaging studies find that reappraisal engages lateral and medial prefrontal regions and the parietal cortex, which are associated with cognitive control and the representation of meaning, and reduces activity in the [[amygdala]] and related affective regions (Ochsner and Gross, 2005; Buhle et al., 2014). This is consistent with a model in which control regions modulate affective responses (see [[dorsolateral_prefrontal]] and [[cognitive_control]]). Causal claims are limited by the correlational nature of imaging, and the specific pathways remain debated.

## Use and training
Reappraisal underlies parts of cognitive therapies, where patients learn to examine and modify interpretations that maintain distress (see [[metacognitive_therapy]]). Success depends on cognitive resources, practice and the intensity of the emotion, since reappraisal is more difficult at high intensity. This page describes the strategy neutrally and is not clinical guidance.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an interpretation. If emotions result from inference about the significance of bodily and external events, reappraisal can be described as revising higher-level beliefs that provide the context for lower-level affective predictions, so that the same sensory evidence is explained differently (see [[predictive_processing]] and [[hesp_2021]]). Top-down change of prior beliefs would then alter the interoceptive and autonomic predictions, which supports the claim that meaning influences feeling. Formal models of reappraisal in Active Inference are still at an early stage.
