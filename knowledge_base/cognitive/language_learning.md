# Language Learning

## Overview
Language learning is the process by which a person acquires the sounds, words, grammar, and conventions of a language. Research distinguishes first-language acquisition in infancy and childhood from second-language learning, which differs in age, input, and prior linguistic knowledge. The term here means acquisition across the lifespan, as distinct from adult comprehension and production, which are covered in [[language_processing]].

## Mechanism
**Early perception.** Infants begin by discriminating nearly all phonetic contrasts and, during the first year, tune toward the contrasts of their native language while declining for non-native ones. Kuhl's work describes this as learning driven by distributional statistics of speech plus social interaction.

**Statistical learning.** Eight-month-old infants can segment a continuous syllable stream into word-like units from transitional probabilities between syllables (Saffran, Aslin, and Newport, 1996). Distributional cues also support learning of categories and some word-order regularities (see [[statistical_learning]]).

**Word and grammar learning.** Vocabulary growth depends on joint attention, social cues, and constraints on word meaning ([[joint_attention]]). Grammar is learned from sparse and noisy input; how much innate structure is required remains disputed between nativist and usage-based accounts.

**Age effects.** Learners who begin a second language earlier generally reach higher final proficiency, particularly in phonology and grammar, but the shape of the decline (an abrupt critical period versus gradual change) and its causes (maturation, prior-language entrenchment, differing input) are debated. See [[critical_periods]].

**Practice and feedback.** Retrieval practice, spaced repetition, and interaction improve retention in adult learners, consistent with general principles in [[learning_theory]].

## Role in Active Inference
Acquisition can be framed as learning a hierarchical generative model of speech and communication whose levels span phonemes, words, and syntax. Prediction errors on upcoming speech drive both inference about the current utterance and slower updating of the model's parameters. Friston and colleagues have developed generative-model treatments of linguistic communication in this framework, including active sampling of a speaker by a listener. Such treatments are formal proposals, and quantitative fits to developmental data on acquisition are limited. Structure learning ([[structure_learning]]) is the natural counterpart of grammar induction, though how it is realized is unresolved.
