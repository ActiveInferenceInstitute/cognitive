# Emotion Contagion

## Overview
Emotion contagion is the spread of emotion from one person to another, so that a person comes to feel, or to express, what others around them feel without deliberate effort. Hatfield, Cacioppo and Rapson (1994) defined it as the tendency to automatically mimic and synchronise facial expressions, vocalisations, postures and movements with those of another person, and consequently to converge emotionally. It is distinguished from empathy by being automatic and not necessarily involving understanding or concern for the other.

## Proposed mechanism
Accounts typically describe a sequence. An observer perceives another's expressive behaviour, mimics it, often at a level too small to see, and the afferent feedback from the mimicked facial muscles or posture contributes to the observer's own emotional state. Electromyography studies show that people respond to pictures of happy and angry faces with subtle, rapid activity in the muscles that produce the same expressions, even when the faces are masked from awareness (Dimberg, Thunberg and Elmehed, 2000). Alternative or additional routes include conscious appraisal, perspective taking and learned association.

## Evidence and moderators
Contagion has been observed in infants who cry on hearing other infants cry, in laboratory studies of mood transfer in pairs and groups, and in analyses of workplace and social networks. An online experiment reported that manipulating the emotional content of users' news feeds produced small shifts in the emotional tone of their own posts, although the study drew debate about both effect interpretation and ethics. The strength of contagion varies with closeness, status, attention to the other person, and individual differences in expressiveness and susceptibility. The role of facial feedback itself has been questioned in replication efforts.

## Relation to Active Inference
Friston and Frith's "duet for one" proposal treats communicating agents as coupling their generative models so that their expectations converge, which may offer a route to shared affective states. Interoceptive inference accounts of emotion suggest that bodily signals triggered by mimicry could update the observer's own affective predictions. These are theoretical proposals, and quantitative tests of emotion contagion within Active Inference are limited.
