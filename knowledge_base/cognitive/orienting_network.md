# Orienting Network

## Overview
The orienting network is one of three attentional networks distinguished by Posner and Petersen (1990), alongside alerting and executive control. Its function is to select information from the many sensory inputs available, by directing attention to a location or object. Orienting may be overt, with movement of the eyes or head, or covert, with attention shifted while gaze remains fixed (see [[overt_attention]] and [[covert_attention]]).

## Components
Human imaging and lesion studies implicate a set of regions that are associated with spatial shifts of attention, including the superior parietal lobe, the temporoparietal junction, the frontal eye fields ([[frontal_eye_fields]]) and subcortical structures such as the superior colliculus and the pulvinar. Corbetta and Shulman (2002) distinguished a dorsal frontoparietal system associated with goal-directed selection from a ventral system associated with detecting behaviourally relevant, unexpected stimuli. The two systems interact, and their anatomical boundaries are not sharp.

The network is modulated by acetylcholine. Pharmacological studies in monkeys and humans suggest that cholinergic manipulation affects the benefit of a valid spatial cue, a finding that distinguishes it from the noradrenergic modulation commonly linked to alerting ([[phasic_alertness]]).

## Measurement
The standard probe is the spatial cueing task (Posner, 1980). A cue indicates the likely location of a target, and responses are faster for valid than for invalid cues. The difference between invalid and valid trials estimates the cost and benefit of orienting. The attention network test of Fan and colleagues (2002) combines cueing and flanker conditions to give separate efficiency scores for alerting, orienting and executive control within one task.

## Relation to Active Inference
In Active Inference and predictive coding, attention is described as the optimisation of precision, the weight given to sensory prediction errors. Feldman and Friston (2010) proposed that attention corresponds to increasing the precision of prediction errors from the attended location, which accounts for the speeding of responses to validly cued targets. Parts of the dorsal system can then be seen as setting expected precisions. This is a modelling proposal that reproduces behaviour in cueing tasks, and its specific anatomical mapping is still being tested (see [[precision_weighting]]).
