# Social Capital

## Overview
Social capital refers to the value that people and groups derive from their social relationships. The idea is that connections, trust and shared expectations function as a resource, comparable in some respects to physical or human capital, that can make cooperation and coordinated action easier. The term has several definitions that emphasise different things, and it is used in sociology, economics, political science and public health.

## Major formulations
- **Bourdieu** defined social capital as the aggregate of actual or potential resources linked to possession of a durable network of relationships of mutual acquaintance or recognition. His emphasis was on how such resources help reproduce advantage within a class structure.
- **Coleman** defined it by its function: aspects of social structure that facilitate the actions of actors within it. He highlighted obligations and expectations, information channels, and norms with sanctions, and argued that closed networks help enforce trustworthiness.
- **Putnam** applied the concept at the level of communities and nations, treating networks, norms and trust as features that support collective action. He distinguished bonding capital, which strengthens ties within a group, from bridging capital, which links people across groups. Bonding ties can provide solidarity and support but may also reinforce exclusion.

Measurement is contested. Common proxies include survey items on generalised trust, associational membership, and network measures such as density or brokerage. Critics have noted that the concept can be vague, that it is sometimes defined partly by its supposed effects, which makes causal claims circular, and that outcomes depend on which resources a network actually gives access to.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no established derivation linking social capital to the free energy principle. A loose connection exists through trust: in an Active Inference reading, trust corresponds to confident beliefs about the reliability of other agents, and dense, stable networks and shared norms make others more predictable and reduce uncertainty in coordination (see [[social_norms]] and [[cooperation]]). This is an analogy, not a tested model.
