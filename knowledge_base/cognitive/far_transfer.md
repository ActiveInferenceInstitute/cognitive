# Far Transfer

## Overview
Far transfer is the use of something learned in one context in a context that is quite different from it, for example when training in chess is claimed to improve mathematics, or when working-memory exercises are claimed to improve reasoning. It is contrasted with [[near_transfer]], where the new task shares surface features, materials or procedures with the trained task. The distinction is a matter of degree, and it has been a long-running subject of debate because the practical value of education and cognitive training depends heavily on it.

## Dimensions of distance
Barnett and Ceci (2002) proposed a taxonomy in which transfer distance is described along two groups of dimensions. The content dimension covers what is transferred: the learned skill (a procedure, a representation, or a principle), the performance change, and the memory demands of the task. The context dimension covers differences in knowledge domain, physical setting, temporal separation, functional setting, social setting and modality. Far transfer is where the context differs on many of these dimensions at once. Using such dimensions makes claims about transfer easier to compare and to test, since studies otherwise label different degrees of difference with the same words.

## Evidence
Thorndike and Woodworth (1901) found that improvement on one mental function had limited effects on others, and argued for transfer through shared elements. Later reviews of far transfer from working-memory training, music training and game-based training generally report small or absent transfer to untrained abilities, while near transfer to similar tasks is more reliable. Sala and Gobet (2017) argued from meta-analyses that far transfer is rarely demonstrated. Transfer is more likely when learners understand underlying principles, practise in varied contexts, and are prompted to see connections, as in the work on analogical transfer (see [[analogical_reasoning]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy. Transfer can be described as reuse of a generative model's structure in a new domain. Structure learning in Active Inference concerns how models acquire abstract latent structure that supports generalisation, which is one route to far transfer (see [[structure_learning]]). Concrete computational models of human far transfer remain limited.
