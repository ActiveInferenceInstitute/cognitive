# Affective Neuroscience

## Overview
Affective neuroscience investigates how brain systems generate and regulate emotion, mood and motivation, using animal experiments, lesion studies, pharmacology and human neuroimaging. The name was popularised by Jaak Panksepp, who argued that mammals share subcortical emotional systems that can be studied through behaviour and stimulation. The field overlaps with [[social_neuroscience]], [[motivation]] and the study of psychiatric conditions.

## Main research themes
- **Subcortical systems.** Panksepp proposed several primary emotional command systems, which he labelled SEEKING, RAGE, FEAR, LUST, CARE, PANIC/GRIEF and PLAY, anchored in structures such as the periaqueductal gray and hypothalamus (see [[periaqueductal_gray]]). The framework is influential but contested, in part because it relies on inferences from animal behaviour to feeling.
- **Threat circuits.** Work on fear conditioning in rodents, especially by LeDoux and colleagues, identified the [[amygdala]] as a central node for learning about threat (see [[fear_circuit]] and [[fear_conditioning]]). Researchers disagree about whether such circuits should be described as fear or as defensive survival circuits.
- **Cortical and insular contributions.** The [[insula]], [[orbitofrontal]] and medial prefrontal regions contribute to representing bodily state, value and regulation (see [[emotion_regulation]]).
- **Constructionist views.** Some theorists, for example Barrett, argue that emotions are constructed from core affect, bodily signals and conceptual knowledge, with no dedicated circuit per emotion category.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an active research programme. Interoceptive inference treats feelings as the brain's inference about the causes of bodily signals, with homeostatic regulation carried out by autonomic and behavioural action (Seth, 2013; Barrett and Simmons, 2015; see [[seth_2021]] and [[allostatic_regulation]]). Within Active Inference, affective valence has been proposed to track the rate of change of free energy or the confidence in policies, and precision at higher levels has been linked to the dopaminergic and other neuromodulatory systems (see [[precision_weighting]] and [[hesp_2021]]). These are theoretical proposals under test, not established findings.
