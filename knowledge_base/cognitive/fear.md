# Fear

## Overview
Fear is the emotional response to a perceived present threat, comprising changes in physiology, behaviour and, in humans, subjective experience. Darwin (1872) treated it as one of the emotions whose expression is shared across species. Fear is commonly distinguished from anxiety, which concerns diffuse or anticipated threat, although the boundary is blurred and the two share circuitry (see [[anxiety]]). Fear promotes survival by prompting freezing, flight or defensive aggression, depending on the proximity of the threat and the options available.

## Neural basis and learning
- **Amygdala.** Pavlovian fear conditioning, in which a neutral cue acquires the ability to elicit defensive responses after being paired with an aversive event, depends on the amygdala, particularly its lateral and central nuclei. The central nucleus projects to hypothalamic and brainstem sites, including the periaqueductal gray, that generate freezing and autonomic responses (LeDoux, 2000).
- **Fast and slow routes.** Sensory information can reach the amygdala by a rapid thalamic route and by a slower, more detailed cortical route, allowing a quick response to potential threat and later correction.
- **Extinction.** Repeated presentation of the cue without the aversive event reduces responding by forming a new, context-dependent inhibitory memory involving the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, rather than erasing the original, so fear can return.
- **Feeling versus defence.** LeDoux (2012) argues that survival circuits producing defensive behaviour are distinct from the processes that give rise to conscious feelings of fear, whereas other researchers hold that the same circuits underlie the subjective state. The question is unresolved.

## Relation to Active Inference
Within the framework, fear can be understood as inference that the current state is one in which the agent expects high surprise, such as tissue damage, together with policies that reduce that expected surprise, notably freezing or escape. Constructive accounts of emotion as inference about the causes of interoceptive and exteroceptive signals (Seth, 2013) treat the experienced quality of fear as a high-level model of bodily and environmental state. These proposals fit the conditioning and extinction literature, but they are theoretical interpretations and not an established quantitative model of the amygdala circuit.
