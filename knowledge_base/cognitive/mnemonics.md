# Mnemonics

## Overview
Mnemonics are techniques that make information easier to remember by linking it to structures that the mind retains well, such as vivid images, familiar places, rhymes or meaningful patterns. They have been used since antiquity, when orators relied on trained memory in the absence of written notes, and the classical tradition is documented in historical studies such as Yates (1966).

## Techniques
- **Method of loci.** Items are imagined at successive locations along a familiar route, and recall proceeds by mentally walking the route.
- **Peg systems.** A learned list of cue words, often tied to numbers, serves as hooks on which to attach new items by imagery.
- **Keyword method.** A foreign word is linked to a similar-sounding native word, and an image joins that word to the meaning. It is widely applied to vocabulary learning.
- **Acronyms and acrostics.** The initial letters of a list are turned into a word or sentence.
- **Rhymes and songs.** Rhythm and rhyme constrain wording and aid retrieval.
- **Chunking.** Material is grouped into meaningful units, which reduces the number of items to hold (see [[chunking]]).

## Cognitive basis and evidence
Most mnemonics combine three ingredients: imagery, organisation and elaboration. Paivio's dual-coding theory proposed that concrete material encoded both verbally and as an image has two routes to retrieval. Experimental studies show that mnemonic instruction can substantially improve recall of lists and vocabulary relative to rote repetition, particularly when the material is arbitrary. Gains are smaller for tasks that demand conceptual understanding, and the benefits depend on the learner practising the method. Neuroimaging of memory champions suggests that they rely on a trained use of spatial and imagery-related systems, and not on exceptional anatomy.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no established Active Inference account of mnemonics. A loose analogy is that a mnemonic supplies a strong prior structure, such as a route or a rhyme, into which new items are inferred and from which they can be reconstructed. The idea that spatial scaffolds engage hippocampal processing is an interpretation consistent with the wider literature on predictive models of memory.
