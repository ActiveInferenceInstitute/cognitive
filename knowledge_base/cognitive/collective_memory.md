# Collective Memory

## Overview
Collective memory is a family of ideas about remembering that is shared among the members of a group, whether a couple, an organisation or a nation. The term is used in two broad ways. In sociology and history it names publicly maintained representations of the past, such as commemorations, archives and narratives. In psychology it names the aggregation or interaction of individual memories within a group. The two uses overlap but are studied with different methods.

## Main accounts
- **Social frameworks.** Halbwachs (1992) argued that individual remembering depends on the frameworks supplied by groups such as family, religion and class, so that even private memories are socially organised. Olick and Robbins (1998) review the later field of social memory studies and its distinction between shared individual memory and public mnemonic practices.
- **Transactive memory.** Wegner (1987) proposed that close pairs and teams develop a shared system for encoding, storing and retrieving information, in which each member knows who holds which knowledge. Such a system can store more than any individual, at the cost of dependence on partners.
- **Collaborative remembering.** Experimental work compares group recall with the pooled recall of the same people working alone. Groups often recall less than the pooled individual total, a phenomenon called collaborative inhibition, but group discussion can correct errors and build a more shared account.

## Transmission and change
Collective memories are carried by conversation, media, ritual and institutions, and they are reconstructed each time they are retold. This makes them selective and open to revision as present concerns shift, which parallels the reconstructive character of individual memory (see [[episodic_memory]]).

## Role in Active Inference
The link is an analogy. Shared generative models, cultural practices that shape a common niche, and communication that aligns beliefs between agents are themes in Active Inference work on culture. This offers a vocabulary for how groups maintain common expectations about the past, but no model reproduces the findings on collective memory, and the connection remains conceptual.
