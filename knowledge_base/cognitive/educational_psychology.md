# Educational Psychology

## Overview
Educational psychology applies psychological theory and methods to teaching and learning. It asks how people acquire knowledge and skills, how development shapes what can be learned at different ages, how motivation and emotion affect engagement, and how instruction and assessment can be designed more effectively. It draws on cognitive, developmental, social and measurement psychology, and interacts with the learning sciences and education research.

## Core areas
- **Learning and memory.** Behaviourist, cognitive and sociocultural accounts of learning, and applied findings such as retrieval practice and spaced study (see [[spaced_repetition]]).
- **Development.** Piaget's stages, Vygotsky's sociocultural theory and later work on cognitive and socioemotional change shape expectations about what learners can do at different ages.
- **Motivation and self-regulation.** Theories of self-efficacy, goals, interest and attribution explain persistence, and models of self-regulated learning describe planning, monitoring and adjusting one's own study.
- **Instruction and assessment.** Research on explicit teaching, scaffolding, feedback, classroom management and the design and validity of tests.
- **Individual differences.** Ability, prior knowledge, language background and special educational needs.

## Methods and cautions
Studies include randomised experiments, quasi-experimental classroom comparisons, longitudinal designs and large-scale meta-analyses such as Hattie's synthesis of influences on achievement. Findings from controlled laboratory studies do not always carry over to classrooms, and effect sizes vary with context, measure and age group. Popular ideas such as fixed learning styles have not been supported by good evidence, so practical recommendations rely on replicated findings.

## Relation to Active Inference
Educational psychology has no unified connection to Active Inference. Some researchers have used predictive-processing ideas to discuss curiosity, prediction error and conceptual change in learning, and the framework offers models of how beliefs are updated under uncertainty. These contributions are exploratory. Educational claims rest on educational evidence, not on the free energy principle.
