# Orthoses

## Overview
An orthosis is a device applied externally to the body to modify the structural or functional characteristics of the neuromuscular or skeletal system. Common examples are foot insoles, ankle-foot orthoses, knee braces, spinal braces and hand or wrist splints. An orthosis differs from a prosthesis, which replaces a missing body part, and from a general mobility aid such as a walker, which does not act on a specific body segment ([[assistive_technology]]).

## Purposes and types
Orthoses are prescribed for several reasons, and one device may serve more than one:
- **Support or stabilisation**, to supply stability to a weak or unstable joint.
- **Immobilisation or protection**, to limit motion during healing after injury or surgery.
- **Correction or prevention of deformity**, for example in growing children with spinal curvature or in people at risk of contracture.
- **Offloading**, to redistribute mechanical load away from a painful or ulcer-prone area.
- **Assistance of function**, as when an ankle-foot orthosis holds the foot upward in a person with foot drop, so that the toes clear the ground during walking.

They are classified by the region they cross, such as foot, ankle-foot, knee, hip, spinal or upper limb, and by whether they are prefabricated or custom made from a mould or scan. The design of a device draws on [[biomechanics]], and its prescription is commonly the responsibility of an orthotist, physician or therapist (Lusardi, Jorge and Nielsen, 2013).

## Effects on movement and sensation
An orthosis changes the mechanics of a joint and also alters the sensory information from the limb, since contact pressure and restricted motion change cutaneous and proprioceptive signals ([[proprioception]]). Users generally need a period of adaptation, during which gait or hand use adjusts to the device ([[motor_learning]]). Evidence for the benefit of specific devices varies by condition, and recommendations are best based on current clinical guidelines and systematic reviews.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an interpretive one, and no research programme studies orthoses specifically within the framework. An orthosis changes the mapping from motor commands to sensory outcomes, which forces the nervous system to update its internal model of the body's dynamics. Adaptation to such changes is a case of the sensorimotor learning that Active Inference and related theories describe, as discussed for [[motor_control]].
