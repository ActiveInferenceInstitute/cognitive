# Periaqueductal Gray

## Overview
The periaqueductal gray (PAG) is the column of grey matter surrounding the cerebral aqueduct in the midbrain. It receives input from the amygdala, hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex, and projects to brainstem and spinal circuits. In this position it converts higher-level assessments of threat or injury into coordinated behavioural, autonomic and analgesic outputs.

## Organisation and function
- **Longitudinal columns.** Work in animals divides the PAG into dorsomedial, dorsolateral, lateral and ventrolateral columns (Bandler & Shipley, 1994). Dorsal and lateral columns have been linked to active defence, including escape and confrontation, with raised heart rate and blood pressure. The ventrolateral column has been linked to passive coping such as freezing and quiescence, with reduced blood pressure. This scheme is widely used but is a simplification, and the small size of the structure limits how well it can be resolved in human imaging.
- **Threat imminence.** In a human imaging study, activity shifted from prefrontal regions toward the PAG as a simulated threat came closer (Mobbs et al., 2007), consistent with the view that control passes to more reflexive circuits as danger becomes proximal.
- **Pain modulation.** The PAG drives descending pathways through the rostral ventromedial medulla that suppress nociceptive transmission in the spinal cord, and it is a site of opioid action.
- **Other roles.** It also contributes to vocalisation and to reproductive and maternal behaviours (Behbehani, 1995).

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no dedicated Active Inference model of the PAG. The link is an interpretive one: in hierarchical accounts of interoception and emotion, descending predictions about bodily state are fulfilled by autonomic and motor reflex arcs, and the PAG is a plausible brainstem stage where such predictions become coordinated actions (see [[interoception]] and [[emotion_circuits]]). This remains an analogy to be tested rather than a derivation.
