# Social Action

## Overview
In sociology, social action is conduct to which the actor attaches a subjective meaning and which, by that meaning, takes account of the behaviour of others and is oriented to it. The definition comes from Max Weber, who made it the basic unit of interpretive sociology. Behaviour counts as social action only when it is meaningfully directed at others, so two cyclists independently avoiding rain are not engaged in social action, whereas dodging one another's path is.

## Weber's types
Weber distinguished four ideal types of action by their motivational orientation. They are analytic constructs; real conduct usually mixes them.
- **Instrumentally rational** (zweckrational): action guided by expectations about the behaviour of objects and other people, used as means to the actor's own rationally weighed ends.
- **Value-rational** (wertrational): action carried out for its own sake in service of a conscious belief in an ethical, aesthetic or religious value, regardless of outcome.
- **Affectual**: action determined by emotional states.
- **Traditional**: action determined by ingrained habit.

Talcott Parsons later built a general theory of social action around the "unit act", combining an actor, ends, means, conditions and normative orientation, and linked it to the problem of social order. Other traditions diverge from this action-theoretic starting point: structural and systems approaches give priority to institutions and relations rather than to individual meaning. Phenomenological sociology, following Alfred Schutz, examines how actors interpret one another's intentions.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is conceptual rather than derived. Weber's emphasis on an actor orienting to the expected conduct of others parallels accounts in which agents hold generative models of other agents and select actions in light of predicted responses; see [[social_cognition]] and [[theory_of_mind]]. Active Inference work on shared generative models and joint action addresses some of the same questions about coordination (see [[joint_action]]), but these programmes do not adopt Weber's typology, and mapping the four types onto policy priors, preferences or habits is an interpretive exercise.
