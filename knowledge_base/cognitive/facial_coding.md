# Facial Coding

## Overview
Facial coding is the systematic annotation of movements of the face. The best known system is the Facial Action Coding System (FACS) of Ekman and Friesen (1978), which describes any visible facial movement in terms of the underlying muscle actions. It separates description from interpretation: coders record which movements occurred, and judgments about what these mean are made afterwards.

## The Facial Action Coding System
FACS divides the face into numbered **action units** (AUs), each corresponding to a contraction of one or more muscles. Examples are AU1, the inner brow raiser, AU4, the brow lowerer, AU6, the cheek raiser produced by the orbicularis oculi, and AU12, the lip corner puller produced by the zygomaticus major. Coders can also score intensity and timing. Trained human coders typically need many hours of instruction and review video frame by frame. Related approaches include electromyography, which records muscle activity directly and can detect movements too small to be visible, and automated systems based on computer vision that estimate action units from video.

## Uses
- Research on emotion, development and social interaction, including studies of infants and clinical groups.
- Analysis of spontaneous compared with posed expressions.
- Applied settings such as animation and human-computer interaction, where action units parameterise facial models.

## Limits
Action units describe movement, not feelings. Darwin (1872) argued that expressions were evolved signals, and Ekman's tradition treated certain configurations as reliable markers of basic emotions. A review by Barrett and colleagues (2019) concluded that the mapping between facial configurations and emotion categories varies across contexts, people and cultures, and that inferring emotion from facial movement alone is unreliable. Researchers disagree about how strong the universality evidence is.

## Role in Active Inference
FACS itself is theory-neutral. In predictive accounts of social perception, a face is treated as a sensory signal that an observer explains using a generative model of others' states and intentions, and expressions are actions that reduce another agent's uncertainty. This is an interpretive framing and not a tested model of facial coding.
