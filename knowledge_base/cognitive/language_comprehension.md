# Language Comprehension

## Overview
Language comprehension is the set of processes by which a listener or reader derives meaning from spoken, signed or written language. It involves several levels that operate partly in parallel: perceiving the signal, recognising words, assigning syntactic structure, integrating meaning with context, and constructing a representation of the situation the text or speech describes. Comprehension is normally rapid and automatic, but it depends on attention, memory and world knowledge.

## Levels of analysis
- **Perception and word recognition.** Acoustic or visual input is mapped to word forms and meanings (see [[speech_perception]] and [[word_recognition]]). Context influences which candidates are activated.
- **Sentence level.** Words are combined into phrases and clauses, and thematic roles such as who did what to whom are assigned (see [[sentence_processing]]).
- **Discourse level.** Comprehenders link sentences through reference and inference, for example resolving what a pronoun refers to and filling in unstated causal connections.
- **Situation models.** The Construction-Integration model of Kintsch (1988) proposes that readers first build a loosely constrained network of propositions from the text and prior knowledge, then settle on the coherent subset. Gernsbacher (1990) described comprehension as structure building, in which incoming information either maps onto the current structure or starts a new substructure.

## Individual and contextual factors
Comprehension depends on vocabulary, working memory capacity, background knowledge and reading skill. Skilled comprehenders monitor coherence and repair misunderstanding, whereas weaker comprehenders often make fewer inferences. Comprehension also differs between modalities, since speech is transient and carries prosody, whereas text can be reread.

## Relation to Active Inference
This is an active research direction, not a settled account. Predictive models describe comprehension as inference over hierarchical causes: higher levels, such as the situation model, generate predictions for lower levels, such as sentences and words, and mismatches drive revision. Comprehension of others' speech can also be cast as inferring their intentions. Experimental evidence supports prediction in language processing, but it does not single out this framework.
