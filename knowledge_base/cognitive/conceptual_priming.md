# Conceptual Priming

## Overview
Conceptual priming is a form of implicit memory in which prior processing of an item's meaning changes later performance on tasks that draw on meaning, without requiring conscious recollection of the earlier episode. A person who has recently processed the word "hammer" may, for instance, produce it more readily when asked to list tools, even if unable to recall having seen it. The phenomenon belongs to the wider set of priming effects reviewed by Tulving and Schacter (1990).

## Distinction from perceptual priming
Perceptual priming depends on the form of the stimulus, such as its visual shape or modality, and is typically measured with word-stem completion or fragment identification. Conceptual priming depends on meaning and is measured by tasks such as generating category exemplars, answering general knowledge questions or producing free associates. Manipulations during study show a double dissociation. Semantic processing at study enhances conceptual tests more than perceptual ones, and changing the modality or typeface between study and test reduces perceptual priming more than conceptual priming. These patterns fit the transfer-appropriate processing framework (Roediger, 1990).

## Neural correlates
Repeated meaning-based processing is usually accompanied by reduced activity in left inferior prefrontal and lateral temporal regions, an effect termed repetition suppression, and priming for perceptual form is associated with reductions in more posterior occipital and temporal areas (Schacter & Buckner, 1998). Conceptual priming is generally preserved in amnesia, although its preservation is less uniform than for perceptual priming, which is one reason for caution in generalising across patient groups.

## Relation to Active Inference
Repetition suppression is frequently interpreted in predictive coding terms: a repeated stimulus is better predicted, so prediction error is smaller (Auksztulewicz & Friston, 2016). Priming would then reflect altered priors or precisions in a hierarchy that encodes meaning. This is an active research programme for repetition effects in general, but accounts based on neuronal fatigue or sharpening of representations remain competitors, and no model is specific to conceptual priming.
