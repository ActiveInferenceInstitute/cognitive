# Speech Acts

## Overview
Speech act theory holds that to say something is often to do something. Uttering "I promise to come" is not a description of a promise but the making of one, and "I name this ship..." changes a social fact. The theory, founded by J. L. Austin and developed by John Searle, moved the analysis of language from truth conditions of sentences toward the actions that speakers perform with them. It is a core part of [[pragmatics]].

## Structure of an act
Austin (1962) separated three aspects of one utterance.
- **Locutionary act**: producing a meaningful sentence with a given sense and reference.
- **Illocutionary act**: the action performed in speaking, such as asserting, asking, warning, ordering or promising. Its type is the illocutionary force.
- **Perlocutionary act**: the effect on the hearer, such as persuading, alarming or convincing, which may or may not occur.

Searle (1969, 1976) analysed illocutionary acts by their felicity conditions and sorted them into five broad classes: assertives, which commit the speaker to the truth of a proposition; directives, which try to get the hearer to act; commissives, which commit the speaker to future action; expressives, which convey attitudes; and declarations, which bring about a state of affairs by being uttered under suitable authority. An act can misfire if its conditions fail, for example if the speaker lacks authority or sincerity.

Indirect speech acts are common: "Could you open the window?" is grammatically a question but functions as a request. Interpreting them requires inference about the speaker's goals, which connects the topic to [[theory_of_mind]] and to the study of intentional [[communication]].

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is conceptual rather than derived. Active Inference casts behaviour as policies chosen to realise preferred outcomes, and utterances can be treated as one kind of action whose consequence is a change in another agent's beliefs or behaviour. This mirrors the perlocutionary and illocutionary distinction: an agent selects words to bring about an expected state of its social environment, and the listener infers the underlying intent. Models of communicating agents that share a generative model make this concrete in simple settings. They do not yet capture the institutional facts that declarations create, so speech act theory remains mainly a philosophical resource that sets the explanatory targets for such models. See [[social_cognition_detailed]].
