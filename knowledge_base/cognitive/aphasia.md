# Aphasia

## Overview
Aphasia is an acquired loss or impairment of language caused by damage to the brain, most often in the left hemisphere. It can affect speaking, understanding, reading and writing to different degrees, while leaving general intelligence and the basic motor and sensory apparatus of speech largely intact. The most common cause is stroke; head injury, tumour, infection and neurodegenerative disease also produce it. It differs from developmental conditions such as [[specific_language_impairment]] because language had previously developed normally.

## Classical syndromes
The traditional classification, descending from nineteenth-century work by Broca and Wernicke, groups patients by fluency, comprehension and repetition.
- **Broca-type (non-fluent) aphasia**: effortful, halting speech with simplified grammar, relatively preserved comprehension of simple sentences, and damage often involving left inferior frontal regions.
- **Wernicke-type (fluent) aphasia**: fluent, well-articulated speech that may contain wrong or invented words, with poor comprehension; lesions typically involve posterior temporal cortex (see [[superior_temporal]]).
- **Conduction aphasia**: fluent speech and good comprehension but disproportionate difficulty repeating.
- **Global aphasia**: severe impairment of all language functions after extensive damage.
- **Anomia**: difficulty retrieving words, which is a feature of nearly all types (see [[lexical_access]]).

Modern accounts treat these labels as clusters rather than sharp categories. Language depends on distributed networks, often described as dorsal and ventral streams linking sound, meaning and articulation, and lesion patterns rarely match the textbook picture exactly. Recovery is variable and involves both restoration of damaged tissue function and recruitment of other regions, and speech-language therapy is the main treatment.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is indirect. Predictive accounts of language comprehension and production treat the brain as generating expectations about upcoming words and sounds, and aphasia can be read as a loss of the representations or connections that supply those expectations. Computational lesion studies in connectionist models of word retrieval have reproduced several aphasic error patterns, and Active Inference models of communication between two agents sharing a generative model exist. However, there is not yet an established Active Inference programme that models specific aphasic syndromes; the connection is an interpretive framing, useful for thinking about how damaged hierarchical models would degrade prediction, rather than a tested derivation. See [[language_processing]] and [[predictive_processing]].
