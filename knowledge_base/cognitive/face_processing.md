# Face Processing

## Overview
Face processing is the set of computations by which the visual system detects a face, identifies who it belongs to, and extracts changeable information such as expression, gaze and speech movements. It is a highly practised, socially critical skill and a standard test case for questions of domain-specific processing and perceptual expertise.

## Functional and neural organisation
**Functional models.** Bruce and Young (1986) proposed separate routes for recognising identity and for analysing expression, with a shared structural encoding stage. The separation is now regarded as partial, since the two interact.

**Holistic processing.** Faces are processed as integrated wholes rather than as lists of parts. The face inversion effect (Yin, 1969) shows that recognition is disproportionately impaired for upside-down faces, taken as evidence for configural coding sensitive to the spatial relations among features.

**Neural network.** Haxby, Hoffman and Gobbini (2000) distinguished a core system in visual cortex (occipital face area, fusiform face area, superior temporal sulcus) from an extended system (amygdala, insula, frontal regions) for emotion, attention and meaning. The fusiform face area responds more to faces than to other objects (Kanwisher, McDermott and Chun, 1997). Whether this reflects an innately specified module or the outcome of experience-driven expertise is disputed.

**Disorders.** Prosopagnosia is a selective difficulty recognising faces, either acquired after brain injury or developmental, with relatively preserved object recognition.

Faces from familiar and unfamiliar people are processed differently: recognising a familiar face is reliable across viewpoint and lighting, whereas matching unfamiliar faces is error-prone.

## Role in Active Inference
Face perception is used as an example of hierarchical predictive coding in which higher areas predict the presence and configuration of a face and lower areas report the residual error. Expectations about faces change responses in face-selective cortex, consistent with this account. Apps and Tsakiris (2014) used the framework to analyse self-face recognition as the integration of multisensory prediction. These applications are interpretive extensions of the framework to a well-studied domain, and the specific claims about face-area computation are hypotheses under test.
