# Muscle Synergies

## Overview
A muscle synergy is a pattern of coactivation across a set of muscles that is recruited as a single unit. On this view, the nervous system does not specify the activity of each muscle independently but combines a small number of synergies, each with its own weighting across muscles and its own time course, to build a wide range of movements. The idea addresses the redundancy problem formulated by Bernstein (1967): the body has many more muscles and joints than are required for a task, so the number of possible solutions is very large.

## Evidence
- **Spinalised frogs.** Tresch, Saltiel and Bizzi (1999) reported that the hindlimb muscle activation patterns elicited in frogs by cutaneous stimulation could be accounted for by flexible combinations of a small set of muscle synergies.
- **Natural behaviour.** d'Avella, Saltiel and Bizzi (2003) found that muscle patterns of intact, unrestrained frogs kicking in different directions could be described as combinations of three time-varying synergies, with recruitment related to movement kinematics and some similarity to synergies from other behaviours.
- **Humans.** Similar decompositions have been used for walking, reaching, posture and cycling, and the number of synergies needed is often lower than the number of muscles recorded. In clinical settings, changes in synergy structure have been reported after stroke.

## Extraction and interpretation
Synergies are typically extracted from electromyographic recordings with matrix factorisation techniques such as non-negative matrix factorisation or principal component analysis (see [[dimension_reduction]]). A recorded set is approximated as $\mathbf{m}(t)\approx\sum_k c_k(t)\,\mathbf{w}_k$, where each $\mathbf{w}_k$ is a fixed muscle weighting and $c_k(t)$ is its activation coefficient. A central debate is whether low-dimensional structure reflects neural modules, or whether it arises from biomechanical constraints and task demands, so that good fit alone does not show a neural origin (see [[motor_control]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
In Active Inference, movement is generated by descending predictions about proprioceptive and exteroceptive sensations, which reflex arcs fulfil (see [[motor_units]]). Low-dimensional structure in movement can be read as structure in the priors, for example in hierarchical models that specify a few movement primitives at high levels and details at lower ones. This is an interpretive link. Active Inference does not require synergies and has not shown that they are the representational format used by the nervous system.
