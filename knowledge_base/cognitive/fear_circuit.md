# Fear Circuit

## Overview
The fear circuit is the set of brain structures that detect signals of threat and generate defensive responses, including behavioural, autonomic and hormonal changes. Its best-characterised hub is the [[amygdala]], and much of the knowledge of the circuit comes from rodent studies of Pavlovian fear conditioning. Human imaging studies support a broadly similar organisation, although the mapping from animal circuits to the human experience of fear is debated.

## Organisation
In the standard scheme (LeDoux, 2000), sensory information reaches the lateral nucleus of the amygdala by two routes. A fast subcortical route runs from the thalamus directly to the amygdala, and a slower route passes through sensory cortex and gives more detailed representations. Within the amygdala, signals pass from the lateral nucleus, where associations between cues and aversive outcomes are formed, to the central nucleus, which projects to effector regions:
- the periaqueductal grey, which organises defensive behaviours such as [[freezing]];
- the hypothalamus, which drives autonomic and endocrine responses;
- brainstem nuclei involved in startle and cardiovascular change.

Tovote, Fadok and Lüthi (2015) reviewed work with cell-type-specific manipulations showing that microcircuits within the amygdala and its targets select between behaviours such as freezing and flight.

## Regulation
The medial prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus modulate the circuit. Prefrontal regions contribute to the extinction of conditioned fear and to the regulation of its expression, while the hippocampus supplies contextual information about where a threat was encountered. Dysregulation of these interactions is implicated in anxiety disorders and post-traumatic stress, though the relation between circuit findings and clinical conditions is an active research area.

## Relation to Active Inference
In Active Inference accounts, threat responses can be described as inference about hidden causes, with defensive actions selected to avoid expected aversive outcomes. Proposals link amygdala and autonomic circuitry to interoceptive inference and to the precision assigned to threat-related predictions. These are theoretical proposals with limited direct tests, and they sit alongside competing accounts, including the view that the subjective feeling of fear depends on cortical systems that are distinct from the defensive circuit.
