# Animal Cognition

## Overview
Animal cognition, also called comparative cognition, studies how non-human animals acquire, store and use information to guide behaviour. It covers perception, learning, memory, categorisation, numerical and spatial abilities, communication, tool use and social understanding. The field draws on psychology, ethology and neuroscience, and asks both what animals can do and which mechanisms underlie it.

## Methods and findings
- **Associative learning.** Much behaviour can be explained by conditioning, and these accounts form the baseline explanation against which richer ones are tested (see [[associative_learning]] and [[operant_conditioning]]).
- **Memory and planning.** In experiments with western scrub-jays, birds that had cached perishable worms and non-perishable peanuts searched preferentially for worms after a short delay and for peanuts after a long one, which Clayton and Dickinson (1998) described as episodic-like memory for what, where and when. Emery and Clayton (2004) review this and related work on corvids. Interpretation remains debated.
- **Spatial navigation.** Many species use path integration, landmarks and, in vertebrates, hippocampal and entorhinal systems (see [[spatial_cognition]] and [[path_integration]]). Insects such as ants and bees navigate with compact neural circuits (see [[social_insect_cognition]]).
- **Social cognition.** Evidence of understanding what others see or know is clearest in some primates and corvids, and interpretations are contested (see [[theory_of_mind]]).
- **Tool use.** Chimpanzees, New Caledonian crows and others make or modify tools, which suggests flexible problem solving.

## Methodological issues
Researchers guard against over-interpretation using Morgan's canon, the principle that behaviour should not be attributed to a higher mental process if a simpler one explains it. They also guard against under-attribution that results from tests designed around human abilities. Cognition in invertebrates and plants raises similar questions of definition (see [[plant_intelligence]] and [[fungal_intelligence]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The free energy principle is formulated for living systems in general, so in principle it covers animals of any taxon. In practice, Active Inference models of non-human behaviour are fewer than human ones, with work on foraging, navigation and collective behaviour in social insects as one developing area (see [[active_inference_social_insects]] and [[epistemic_foraging]]). Applying the framework to species-specific cognition is an ongoing research programme, and most claims about particular species remain hypotheses.
