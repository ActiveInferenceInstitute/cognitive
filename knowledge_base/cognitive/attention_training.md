# Attention Training

## Overview
Attention training refers to structured practice intended to change how attention is allocated or controlled: its selectivity, its sustained maintenance, its switching, or its biases toward particular kinds of stimuli. It is a subtype of [[cognitive_training]] and rests on the premise that attentional processes are plastic with practice.

## Approaches
- **Meditation-based training**: focused-attention and open-monitoring practices. Slagter and colleagues (2007) reported that an intensive retreat reduced the attentional-blink deficit, interpreted as a change in how limited attentional resources were distributed. Reviews such as Tang, Hölzel and Posner (2015) summarise associated changes in attention-related networks, with the caveat that many studies are small and lack active controls.
- **Action video games**: Green and Bavelier (2003) reported that action-game players showed better visual selective attention, and that non-players trained on an action game improved. Replication and the extent of transfer beyond trained skills remain debated.
- **Task-based drills**: repeated practice on sustained-attention, visual-search or cueing tasks, usually producing gains that are specific to the trained stimuli and task structure.
- **Bias-modification paradigms**: tasks that systematically reward orienting toward or away from stimulus classes to shift an attentional bias.

## Specificity and transfer
Improvements reliably appear on the trained task. Transfer to untrained tasks is smaller and less consistent, a pattern that mirrors findings in [[perceptual_learning]] (specificity to stimulus features and retinal location) and in broader cognitive training. Claims should therefore be tied to the measured outcome, and active control groups are needed to separate training effects from expectation effects.

## Role in Active Inference
Within predictive processing, attention is the setting of precision on prediction errors (see [[precision_weighting]], [[attention_allocation]]). Training can then be described as learning better priors over precision: which channels, features and moments are usually informative in a task. This is a reinterpretation, not a derivation, and it generates predictions mostly about task-specific learning, which matches the observed specificity. Whether meditation or game play alters precision at a general level is an empirical question the framework frames but does not settle.
