# Empathy

## Overview
Empathy is the capacity to be affected by, and to understand, the emotional state of another person. The term covers several related processes, and different authors weight them differently. Empathy is distinguished from sympathy or compassion, which involve concern for the other, and from personal distress, which is self-oriented. It is studied in developmental psychology, social neuroscience, clinical research and ethology.

## Components
Researchers commonly separate three components:
- **Affective (emotional) empathy**: sharing or resonating with another's feeling, for example through emotional contagion.
- **Cognitive empathy**: inferring what another feels or thinks, closely related to perspective taking and [[theory_of_mind]].
- **Empathic concern**: motivation to help, linked to [[cooperation]] and prosocial behaviour.

Preston and de Waal (2002) proposed a perception-action model in which perceiving another's state activates the observer's own representations of that state, with the process building on older mechanisms such as emotional contagion that are found in other mammals. Imaging studies of pain provide a well-known example. Singer and colleagues (2004) found that observing a loved one in pain engaged the anterior insula and anterior cingulate, regions also involved in the affective side of first-person pain, but not the primary somatosensory areas associated with its sensory side (see [[insula]], [[anterior_cingulate]]). Modulation by the relationship to the other person, perceived fairness and the observer's regulation strategies shows that empathic responses are not automatic and fixed (see [[emotion_regulation]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
In Active Inference accounts of social cognition, an agent models other agents as sources of sensory evidence with their own hidden states, including affective states. Friston and Frith (2015) described communication between agents sharing a generative model, in which each infers the other's states and aligns its own predictions. On this view empathic resonance is a form of inference about another's interoceptive and affective state, using the observer's own model of how such states arise. This is an active research direction with computational proposals and limited direct empirical tests, so it should be read as a framework and not as an established mechanism (see [[social_cognition]]).
