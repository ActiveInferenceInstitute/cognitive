# Disorders of Consciousness

## Overview
Disorders of consciousness are conditions, usually following severe brain injury, in which wakefulness (arousal) or awareness of self and environment is reduced or absent. Two dimensions are typically distinguished. Wakefulness depends on brainstem and thalamic arousal systems and is assessed by eye opening and sleep-wake cycles. Awareness refers to the content of experience and is inferred from purposeful behaviour. The categories below differ in how these two dimensions combine.

## Categories
- **Coma.** Eyes remain closed, there are no sleep-wake cycles, and no purposeful response occurs. It generally evolves within days to weeks into recovery, death or another state (see [[coma]]).
- **Vegetative state, or unresponsive wakefulness syndrome.** The patient opens the eyes and has sleep-wake cycles, but shows only reflexive behaviour and no evidence of awareness. The latter name was proposed to avoid the connotations of "vegetative" (Laureys et al., 2010).
- **Minimally conscious state.** Behaviour is limited but includes reproducible, though often inconsistent, signs of awareness such as following commands or purposeful movement. Giacino and colleagues (2002) set out its diagnostic criteria. Emergence is marked by reliable functional communication or object use.
- **Locked-in syndrome.** Awareness is intact but nearly all voluntary movement is lost. It is not strictly a disorder of consciousness, but it is a key diagnostic alternative.

## Assessment issues
Diagnosis rests on repeated standardised bedside examination (see [[coma_scale]]), and behavioural assessment can underestimate awareness in patients who cannot move or respond reliably. Owen and colleagues (2006) reported that a patient meeting criteria for vegetative state showed brain activation on functional imaging, when asked to imagine playing tennis or moving through a house, that resembled healthy participants, an observation that prompted work on covert awareness and on the diagnostic use of imaging and electrophysiology. Prognosis depends on cause, time since injury and state, and interpretation of such tests in individual patients remains a matter of active clinical research.

## Relation to Active Inference
Theories of consciousness within the Active Inference tradition relate awareness to hierarchical inference and to precision, and some propose that reduced levels of consciousness reflect altered inference about the self and the world. These proposals are speculative and have not been validated as clinical models.
