# Deliberate Practice

## Overview
Deliberate practice is a form of training in which a learner works on tasks just beyond their current ability, with a clear goal, immediate feedback and repeated opportunities to correct errors. The concept was set out by Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) in a study of musicians, who argued that differences in expert performance are largely explained by accumulated hours of this kind of practice, not by innate talent alone.

## Components
- **Specific goals.** Practice targets identified weaknesses and not the whole performance.
- **Full attention.** The activity demands concentration and is usually effortful and not intrinsically enjoyable.
- **Feedback.** An instructor, recording or result tells the learner how performance departs from the target.
- **Repetition with refinement.** The learner repeats, adjusts and re-tests until the component is reliable.
- **Mental representations.** Ericsson later emphasised that experts build detailed representations that guide planning and monitoring of their own performance.

Deliberate practice is contrasted with naive repetition, which can consolidate a skill at an unsatisfactory level, and with play or performance, which are not aimed at improvement. It tends to move a learner away from automatic execution, since the aim is to rework what has become routine (see [[automaticity]]).

## Evidence and critique
Correlational studies of musicians, chess players and athletes find that accumulated practice is associated with level of attainment. Meta-analytic work by Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald (2014) found that practice accounted for a smaller share of performance variance than the original claims suggested, and that the share differed by domain. Other researchers dispute the coding of activities as deliberate and point to age of starting, working memory and genetic factors. The number of hours required for expertise varies widely and no universal threshold is supported.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy. Practice that targets errors and uses feedback resembles learning driven by prediction error, with attention directed to components where predictions fail. Active Inference accounts of skill learning treat it as refinement of a generative model, but no specific model of deliberate practice has been established.
