# Guilt

## Overview
Guilt is a negative emotion that arises when a person believes they have violated a moral or social standard, or caused harm to someone, and feels responsible for it. It is classed with the self-conscious or moral emotions, which rely on the capacity to evaluate one's own conduct against internalised standards. It is typically focused on a specific act or omission rather than on the self as a whole, and it is accompanied by tension, rumination and a pull toward making amends.

## Functions and correlates
- **Interpersonal function.** Baumeister, Stillwell and Heatherton (1994) argued that guilt is primarily an interpersonal emotion that serves to maintain close relationships. It motivates apology, confession and reparation, helps to redistribute emotional burdens among partners, and discourages behaviour that would damage ties. On this view, guilt promotes cooperation (see [[cooperation]]).
- **Relation to shame.** Guilt is usually distinguished from [[shame]] by its focus on behaviour rather than the global self. Research reports that guilt-proneness is generally linked to constructive responses such as reparation, while shame-proneness is more often linked to withdrawal and defensiveness (Tangney & Dearing, 2002). The two emotions frequently co-occur, and the strength of this contrast remains debated.
- **Empathy and perspective.** Guilt depends on recognising the effect of one's action on another person, and so is related to empathy and perspective-taking, both of which are developmentally later achievements.
- **Neural correlates.** Imaging studies tend to implicate medial prefrontal, temporal and insular regions during guilt-related tasks, though results vary with the paradigm.
- **Clinical aspects.** Excessive or inappropriate guilt is a listed symptom of a major depressive episode, and pathological guilt features in obsessive-compulsive and trauma-related presentations.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is weak and conceptual. Guilt involves counterfactual reasoning about what one could have done and an estimate of one's responsibility for an outcome, which can be framed in terms of a generative model that includes other people's states and the agent's own policies. Social norms would serve as shared priors, and guilt as a signal that one's action had violated them, motivating policies that restore expected relations. No dedicated model of guilt has been developed and tested, so this is an interpretive framing only.
