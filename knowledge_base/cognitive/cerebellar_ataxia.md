# Cerebellar Ataxia

## Overview
Cerebellar ataxia is a disorder of movement coordination caused by dysfunction of the cerebellum or its connections. Strength is relatively preserved, but movements are inaccurate in force, timing and direction, and the patient has difficulty combining joints in smooth, coordinated actions. Ataxia is a sign and not a single disease, so it has many possible causes.

## Clinical features
Holmes (1939) described the characteristic signs in patients with cerebellar injury. They include dysmetria, in which reaching movements overshoot or undershoot the target, and intention tremor, which increases as the limb nears a target. Decomposition of movement breaks multi-joint actions into sequential steps, and dysdiadochokinesia is the impaired performance of rapid alternating movements. Gait becomes wide-based and unsteady, speech can be slurred and irregular in rhythm (ataxic dysarthria), and eye movements show abnormalities such as nystagmus. Signs are usually on the same side as a lateral cerebellar lesion. Schmahmann and Sherman (1998) also described a cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome, in which patients with cerebellar lesions show executive, visuospatial and language difficulties and altered affect.

## Causes
Causes include stroke, tumour, multiple sclerosis, chronic alcohol use, certain medications and toxins, vitamin deficiencies, autoimmune disease, and inherited disorders such as the spinocerebellar ataxias and Friedreich ataxia. Treatment depends on the cause. Where no specific therapy exists, management relies on physical and occupational therapy and on assistive devices.

## Internal-model account
A widely held proposal is that the cerebellum provides forward models that predict the sensory consequences of motor commands, and that supports timing and online correction (Wolpert, Miall and Kawato, 1998). Without accurate predictions, movements rely on delayed sensory feedback, and errors in timing and amplitude result. Studies of adaptation in patients show impaired motor learning, consistent with this view, but other accounts stress timing mechanisms, and the evidence does not settle which is correct.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an interpretation within an active research area. Active Inference treats motor control as the fulfilment of proprioceptive predictions, with precise predictions needed for smooth movement. Cerebellar damage could be described as degrading the predictions or their precision, but this is a conceptual mapping and not a validated clinical model.
