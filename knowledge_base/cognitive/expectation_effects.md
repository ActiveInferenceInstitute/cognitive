# Expectation Effects

## Overview
Expectation effects are changes in perception, behaviour and neural activity that follow from what an observer anticipates. Expected stimuli are typically detected and recognised faster and more accurately, and ambiguous input tends to be interpreted in line with expectation. Placebo responses, context effects in word recognition and perceptual illusions of expectation all belong to this family.

## Behavioural and neural findings
**Behaviour.** Words are recognised faster in predictive contexts, and degraded or ambiguous images are disambiguated according to preceding cues. Prediction in language is reflected in the N400, an ERP component whose amplitude is larger for words that are less expected in context (Kutas and Hillyard, 1980).

**Expectation suppression.** Expected stimuli evoke weaker neural responses in sensory cortex than unexpected ones. Summerfield et al. (2008) showed that repetition suppression in face-selective cortex is itself modulated by how likely a repetition is, indicating a role for expectation and not only neural adaptation.

**Sharpening or dampening.** Reduced overall response to expected stimuli could mean that the neural representation is sharpened, with uninformative units suppressed, or dampened, with the expected component removed. Review of the evidence finds support for both depending on conditions (de Lange, Heilbron and Kok, 2018).

**Expectation versus attention.** Attention and expectation are conceptually distinct: attention concerns task relevance and expectation concerns probability. They can have opposite effects on sensory responses, which complicates interpretations (Summerfield and Egner, 2009).

Whether the effects reflect changed perception or changed response bias or decision criteria is a continuing methodological concern.

## Role in Active Inference
Predictive processing accounts use expectation effects as a core line of evidence. Priors generate top-down predictions that explain away expected input, so only prediction errors ascend; this predicts suppression for expected stimuli. Precision weighting sets how much sensory evidence is allowed to override priors, and thereby explains how the same expectation can strengthen or weaken perception depending on estimated reliability. Because simple predictive coding favours dampening, observed sharpening requires further assumptions, such as precision-weighted prediction errors. The framework fits the data but does not uniquely predict them, as other accounts (for example, neural adaptation or efficient coding) also explain some of the suppression.
