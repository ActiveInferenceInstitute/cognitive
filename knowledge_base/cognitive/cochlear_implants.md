# Cochlear Implants

## Overview
A cochlear implant is a surgically implanted device that restores a sense of sound to people with severe to profound sensorineural hearing loss, usually caused by loss of the sensory hair cells of the cochlea. Instead of amplifying sound as a hearing aid does, it bypasses the damaged hair cells and stimulates the auditory nerve directly with electrical currents. It is among the most successful neural prostheses (see [[neuroprosthetics]]).

## Design
- **External components.** A microphone and sound processor break incoming sound into frequency bands and extract envelope information, and a transmitter sends the signal across the skin.
- **Internal components.** A receiver under the skin drives an electrode array inserted into the scala tympani of the cochlea. Electrodes near different positions along the tonotopic axis stimulate different populations of nerve fibres, so that high frequencies are assigned to the base and low frequencies to the apex (see [[auditory_processing]]).
- **Coding strategies.** Wilson and colleagues (1991) showed that continuous interleaved sampling, which presents pulses to the electrodes in non-overlapping sequence to limit interaction, markedly improved speech recognition. Modern processors build on this idea.

## Outcomes and limitations
Many recipients reach good open-set speech recognition in quiet, and children implanted early can acquire spoken language, although results vary with age at implantation, duration of deafness and other factors (see [[critical_periods]]). The device conveys a coarse spectral representation, since only a limited number of independent channels can be used, and the fine structure that supports music, pitch perception and speech in noise is poorly transmitted (Wilson and Dorman, 2008). Bilateral implantation and combining electrical with residual acoustic hearing can improve localisation and hearing in noise.

## Brain adaptation
The signal received is unlike normal hearing, and recipients typically need months of experience to interpret it. Learning involves [[neural_plasticity]] and [[perceptual_learning]], and early experience with deafness changes the organisation of auditory cortex, including recruitment of auditory areas by other senses.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy. Adapting to an implant can be described as the brain updating its generative model of how sounds map onto neural activity, so that the new, impoverished input becomes explicable and informative predictions can be formed, which also fits the importance of context and top-down knowledge for speech understanding with implants (see [[predictive_processing]] and [[speech_perception]]). This description is interpretive and has not been developed into specific quantitative models of implant use.
