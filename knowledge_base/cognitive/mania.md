# Mania

## Overview
Mania is a distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive or irritable mood, together with abnormally increased goal-directed activity or energy, lasting at least one week or of any duration if hospitalisation is required (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). It is the defining feature of bipolar I disorder (see [[bipolar_disorder]]). Hypomania is a similar but milder and shorter state, lasting at least four days, that does not cause marked impairment. Manic symptoms can also be produced by substances, medications and medical conditions.

## Features and mechanisms
- **Symptoms.** Diagnosis requires several accompanying features, such as inflated self-esteem or grandiosity, reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts or flight of ideas, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity or agitation, and engagement in risky activities with potential for harm.
- **Severity.** Episodes may involve psychotic features, such as delusions of grandeur, and can seriously affect work, finances and relationships. Insight is often limited during the episode.
- **Proposed mechanisms.** One influential account describes mania as a disorder of reward and goal pursuit, in which heightened sensitivity to reward cues and excessive confidence in goal attainment drive escalating activity (Johnson, 2005). Dopaminergic involvement is often proposed, partly because stimulant drugs can provoke manic-like states, but the evidence is indirect (see [[dopamine_system]]).
- **Treatment.** Lithium, anticonvulsant mood stabilisers and antipsychotic drugs are used for acute episodes and prevention, often with psychosocial support (Goodwin & Jamison, 2007).

## Relation to Active Inference
No Active Inference model of mania has been validated. A conceptual proposal builds on the idea that mood tracks the agent's estimate of how well it is reducing prediction error (Clark et al., 2018). If this estimate is inflated, the agent would assign excessive precision to its policies and to predictions of success, producing overconfident action, reduced sensitivity to disconfirming evidence and a diminished perceived need for rest. This reading is consistent with reward-based accounts, but it is speculative and, at present, not supported by dedicated experimental tests.
