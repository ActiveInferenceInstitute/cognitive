# Pure Alexia

## Overview
Pure alexia, also called alexia without agraphia, letter-by-letter reading or word-form dyslexia, is an acquired reading impairment that occurs after brain damage in people who previously read normally. Patients can write, spell aloud and speak normally and can understand spoken language, yet cannot read fluently, and they often cannot read what they have written themselves. The disorder was described by Dejerine in 1892. It is distinguished from other acquired alexias that accompany aphasia ([[alexia]]) and from developmental [[dyslexia]], which is not caused by acquired lesions.

## Clinical features
The most characteristic sign is a letter-by-letter strategy. The patient identifies each letter, sometimes by naming or tracing it, and then assembles the word, so reading time increases with the number of letters in a word (Warrington and Shallice, 1980). Letter identification may itself be slow or error-prone, but the impairment is more severe for words than the identification of single letters would predict. Recognition of objects and faces is usually adequate, and numbers and other non-alphabetic symbols may be read more easily than words.

## Neural basis
The typical lesion involves the left occipital or occipitotemporal cortex, or the white matter that carries visual information to language areas. Damasio and Damasio (1983) proposed a disconnection mechanism, in which a left occipital lesion that also affects the splenium of the corpus callosum prevents visual information from the intact right hemisphere from reaching language regions in the left hemisphere. A different account locates the deficit in the left ventral occipitotemporal cortex, where a region now called the visual word form area responds to written words ([[visual_word_form]]). Cohen and colleagues (2000) located this region in healthy readers and in patients with posterior callosal lesions, which bears on how the two accounts relate. The relative importance of the two mechanisms is still discussed, and both may apply in different patients.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an interpretation of the ventral reading system. In predictive accounts, the occipitotemporal region combines bottom-up visual evidence with top-down predictions from language areas, and reading depends on the interaction of the two (Price and Devlin, 2011). Damage that weakens the bottom-up input or the interaction would be expected to cause a reliance on slower, serial strategies. This account is a framing consistent with the clinical picture, not an established model of pure alexia.
