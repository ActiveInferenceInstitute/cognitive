# Stimulants

## Overview
Stimulants are psychoactive substances that increase alertness, wakefulness and activity of the central nervous system. They include caffeine, nicotine, amphetamines, methylphenidate and cocaine. Their effects depend on dose, and on the individual and context, and the substances differ greatly in mechanism, potency and risks. This page describes their actions in general terms and does not offer medical advice.

## Mechanisms
- **Catecholamine enhancers.** Amphetamines and methylphenidate increase extracellular dopamine and noradrenaline. Methylphenidate and cocaine block the transporters that clear these transmitters from the synapse, and amphetamines also promote their release (Brunton et al., 2018).
- **Adenosine antagonism.** Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, mainly A1 and A2A, and so opposes the sleep-promoting and inhibitory actions of adenosine, with downstream effects on dopamine signalling (Fredholm et al., 1999).
- **Nicotinic activation.** Nicotine acts on acetylcholine receptors of the nicotinic type and promotes release of several transmitters.

## Cognitive effects
In moderate doses, stimulants tend to improve vigilance, reaction time and sustained attention, especially in people who are fatigued. Effects on higher functions such as working memory and executive control are more variable and depend on baseline performance and dose, and an inverted-U relation between arousal and performance has often been proposed. High doses can impair performance, cause anxiety, and affect sleep, and repeated use of some stimulants carries a risk of dependence. Medically supervised use of certain stimulants for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder is established clinical practice.

## Relation to Active Inference
In Active Inference accounts, dopamine is associated with encoding the precision of beliefs about policies (Friston et al., 2012), and noradrenaline and acetylcholine have been linked in related proposals to the precision of other quantities. Under this reading, a stimulant that raises catecholamine signalling raises the confidence placed on certain predictions or prediction errors, affecting how attention is allocated and how decisively actions are chosen. These are theoretical proposals supported by simulations and some pharmacological studies and are not a settled description of drug action.
