# Social Anxiety

## Overview
Social anxiety is marked fear or anxiety about social situations in which a person may be scrutinised or judged negatively. It occurs on a continuum, from common shyness to the clinical condition called social anxiety disorder. This page describes cognitive and computational accounts as hypotheses under active research; it makes no treatment claims.

## Cognitive accounts
- **Core feature.** The central concern is evaluation by others, with fear of showing anxiety symptoms or acting in ways judged as inadequate.
- **Cognitive model.** In the model of Clark and Wells, a social situation activates assumptions about the self and others, which raises self-focused attention. The person then forms an impression of how they appear from internal cues such as bodily sensation and felt thoughts, and treats this as evidence about how others see them.
- **Maintaining processes.** The model highlights avoidance, subtle safety behaviours, anticipatory worry, and post-event rumination, each of which can prevent disconfirmation of the feared outcome.
- **Attention and interpretation.** Studies report biased attention to threat cues and negative interpretation of ambiguous social events, although effect sizes and causal direction are debated.

## Role in Active Inference
Computational-psychiatry accounts treat the above as properties of a generative model of social interaction. Candidate hypotheses include strong prior expectations of negative evaluation ([[prior_beliefs]]), altered precision on social and interoceptive prediction errors ([[precision_weighting]], [[interoception]]), and policy selection that favours avoidance, which reduces the evidence that could revise the priors. Models of how people infer others' beliefs about them rely on [[theory_of_mind]]. These are proposals; empirical tests that discriminate them from simpler accounts are limited.
