# Dyslexia

## Overview
Dyslexia is a specific learning difficulty marked by problems with accurate or fluent word recognition, decoding and spelling that are unexpected given a person's general ability and educational opportunity. It is neurobiological in origin, runs in families, and persists into adulthood, although its expression changes with age and with the writing system. It is not caused by poor vision or low intelligence, and it differs from acquired reading loss after brain damage (see [[aphasia]]).

## Cognitive basis
The best-supported account is a phonological deficit: difficulty representing and manipulating the speech sounds of a language. It shows up as poor phonological awareness, slow rapid naming and limited verbal short-term memory (see [[phonological_loop]]), and it hampers the mapping between letters and sounds that underlies [[word_recognition]]. Orthographic consistency matters, so the reading problem appears mainly as slowness in languages with regular spelling and as inaccuracy in languages such as English. Other proposals add deficits in rapid auditory processing, visual attention or the cerebellum; these may explain subgroups but remain debated.

Imaging studies commonly report reduced activity in left temporoparietal and occipitotemporal reading regions, with compensatory activation elsewhere, and reading intervention can alter these patterns. Early signs include delayed speech development and a family history. Structured, explicit instruction in letter-sound correspondences is the best-supported intervention, and difficulties often co-occur with attention or language disorders such as [[specific_language_impairment]].

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection to Active Inference is speculative and recent. One line of work proposes that people with dyslexia make less efficient use of recent stimulus statistics, for example by being slower to build up an expectation from repeated sounds, which would reduce the benefit of context in perceiving speech and print. This is related to the anchoring-deficit hypothesis and to findings of reduced neural adaptation to repeated stimuli. In predictive-processing language, it corresponds to priors updating too slowly or being weighted differently. The evidence is mixed, and these are hypotheses within perceptual inference rather than an established derivation or a clinical tool. See [[language_processing]].
