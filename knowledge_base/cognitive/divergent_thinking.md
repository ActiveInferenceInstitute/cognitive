# Divergent Thinking

## Overview
Divergent thinking is the production of many different possible answers to a question with no single correct response, such as listing uses for a brick. J. P. Guilford (1950, 1967) introduced the contrast with convergent thinking, which seeks the one best answer to a well-defined problem. Divergent thinking tasks are the most widely used measures of creative potential, although creative achievement involves much more than idea generation.

## Measurement
Guilford and, later, Torrance developed tests in which responses are scored on several dimensions.
- **Fluency**: the number of ideas produced.
- **Flexibility**: the number of distinct categories the ideas span.
- **Originality**: the statistical rarity or judged novelty of ideas.
- **Elaboration**: the amount of detail developed.

Typical tasks are the Alternate Uses task, consequence generation, and figural completion. Scoring is partly subjective and fluency strongly influences the other scores, so researchers increasingly use ratings by independent judges and, more recently, computational measures of semantic distance.

## Findings and cautions
Performance relates to openness to experience, to some extent to intelligence and executive functions, and varies with instructions. Telling participants to "be creative" or to be original tends to raise originality. Runco and Acar (2012) argued that divergent thinking tests indicate creative potential and not creative performance as such, and that their predictive validity depends on the scoring method and criterion. The dichotomy with convergent thinking is a simplification, since real problem solving alternates between generating options and selecting among them.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy. Generating varied candidates parallels the exploratory side of the exploration-exploitation trade-off, which in Active Inference is handled through the epistemic value of expected free energy and the entropy of policy priors. However, divergent thinking tests measure the output of semantic search in humans, and Active Inference does not currently provide a validated model of them.
