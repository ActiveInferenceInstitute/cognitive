# Musical Intelligence

## Overview
Musical intelligence is one of the intelligences in Howard Gardner's theory of multiple intelligences, presented in Frames of Mind (1983). It refers to sensitivity to pitch, rhythm, timbre and musical structure, and to the capacity to perform, compose and appreciate music. Gardner listed it among the original seven intelligences, partly because it appears early in development and is prominent in child prodigies. The term is used in education, while in psychology the same abilities are usually discussed as musical aptitude or musical ability.

## Evidence for separable music processing
- **Congenital amusia.** Some people with normal hearing and general intelligence have lifelong difficulty perceiving pitch relations and recognising melodies.
- **Acquired deficits.** Brain damage can impair music perception while sparing speech comprehension, and the reverse pattern also occurs, which supports at least partial separation of the underlying systems (Peretz and Zatorre, 2005).
- **Neural specialisation.** Imaging shows distributed networks involving auditory cortex, frontal regions and motor areas, with some right-lateralised pitch processing. The degree to which music uses mechanisms distinct from language remains debated (see [[auditory_processing]] and [[rhythm_perception]]).

## Criticism
Gardner's theory has been criticised for relying on informal criteria and for lacking support from psychometric studies. Measures of musical aptitude correlate with other abilities and with [[g_factor]] to a modest degree, and critics argue that such abilities are better described as skills or talents than as independent intelligences. Training studies of transfer from music lessons to general cognition give mixed results.

## Relation to Active Inference
Music perception is a prominent application of predictive processing. Listeners form expectations about melody, harmony and rhythm, and musical affect and pleasure are linked to the interplay of fulfilled and violated predictions, with syncopation and tension as examples (Koelsch, Vuust and Friston, 2019; see [[predictive_processing]]). The framework provides a well-supported general account of expectation in music, but it does not itself settle whether musical intelligence is a distinct faculty.
