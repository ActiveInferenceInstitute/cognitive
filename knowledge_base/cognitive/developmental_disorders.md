# Developmental Disorders

## Overview
Developmental disorders, classified in the DSM-5 as neurodevelopmental disorders, are conditions with onset in childhood that alter the typical trajectory of cognitive, language, motor or social development. Examples include autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, specific learning disorders such as dyslexia, developmental language disorder, developmental coordination disorder and intellectual disability. This page describes cognitive and computational perspectives only; it gives no diagnostic or treatment guidance.

## Cognitive perspective
Cognitive accounts ask which component of a normally developing system differs, and how an early difference propagates through later development. Examples include phonological processing in dyslexia, executive control and inhibition in ADHD, and social-communication processes such as joint attention and belief attribution in autism. Three features complicate any single-deficit explanation. Conditions overlap heavily, so comorbidity is the rule rather than the exception. Each is heterogeneous, with different profiles across individuals. And development is interactive: an early atypicality changes the input a child receives, shaping later learning. Many researchers also describe these differences within a neurodiversity framework that separates variation in cognition from the difficulties imposed by environments.

## Role in Active Inference
Predictive-processing accounts treat development as the learning of a generative model and ask whether atypical precision or prior settings alter that learning. For autism, Pellicano and Burr (2012) proposed that perception relies on weaker priors, and Lawson, Rees and Friston (2014) proposed aberrantly high sensory precision as an alternative. These are competing hypotheses with mixed empirical support, and they were formulated mainly for autism; extensions to ADHD or language disorders are more tentative. The strength of the connection is therefore an active research programme within computational psychiatry rather than an established explanation. Critical periods are relevant because the timing of precision and plasticity changes would determine which representations stabilise.
