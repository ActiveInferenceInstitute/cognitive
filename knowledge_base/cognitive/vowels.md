# Vowels

## Overview
Vowels are speech sounds produced with a relatively open vocal tract, without a constriction that would cause audible friction or a complete closure. They are normally voiced and are the typical peaks, or nuclei, of syllables (see [[syllable_structure]]). They contrast with [[consonants]], which involve greater constriction. The line is not absolute: glides such as English /j/ and /w/ are vowel-like in articulation but behave as consonants in syllable structure.

## Description
- **Articulatory dimensions.** Vowels are classified by tongue height (close to open), backness (front to back) and lip rounding. Other features include length, nasalisation and voice quality. The cardinal vowel system, devised by Daniel Jones, provides reference points for transcription.
- **Acoustic structure.** Vowels are characterised by formants, which are resonance peaks of the vocal tract that appear as bands of energy in a spectrogram. The first formant, F1, varies inversely with tongue height, so that open vowels have high F1. The second formant, F2, varies with tongue backness, so that front vowels have high F2, and lip rounding lowers it. Plotting F1 against F2 recovers a shape close to the articulatory vowel chart.
- **Systems.** Languages differ in how many vowel qualities they contrast. Five-vowel systems, such as /i e a o u/, are common, while others have many more, and some add contrasts of length, nasalisation or tone (Ladefoged and Maddieson, 1996).

## Perception
Vowel productions overlap considerably across speakers: in a classic study of American English, the F1-F2 values of different vowel categories showed overlap across men, women and children, so listeners must normalise for speaker (Peterson and Barney, 1952). Infants' perception becomes tuned to the vowels of the native language during the first year (Kuhl et al., 1992). Within a category, listeners are less sensitive to differences near the category prototype, the perceptual magnet effect.

## Relation to Active Inference
Feldman, Griffiths and Morgan (2009) explained the perceptual magnet effect as optimal statistical inference: a listener who combines a noisy percept with a prior distribution over category members will shift estimates toward the category centre. That treatment is Bayesian perception within the family of ideas underlying predictive processing, and it makes quantitative predictions about categorisation. It does not commit to the more specific machinery of Active Inference, such as expected free energy.
