# Familiarity

## Overview
Familiarity is the feeling that a stimulus has been encountered before, in the absence of retrieval of when, where or how. A face may seem known although its owner's name and the circumstances of meeting cannot be recalled. In dual-process theories of recognition, familiarity is one of two bases for a "yes, I have seen this" judgement; the other is [[recollection]], which retrieves specific contextual detail. The distinction was articulated by Mandler (1980), who described recognition as based on both an intra-item familiarity process and an elaborative retrieval process.

## Characteristics
Familiarity is typically described as fast, graded and based on a single strength-like signal, in contrast with the all-or-none, detail-rich character often attributed to recollection. It is commonly measured by:
- **Remember/know judgements**, where "know" reports reflect familiarity without recollection;
- **Receiver operating characteristic analyses** of recognition confidence, in which the curve is interpreted as containing a curvilinear, signal-detection component and a threshold-like component;
- **Process-dissociation procedures**, which set recollection and familiarity against each other.

Yonelinas (2002) reviewed three decades of such evidence and concluded that the two processes are functionally dissociable. Whether they reflect separate memory systems or different strengths of one signal remains a point of debate.

## Neural basis
Neuropsychological and imaging studies have linked recollection particularly to the hippocampus, and familiarity to the perirhinal cortex and related parahippocampal regions, although this mapping is contested and some findings support a single-system view. Familiarity can also be affected independently of recollection; it is generally better preserved than recollection in healthy ageing, though results differ between studies.

## Relation to Active Inference
Familiarity can be read as a signal that the current input is well predicted by a stored model: a familiar stimulus is one that produces little prediction error. Repetition suppression, the reduced neural response to repeated stimuli, is often interpreted in predictive-processing terms for this reason (see [[prediction_error]]). That interpretation is a hypothesis, and repetition suppression has other proposed explanations. Recollection would correspond to reinstating a full contextual state.
