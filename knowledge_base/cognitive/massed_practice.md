# Massed Practice

## Overview
Massed practice is a schedule in which learning or rehearsal episodes are bunched together with little or no time between them, as in cramming for an examination or repeating a skill for hours in one block. It is contrasted with [[distributed_practice]], in which the same amount of study is spread over longer intervals. The benefit of spacing learning episodes, known as the spacing effect, has been documented since Ebbinghaus's memory experiments of the nineteenth century.

## Evidence
- **Verbal learning.** A quantitative synthesis of many studies found that, for equal study time, longer gaps between study episodes generally produce better retention, with the best gap depending on how long the material must be retained (Cepeda and colleagues, 2006).
- **Immediate versus delayed tests.** Massed schedules often look competitive on a test given soon after study. The disadvantage appears at delay, which can lead learners to overrate how well cramming works.
- **Applied reviews.** In a widely cited review of learning techniques, distributed practice was rated as having high utility for students across materials and ages (Dunlosky and colleagues, 2013).
- **Motor skills.** Results are less uniform. Fatigue can reduce the quality of long continuous practice, while for some tasks the difference is smaller or depends on the task's complexity.

## Explanations
Accounts of the spacing effect include deficient processing, in which a repeated item that is still active in mind receives less encoding effort, and study-phase retrieval, in which a longer gap makes retrieval of the earlier episode more effortful and therefore more beneficial. Contextual variability and consolidation between sessions have also been proposed (see [[memory_consolidation]]). These accounts are not mutually exclusive.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct Active Inference derivation of the spacing effect. A loose connection is that learning rates and the value of re-sampling depend on how much belief uncertainty remains, which gives a qualitative reason for revisiting items later. Treated as a theoretical analogy, this has not been developed into a tested model.
