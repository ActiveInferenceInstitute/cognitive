# Prototype Effects

## Overview
Prototype effects are the systematic differences among members of a category in how well they represent it. In many everyday categories, some members are judged better examples than others: a robin is rated as more typical of the category bird than a penguin. Rosch (1975) showed that such ratings are consistent among participants and that they predict other behaviour. The findings challenged the classical view that categories are defined by necessary and sufficient features and that all members are equally good.

## Behavioural findings
Typicality is related to a range of measures:
- **Verification speed.** People confirm that a typical member belongs to a category faster than an atypical one (Rosch, 1975).
- **Reasoning.** Properties are projected more readily from typical members to the category than the reverse.

Rosch and Mervis (1975) linked typicality to family resemblance: the best examples share many features with other members and few with members of contrasting categories. Prototypes can also arise without having been seen. In studies using distorted dot patterns, participants classified the unseen central pattern about as accurately as the training patterns (Posner and Keele, 1968).

## Theoretical accounts
Prototype theories hold that a category is represented by a summary of its members, such as a central tendency, and that membership is judged by similarity to it. Exemplar theories hold that individual stored instances are compared with the new item and can reproduce typicality effects as well (Medin and Schaffer, 1978). Evidence distinguishes the two only in particular designs, and hybrid and rule-based models are also proposed. Typicality gradients are found for artefacts and abstract concepts as well, though some categories, such as odd numbers, have crisp definitions and graded judgements alongside them, which shows that gradedness does not always imply fuzzy membership.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is a formal analogy through Bayesian models of categorisation. A category can be described as a latent cause in a generative model with a distribution over features, and a prototype corresponds to the centre of that distribution, so typical members have high likelihood and atypical ones low likelihood. Active Inference uses such latent-state models, and learning categories is a case of the inference over hidden states and the learning of likelihood parameters described in the framework. No specific results on prototype effects have been derived from it.
