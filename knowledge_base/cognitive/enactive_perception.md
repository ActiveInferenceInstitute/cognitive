# Enactive Perception

## Overview
Enactive perception is the thesis that perceiving is not the passive reception of sensory input followed by internal construction of a picture, but something an active, embodied agent does. Perceptual content depends on what the perceiver can do. In Alva Noë's formulation, perceiving is a way of acting, and the world is experienced as present through the perceiver's sensorimotor skills.

## Core ideas
- **Sensorimotor contingencies.** O'Regan and Noë (2001) proposed that visual experience is constituted by mastery of the lawful ways that sensory input changes with the observer's movement. The difference between seeing and hearing, for example, is the difference in these laws and not in the activated cortical area.
- **Skilful access.** An object looks three-dimensional because the observer knows, implicitly, how its appearance would change with eye, head and body movement. The world serves as an external memory, available for sampling on demand.
- **Enaction.** The term enactive in the broader tradition of Varela, Thompson and Rosch (1991) denotes the co-determination of organism and environment, in which cognition emerges from the organism's activity and its self-maintaining organisation.
- **Evidence cited.** Supporters point to change blindness, to sensory substitution devices which allow tactile or auditory input to support spatial perception after training, and to adaptation to distorting lenses.

## Criticisms
Critics argue that the sensorimotor account does not explain the phenomenal character of experience, that people can perceive without movement, as in brief presentations or paralysis, and that the evidence for its central claims is indirect. Defenders reply that what matters is possession of sensorimotor knowledge, not actual movement.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference shares the emphasis on action in perception: agents sample the world to confirm predictions and resolve uncertainty, and expected consequences of movement are part of the generative model. Some authors regard it as a formalisation of enactive ideas, while others, such as Bruineberg and colleagues (2018), argue that the free energy principle fits an ecological-enactive perspective only if the brain is not treated as an inferential model builder. The relationship is a live debate, not a settled equivalence.
