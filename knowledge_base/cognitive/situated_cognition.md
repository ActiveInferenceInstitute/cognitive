# Situated Cognition

## Overview
Situated cognition is the view that cognitive activity cannot be fully understood as the manipulation of internal representations in isolation, because it depends on the particular setting in which an agent acts. The setting includes the body, the physical environment, tools, other people and cultural practices. The approach emerged in the 1980s and 1990s from anthropology, educational psychology, robotics and philosophy, partly as a reaction against symbolic accounts of mind as a general-purpose computer.

## Key claims
- **Action is responsive to circumstance.** Suchman (1987), from ethnographic studies of people using a photocopier, argued that plans serve as resources for action but do not determine it; purposeful action is improvised in response to the unfolding situation.
- **Knowledge is tied to activity.** Brown, Collins and Duguid (1989) argued in an educational context that knowledge is partly a product of the activity, context and culture in which it is learned and used, and that decontextualised school tasks may therefore transfer poorly. The related idea of legitimate peripheral participation describes learning as growing participation in a community of practice.
- **The environment does cognitive work.** Agents offload memory, computation and structure onto objects and surroundings, a theme developed in work on distributed and extended cognition.

## Debates
Critics argue that the strong claims about transfer are not well supported, that laboratory studies of abstract reasoning demonstrate substantial context-independent processing, and that the label covers heterogeneous positions. The approach is usually grouped with embodied, embedded, enactive and extended approaches, sometimes called 4E cognition. Wilson (2002) distinguishes several distinct claims within this family, some of which are more defensible than others.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is a conceptual alignment that is actively discussed. Active Inference describes an agent as inferring and acting within its niche, with perception and action driven by the same free energy minimisation, so sensorimotor coupling with the environment is built into the framework. Clark and others have argued that predictive processing shows how bodily action and environmental structure can reduce the burden on internal models. Whether the framework implies the stronger anti-representational claims of some situated approaches is disputed, since it makes essential use of internal generative models.
