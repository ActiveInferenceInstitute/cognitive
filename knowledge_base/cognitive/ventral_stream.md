# Ventral Stream

## Overview
In dual-stream models of speech perception, the ventral stream is the pathway that maps acoustic speech onto meaning, by way of lexical and semantic representations in the temporal lobe. It is the counterpart of the [[dorsal_stream]], which maps sound to articulation. Hickok and Poeppel (2007) developed the model, and it parallels the earlier division of visual cortex by Ungerleider and Mishkin (1982) into a ventral pathway for object identity and a dorsal pathway for spatial relations.

## Anatomy and function
Speech sounds are first analysed in primary and surrounding auditory cortex and in the superior temporal gyrus and sulcus, where phonological representations are thought to be formed. The ventral route then runs toward the posterior middle temporal and inferior temporal regions, which are proposed to form an interface between phonological and lexical-semantic information, and toward the anterior temporal lobe, which is associated with semantic combination. In the model the ventral stream is largely bilateral, in contrast to the stronger left lateralisation of the dorsal stream. Its disruption is associated with impaired comprehension, as in some forms of aphasia, and with semantic deficits (see [[aphasia]] and [[semantic_memory]]). Saur and colleagues (2008) identified white-matter routes in the extreme capsule linking temporal and frontal regions as candidate ventral pathways for sentence comprehension.

## Visual ventral stream
In vision the ventral stream runs from primary visual cortex through V2 and V4 to inferior temporal cortex and supports recognition of objects, faces and scenes. Goodale and Milner (1992) argued that it supports perception while the dorsal stream supports visually guided action. The same concept therefore appears in both vision and language, with the shared idea being a "what" pathway.

## Relation to Active Inference
The ventral stream is a natural substrate for hierarchical generative models, in which successive areas represent increasingly abstract causes of sensory input, from acoustic features through phonemes and words to meaning. Predictive-coding accounts of language comprehension and of object recognition take this hierarchy as their anatomical scaffold. The link is architectural and has substantial supportive evidence, especially for prediction effects in sentence comprehension, but there is no single agreed formal model, and the strict "what versus where" division is itself an oversimplification.
