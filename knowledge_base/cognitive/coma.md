# Coma

## Overview
Coma is a state of profound, sustained unresponsiveness in which a person does not open their eyes, shows no sleep-wake cycle, and does not respond purposefully to stimulation. It is distinguished from sleep because the person cannot be roused, and from the [[vegetative_state]] because eye opening and cycles of wakefulness are absent. It usually lasts days to a few weeks; many patients die, recover, or evolve into a vegetative or minimally conscious state.

## Causes and mechanism
Two broad routes lead to coma. Diffuse bilateral dysfunction of both cerebral hemispheres, as in severe hypoxia, metabolic disturbance, poisoning or widespread injury, can abolish responsiveness. Alternatively, a focal lesion of the upper brainstem or its connections can disable the ascending arousal system (see [[reticular_activation]]), which normally maintains cortical activity compatible with [[wakefulness]]. Because awareness requires both arousal and intact cortical networks, damage to either component produces unresponsiveness.

## Assessment and prognosis
Clinical assessment relies on structured bedside scales, such as the Glasgow Coma Scale, which rates eye, verbal and motor responses, together with brainstem reflexes, imaging and electrophysiology. Prognosis depends strongly on the cause, with outcomes after traumatic injury generally better than after prolonged loss of oxygen. Brain death is a distinct condition, defined by irreversible loss of all brain function including the brainstem.

Behavioural examination can misclassify patients. Neuroimaging studies of patients who seem unresponsive have found some who can modulate brain activity on command, which has prompted use of functional imaging and electroencephalography alongside the bedside examination. This applies mainly to patients beyond the acute coma period.

## Relation to Active Inference
Coma is mainly a clinical and arousal phenomenon, and no Active Inference account of it is established. Predictive-processing work on [[consciousness]] offers a loose framing: ascending arousal systems may regulate the precision or gain of cortical signalling, so that their failure removes the conditions under which hierarchical inference can unfold. Event-related potential paradigms that test responses to violated auditory regularities have been used to probe how deep the prediction hierarchy remains active in patients with disorders of consciousness. These are research directions and analogies, not derivations.
