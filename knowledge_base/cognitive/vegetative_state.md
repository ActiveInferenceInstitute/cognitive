# Vegetative State

## Overview
The vegetative state is a condition in which a person alternates between sleep-like and wake-like periods, opens their eyes, and shows reflexive behaviour, yet gives no reproducible sign of awareness of self or environment. It is often summarised as wakefulness without awareness. The term was introduced by Jennett and Plum in 1972 for patients who survived severe brain damage with preserved brainstem function. Because the label has acquired negative connotations, the descriptive term unresponsive wakefulness syndrome has also been proposed.

## Diagnosis and course
Diagnosis is clinical and behavioural: the examiner looks repeatedly for signs of command following, visual pursuit, purposeful movement or communication. The state is distinguished from [[coma]], where eyes stay closed and there is no sleep-wake cycle, and from the minimally conscious state, in which inconsistent but definite signs of awareness appear (see [[minimal_consciousness]]). Patients often arise from coma within weeks. Persistence is described by duration and cause, since a state lasting more than about a month is labelled continuing or persistent, and the probability of recovery falls with time, particularly after hypoxic injury.

Behavioural diagnosis has a substantial error rate, because a patient may be aware yet unable to produce movement. Functional imaging studies have found patients diagnosed as vegetative who could modulate brain activity when asked to imagine tasks, and electroencephalographic methods are being used for similar purposes. Such results do not show that the whole group is aware, but they have changed assessment practice and raised ethical questions about care and decision-making.

## Relation to Active Inference
The vegetative state illustrates that arousal and awareness can dissociate, a point used in theories of [[consciousness]] to separate level of consciousness from its contents. Predictive-processing accounts suggest that awareness depends on hierarchical inference with adequately weighted prediction errors, which may be impaired even when arousal systems are intact. Evidence on responses to violated auditory expectations in patients with disorders of consciousness is consistent with this picture but does not establish a specific Active Inference mechanism. Treat the link as an active research question and a framing, not a settled account. See also [[consciousness_and_awareness]].
