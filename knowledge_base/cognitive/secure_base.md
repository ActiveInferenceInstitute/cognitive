# Secure Base

## Overview
The secure base is a central idea in attachment theory, developed by John Bowlby and extended by Mary Ainsworth. A caregiver acts as a secure base when the child can leave to explore the environment, confident that the caregiver is available, and can return for comfort and protection when frightened or tired. The caregiver is therefore both a safe haven in times of distress and a platform for exploration.

## Features and evidence
Ainsworth observed infants in the Strange Situation procedure, where a child is briefly separated from and reunited with a caregiver. Reunion behaviour was used to distinguish patterns that came to be labelled secure, avoidant and ambivalent (resistant), with a disorganised pattern added later. In the secure pattern, the child uses the caregiver to settle after distress and then returns to play. Bowlby (1988) argued that repeated experience of a responsive caregiver builds internal working models, expectations about the availability of others and the worth of the self, which guide later relationships. The same idea has been applied to adult partnerships, therapy and teaching, where a trusted figure supports risk-taking.

The model has been criticised for underplaying temperament, cultural variation in caregiving, and the role of multiple caregivers. Measured associations between early attachment security and later outcomes are real but moderate, and they are not deterministic.

## Relation to Active Inference
Attachment can be described in predictive terms: a responsive caregiver makes the world more predictable, reducing uncertainty and allowing the child to tolerate the uncertainty involved in exploration. Internal working models resemble generative models of social expectation. This is an interpretive correspondence; the secure base has been discussed in this vocabulary in theoretical papers, but it is not a quantitative result of the framework. It also bears on the balance between exploring and exploiting (see [[exploration_exploitation]]), since security appears to widen the range of exploration a child will undertake.
