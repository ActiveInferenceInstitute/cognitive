# G Factor

## Overview
The g factor, or general intelligence, is a statistical construct that summarises the positive correlations among scores on diverse cognitive tests. Charles Spearman (1904) observed that children's performance across school subjects and sensory discrimination tasks correlated positively, and proposed a single general factor underlying all of them, plus a factor specific to each task. Measures such as verbal reasoning, spatial ability and arithmetic all load on g to varying degrees.

## Statistical basis
When a battery of varied tests is factor-analysed, the first unrotated factor typically accounts for a large share of the common variance, a pattern called the positive manifold. Later work replaced Spearman's single-factor model with hierarchical ones. In Carroll's three-stratum model, narrow abilities load on broad abilities, such as fluid reasoning, crystallized knowledge, memory and processing speed, which in turn load on g at the top. Bifactor and other alternative models fit data comparably well in many datasets, so the statistical structure alone does not establish that g is a single psychological entity.

## Correlates
General ability scores predict academic achievement, occupational performance and some health outcomes at the population level, and they are partly heritable. Correlations with measures such as working memory capacity, reaction time and processing speed are reported, though their size depends on the tasks used. These are associations at the group level and they do not imply fixed potential for any individual.

## Interpretation
Explanations of the positive manifold differ. Some propose a general mental property, such as neural efficiency or executive attention. Others, including mutualism models and sampling theories, show that positive correlations can emerge from many partly overlapping processes or from reciprocal growth among abilities without any single underlying cause.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no developed link. Active Inference does not currently model individual differences in psychometric intelligence, and any mapping, for example to the quality of hierarchical generative models, would be conjecture.
