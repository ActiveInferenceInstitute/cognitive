# Ecological Rationality

## Overview
Ecological rationality is the view that no strategy is rational or irrational in itself. A strategy is judged by how well it performs in a particular environment, given the limits on time, information and computation under which the decision-maker works. The concept was developed by Gerd Gigerenzer, Peter Todd and the ABC Research Group as the normative companion to their work on simple heuristics. It descends from Herbert Simon's observation that how well an organism can behave depends on the structure of its environment (Simon, 1956), often put as the image of a pair of scissors whose blades are the mind and the environment.

## Core claims
- **Fit, not optimality in general.** A heuristic that ignores most available information can match or beat a complex model when the environment has the right structure. Comparative study of strategies therefore asks in which environments each does well, not which is best in the abstract.
- **Less can be more.** In the "take-the-best" heuristic of Gigerenzer and Goldstein (1996), choices between two options are made by checking cues in order of validity and stopping at the first that discriminates. They reported that in a test on city sizes it could do as well as or better than several linear models that used all of the information.
- **Structure of environments.** Properties such as the distribution of cue validities, the redundancy of cues, and how much data is available for estimating a model determine which strategies fit.
- **Adaptive toolbox.** The mind is described as holding a set of specialised heuristics that are matched to tasks, in place of a single general-purpose calculus.

## Contrast with the heuristics-and-biases programme
Studies of departures from logic or probability theory (see [[availability_heuristic]] and [[representativeness_heuristic]]) treat the departures as errors. Work on ecological rationality asks whether the same behaviour is adaptive when the stimuli and the information format match what the environment normally provides. This is a disagreement about the standard of evaluation as much as about the facts, and the two programmes often use different tasks (see also [[ecological_validity]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is conceptual. Active Inference describes an agent whose generative model embodies the statistical regularities of its niche, so that its inference is adapted to its environment, which is in the same spirit as the claim that cognition should be evaluated relative to environment structure. Active Inference also differs: it supplies a single normative objective, variational free energy, together with approximate inference schemes, while the ecological rationality programme emphasises a toolbox of distinct heuristics. Whether specific heuristics can be derived as approximations to free energy minimisation is a research question, not a settled result.
