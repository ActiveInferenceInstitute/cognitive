# Anxiety

## Overview
Anxiety is a future-oriented state of apprehension and heightened vigilance in response to anticipated or uncertain threat. In normal ranges it is adaptive; when persistent, disproportionate, and impairing it is classified within the anxiety disorders. This page describes research hypotheses and is not clinical advice; it makes no treatment claims.

## Mechanism
Anxiety is distinguished from fear: fear is a response to a present, specific threat, whereas anxiety concerns potential threat and involves risk assessment, hypervigilance, and avoidance. Neural work implicates the amygdala, the anterior insula, the [[anterior_cingulate]], and the [[medial_prefrontal]] and ventrolateral prefrontal regions that regulate them ([[emotion_regulation]]). Cognitive findings include attentional bias toward threatening stimuli and interpretation bias for ambiguous information.

Computational research emphasises uncertainty. One line of work studies how people learn the volatility of aversive environments; Browning and colleagues reported that people with higher trait anxiety adjusted learning rates less appropriately under changing aversive contingencies. Another proposes that anxious individuals have difficulty tolerating or estimating uncertainty, so ambiguous cues are treated as potentially threatening.

## Role in Active Inference
Several Active Inference hypotheses have been proposed. (1) Interoceptive: an altered balance of prior and precision in bodily signals produces persistent interoceptive prediction error, as in Paulus and Stein's insula-centred account. (2) Precision on threat: unusually high precision attached to threat-related prior beliefs or to aversive prediction errors, so evidence of safety fails to update beliefs; see [[precision_weighting]]. (3) Policy-level: low confidence about which policy will reduce expected harm, favouring avoidance and safety behaviours that prevent disconfirming evidence from arising. These hypotheses are under active research. Evidence is mixed, they are not mutually exclusive, and none is an established causal explanation of any anxiety disorder.
