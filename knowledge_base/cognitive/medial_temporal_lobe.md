# Medial Temporal Lobe

## Overview
The medial temporal lobe is the inner part of the temporal lobe. It comprises the hippocampal formation (dentate gyrus, hippocampus proper and subiculum) and the adjacent entorhinal, perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices. The amygdala lies at its anterior end and is sometimes grouped with it. Together these structures are central to the formation of long-term declarative memory, the memory for facts and events that can be consciously recalled (see [[explicit_memory]]).

## Evidence from patients
The role of the region came to prominence through the patient H.M., who in 1953 underwent bilateral removal of medial temporal tissue to treat severe epilepsy and afterwards could no longer form new declarative memories, although his intelligence, perception and short-term memory were largely intact, as were some skill-learning abilities (Scoville and Milner, 1957). Later work on other patients with more restricted lesions and on animal models led Squire and Zola-Morgan (1991) to describe a medial temporal lobe memory system that supports declarative memory, with older memories gradually becoming less dependent on it. Damage to the region is seen in Alzheimer's disease, hypoxia, encephalitis and temporal lobe epilepsy.

## Division of labour
Eichenbaum, Yonelinas and Ranganath (2007) summarise a framework in which perirhinal cortex represents individual items, parahippocampal cortex represents context and spatial layout, and the hippocampus binds items to their contexts to support recollection. Familiarity and recollection may therefore depend partly on different subregions, although the extent to which they are separable is debated, and some researchers argue that the hippocampus contributes to both.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is a reading of function rather than a derivation. Memory and imagination in this system can be treated as the construction and use of a generative model: the hippocampus is proposed to hold relational structure about scenes and episodes that is used to predict or reconstruct experience. Active Inference models of spatial navigation and scene construction use this view. They remain an active research programme, with partial empirical support, and the classical systems-level account of declarative memory does not depend on them.
