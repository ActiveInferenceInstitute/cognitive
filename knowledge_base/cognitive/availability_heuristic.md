# Availability Heuristic

## Overview
The availability heuristic is a judgment strategy in which the frequency or probability of an event is estimated by the ease with which instances or associations come to mind. Tversky and Kahneman (1973) introduced it, and it became one of the three heuristics, with representativeness and anchoring, in their 1974 account of judgment under uncertainty. Because recall tracks real frequency to a useful degree, the strategy often works, but it also produces systematic errors when ease of recall is driven by factors other than frequency.

## Evidence and biases
In a classic study, participants judged whether more English words begin with the letter K or have K as the third letter. Since words are easier to retrieve by first letter, many judged the first category to be larger, although the reverse is true. Vivid, recent, emotionally charged or heavily publicised events tend to be overestimated, for example dramatic causes of death relative to common but less salient ones. Later work distinguished two possible bases for the effect: the number of instances actually retrieved and the subjective experience of fluency in retrieving them. Experiments in which fluency and content were pulled apart suggest that both can matter, depending on how the judgment is framed.

## Boundary conditions
The effect weakens when people have good reason to discount their retrieval experience, for example when told that recall was made artificially easy or difficult. It also depends on whether the sampled memories are representative of the environment. Biased media coverage or personal experience therefore feeds into biased estimates.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an interpretation, not a derivation. Bayesian accounts treat availability as a form of approximate inference by sampling: if the brain estimates probabilities from a small number of samples drawn from memory, estimates will reflect the sampling process and its biases (see [[neural_sampling]]). This matches the view in the Active Inference literature that exact inference is intractable and that the brain implements approximations. Whether the heuristic is better described as sampling, as a precision-weighted cue or as a separate cognitive shortcut is still open.
