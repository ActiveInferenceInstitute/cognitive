# Middle Temporal Gyrus

## Overview
The middle temporal gyrus (MTG) is the broad strip of cortex on the lateral surface of the temporal lobe, lying between the superior temporal sulcus and the inferior temporal sulcus. It is part of association cortex rather than a primary sensory area, and it integrates auditory, visual and conceptual information. Its posterior part, in the left hemisphere, is the portion most often linked to language. Separately, the visual area called MT, or V5, which responds to motion, is named for the middle temporal region of the macaque and lies in humans near the occipito-temporal junction (see [[motion_perception]]); that sense is not the topic here.

## Role in language
- **Lexical-semantic retrieval.** In the dual-stream model of speech processing, posterior middle temporal and neighbouring inferior temporal regions form a lexical interface that links phonological representations to meaning (Hickok and Poeppel, 2007).
- **Word production.** Meta-analysis of imaging studies associates the left middle temporal region with retrieving the abstract lexical entry before its sound form (Indefrey and Levelt, 2004; see [[lemma_retrieval]]).
- **Semantic processing.** A meta-analysis of 120 neuroimaging studies found that a distributed network, including posterior middle temporal gyrus, inferior parietal cortex and the angular gyrus, is consistently engaged by semantic tasks (Binder and colleagues, 2009; see [[angular_gyrus]]).
- **Lesion evidence.** Damage in this area is associated with impaired word comprehension and naming, and the region lies close to classical posterior language areas (see [[wernickes_area]]).

## Other proposed functions
Left posterior MTG has also been linked to knowledge about tools and actions and to retrieval of conceptual information that is selected under competition. The MTG is not a functionally uniform area, and conclusions from group imaging cannot be applied precisely to individual cases.

## Relation to Active Inference
In hierarchical models of speech, intermediate temporal regions are proposed to hold lexical and semantic beliefs that send predictions to auditory areas and receive prediction errors from them. The mapping is plausible but descriptive, and it is not specific to the middle temporal region alone. No dedicated Active Inference account of the gyrus has been established.
