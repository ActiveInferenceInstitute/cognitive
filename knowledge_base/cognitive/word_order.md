# Word Order

## Overview
Word order is the sequencing of words and phrases in a sentence. Languages differ in how fixed it is and in what it signals. In English the order of subject, verb and object largely identifies who did what to whom, as in "the dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog". In languages with rich case marking, such as Latin or Russian, order is freer and is used more for emphasis or topic. Information structure, including given and new information, also shapes order.

## Typology and universals
- **Basic orders.** Languages are classified by the dominant order of subject (S), object (O) and verb (V). Subject-object-verb (as in Japanese, Turkish and Hindi) and subject-verb-object (as in English, Mandarin and Swahili) account for most languages. Verb-subject-object is the next most common, and orders that place the object before the subject are rare.
- **Correlations.** Greenberg (1963) noted patterns linking basic order to other properties; for example, languages with dominant verb-subject-object order are always prepositional. Dryer (1992) later tested such correlations in a much larger sample and argued that they are better explained by whether a category is a single word or a branching phrase than by a head-dependent distinction.
- **Flexibility.** Some languages allow most permutations, though one order is usually pragmatically neutral.

Order also differs within the clause, for instance in the position of adjectives relative to nouns and of auxiliaries relative to verbs (see [[verb_phrases]], [[noun_phrases]]), and by operations such as [[wh_movement]].

## Processing and Active Inference
Hawkins (2004) argued that grammars tend to arrange constituents so that the structure of a phrase can be recognised quickly, an efficiency account of ordering preferences. Gibson and colleagues (2013) proposed a noisy-channel account: because communication is error-prone, languages with fixed order and those with case marking are both ways of making role assignment robust to noise, and the preferred order of a language depends on how reliably it keeps subject and object distinct. This is a Bayesian view of comprehension in which the listener infers the intended message from a noisy signal, and it belongs with predictive processing accounts of language. It is compatible with, but narrower than, the full Active Inference framework.
