# Self

## Overview
The self is what a person refers to when saying "I": the subject of experience and action, and also the object of self-knowledge. The term covers several related ideas that philosophy and psychology often separate. William James (1890) distinguished the self as knower from the self as known, which he subdivided into material, social and spiritual aspects. Whether there is any single thing that deserves the name is itself contested.

## Main positions
- **Substance views.** The self is a persisting entity, such as a soul or an enduring subject, distinct from its changing states.
- **Bundle views.** Associated with Hume, who reported finding only particular perceptions on introspection and no underlying self. On this view the self is a collection of experiences related by memory and causal connection. Buddhist traditions also hold a no-self doctrine, in a different theoretical setting.
- **Narrative views.** The self is constructed through the stories a person tells about their life, which gives coherence over time.
- **Minimal self.** Gallagher (2000) distinguishes a minimal, immediate sense of being the subject of experience and agent of action, which is present from early life, from an extended narrative self that depends on memory, language and social interaction. Components of the minimal self include the sense of ownership of a body and the sense of agency over actions.
- **Self-model theory.** Metzinger (2003) argues that no self exists as an entity; instead the brain generates a transparent model of the organism that is not recognised as a model, so experience seems to belong to a self.

## Empirical work
Studies of body ownership, as in the rubber-hand illusion, show that the felt boundary of the body can be altered by correlated sensory signals. Depersonalisation and delusions of control show that components of the sense of self can be disrupted separately.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an active research programme. Several authors propose that the self is a generative model of the causes of an organism's own interoceptive, proprioceptive and exteroceptive signals. Seth (2013) develops an account of the embodied self based on interoceptive inference, and Hohwy has argued that the brain is "self-evidencing" because it acts to maintain its model's evidence. These accounts remain largely theoretical, and the assumption that a unified self-model exists is contested.
