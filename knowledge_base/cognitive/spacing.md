# Spacing

## Overview
Spacing, or distributed practice, means spreading study or practice across time instead of concentrating it in one session. The spacing effect is the finding that material studied in separated sessions is remembered better, over a delay, than the same amount of study massed together. It is one of the oldest and most reliable findings in the psychology of learning.

## History and findings
Ebbinghaus (1885), in his studies of memorising nonsense syllables, found that distributing repetitions across days reduced the effort needed to relearn a list. The effect has since been shown across materials, ages and species, from word lists and facts to motor skills. A quantitative synthesis of hundreds of comparisons concluded that longer gaps generally benefited later recall, with the best gap depending on the retention interval (Cepeda et al., 2006). A later large study found that the optimal gap is a fraction of the delay until the test, and that this fraction declines as the delay increases (Cepeda et al., 2008). The benefit appears for retention after the delay, and it may not appear in immediate tests, and learners often judge massed practice to be more effective, which leads to under-use of spacing.

## Proposed mechanisms
- **Encoding variability.** Spaced presentations occur in different contexts, so the item is linked to more retrieval cues.
- **Deficient processing.** Massed repetitions receive less attention because the item is still active in mind.
- **Study-phase retrieval.** At a later repetition, learners must retrieve the earlier occurrence, which strengthens the trace.
- **Consolidation.** Time between sessions, including sleep, gives opportunity for stabilisation (see [[memory_consolidation]]).

Models usually combine several of these, and no single mechanism accounts for all results. The related effect of lag, the interval between repetitions, is the basis for scheduling in [[spaced_repetition]] systems.

## Relation to Active Inference
Spacing is a property of human memory, not of Active Inference, which has no standard account of it. It can be loosely interpreted as a case in which sampling an association at varied times yields more informative updates of a learned model than repeated updates from the same state, but this is speculative.
