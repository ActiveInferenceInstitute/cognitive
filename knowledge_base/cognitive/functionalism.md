# Functionalism

## Overview
Functionalism holds that a mental state is individuated by what it does: its typical causes, its effects on behaviour and its relations to other mental states, not by what it is made of. Pain, on this view, is whatever state is caused by tissue damage, produces aversion and avoidance, and interacts in the right way with beliefs and desires. The position grew out of dissatisfaction with behaviourism, which ignored internal states, and with type-identity theories, which tied each mental kind to a specific kind of brain state.

## Main variants
- **Machine-state functionalism.** Putnam (1967) proposed that mental states are computational states of a probabilistic automaton, and argued from multiple realizability: the same mental kind could be realised in brains, in other animals or in artificial systems.
- **Causal-role functionalism.** Lewis and Armstrong defined mental terms by the causal roles they occupy in a folk-psychological theory.
- **Computational-representational functionalism.** Fodor and others treated cognition as rule-governed operations over mental representations, which became the working assumption of much classical cognitive science.

## Objections
Block (1978) argued that functionalism may be too liberal, because a system such as a population of people wired to simulate a brain would share the functional organisation without plausibly having experience. Inverted-spectrum and absent-qualia cases press the same point: functional role seems not to fix phenomenal character (see [[qualia]]). Searle (1980) targeted the stronger claim that running the right program suffices for understanding. Functionalists reply that the intuitions are unreliable, that finer-grained functional description changes the verdict, or that qualia are themselves functionally specified.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference is stated in functional and informational terms: an agent is described by a generative model, a variational density and a Markov blanket separating internal from external states, and the same mathematics can in principle describe different physical systems. This gives the framework a functionalist flavour, and it is often used to model cognition without reference to biophysical detail. The link is a matter of style, not a derivation. Many proponents of the framework stress embodiment, metabolism and self-organisation, which pull toward substrate-sensitive views of the mind (see [[enactivism]], [[autopoiesis]]), so the framework does not settle the debate.
