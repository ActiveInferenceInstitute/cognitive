# Deep Alexia

## Overview
Deep alexia, more often called deep dyslexia in the neuropsychological literature, is an acquired reading disorder in which a person reading aloud produces semantic errors, replacing a word with one related in meaning, for example reading "ill" as "sick". The pattern was described as a syndrome by Marshall and Newcombe (1973) and collected in a volume edited by Coltheart, Patterson and Marshall (1980).

## Core features
- **Semantic errors** are the defining sign, and they co-occur with visual errors (reading a word as one that looks similar) and morphological errors (altering word endings).
- **Imageability effect.** Concrete, imageable words such as "table" are read better than abstract words such as "idea".
- **Word class effect.** Nouns are read better than verbs, and function words such as prepositions are read especially poorly.
- **Nonword reading.** Pronouncing unfamiliar letter strings is severely impaired or absent, which indicates that grapheme-to-phoneme conversion is unavailable.

The disorder is typically seen after large left-hemisphere lesions, often involving perisylvian language areas, and it usually accompanies a non-fluent aphasia (see [[aphasia]]). Patients differ in details, so the syndrome is a cluster of symptoms rather than a single deficit.

## Explanations
Dual-route models of reading assume a lexical-semantic route and a phonological route. In deep alexia the phonological route is lost and the damaged semantic route yields near-miss responses. Two main hypotheses have been proposed. One holds that the reading is carried out by the intact right hemisphere, which is thought to support concrete words but not phonological decoding. The other holds that a single damaged system produces the pattern, and connectionist simulations by Plaut and Shallice (1993) reproduced semantic errors, visual errors and imageability effects after damage to a network linking orthography, semantics and phonology.

## Relation to Active Inference
No Active Inference model of deep alexia has been established. A loose predictive processing reading treats reading as inference in a hierarchy from letters to meaning and sound, in which lesions to one route leave the other to generate predictions that are close in meaning but incorrect in form. This is an analogy, not a tested account.
