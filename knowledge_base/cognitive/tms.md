# Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation

## Overview
Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a non-invasive technique for stimulating the cortex. A coil placed on the scalp carries a brief, strong current that generates a rapidly changing magnetic field. By electromagnetic induction this field produces an electric field in the underlying tissue, which can depolarise neurons. Barker, Jalinous and Freeston (1985) first demonstrated stimulation of human motor cortex in this way, and the method has since been used widely in research and clinical practice (Hallett, 2000).

## Protocols
- **Single pulses** applied to motor cortex produce muscle twitches measured as motor evoked potentials, which are used to assess cortical excitability and corticospinal conduction. Applied to visual cortex, a pulse may produce a brief flash of light known as a phosphene.
- **Paired pulses** probe intracortical inhibition and facilitation.
- **Repetitive TMS** delivers trains of pulses. Depending on frequency and pattern, including theta-burst stimulation, it can change excitability beyond the stimulation period, an effect thought to involve synaptic plasticity.

## Uses and limits
In cognitive neuroscience, TMS is valued because it is interventional. Disrupting a region transiently, sometimes called a virtual lesion, tests whether the region is necessary for a task, which correlational imaging cannot show. It is also combined with EEG to measure how perturbations propagate through networks, and the complexity of the evoked response has been used as an index of the level of consciousness (Casali et al., 2013). Repetitive TMS is an approved treatment for some patients with major depression who have not responded to medication. Spatial resolution is limited to roughly a centimetre, deep structures are hard to reach, the sound and scalp sensation of the pulse create confounds that require control conditions, and the main safety concern is a small risk of seizure, managed by screening and published guidelines.

## Relation to Active Inference
TMS has no intrinsic connection to Active Inference. It is relevant as a tool for causal tests of theories, for example whether disrupting a proposed node of a predictive hierarchy alters behaviour in the way the model predicts. TMS-EEG perturbation work also bears on theories of consciousness, including those within the predictive-processing family, but these tests are theory-dependent and the interpretation of results remains debated.
