# Collective Efficacy

## Overview
Collective efficacy is a group's shared belief that it can organise and execute the courses of action required to reach given levels of attainment. Bandura (1997) developed it as the group-level extension of self-efficacy, the belief of an individual in his or her own capability. It has been studied in sports teams, work teams, schools and military units. A separate use of the same phrase in sociology, introduced by Sampson, Raudenbush and Earls (1997), concerns social cohesion among neighbours combined with willingness to intervene for the common good, and it is not the sense treated here.

## Measurement and sources
Two approaches are common. One aggregates members' judgments of their own capability within the group. The other asks members to judge what the group as a whole can do, which Bandura argued better reflects the emergent, interdependent nature of group functioning. Proposed sources parallel those of self-efficacy: mastery experiences of group success, vicarious experience from comparable groups, persuasion and encouragement, and affective states. Perceived efficacy is thought to shape the goals a group sets, the effort invested and persistence after setbacks (Bandura, 2000).

## Findings
Meta-analyses report positive associations between collective efficacy and group performance. Stajkovic, Lee and Nyberg (2009) found a positive relationship, with the strength varying with factors such as task interdependence and the way performance was measured. The association is bidirectional, since success raises efficacy beliefs and efficacy beliefs support later performance, so correlational data alone do not establish a causal direction.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an analogy, not a derivation. Efficacy beliefs resemble beliefs about the controllability of outcomes, which in Active Inference enter through the precision assigned to policies (see [[agency]]). A group-level analogue would be shared expectations that joint policies will realise preferred outcomes. Work on multi-agent Active Inference and shared generative models is active, but no formal account of collective efficacy as studied in social psychology is established.
