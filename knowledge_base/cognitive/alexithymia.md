# Alexithymia

## Overview
Alexithymia, from Greek roots meaning "no words for emotion", is a personality trait or dimension, not a diagnosis in the DSM-5. The term was introduced by Peter Sifneos in the early 1970s on the basis of clinical observation of psychosomatic patients. People with high levels have difficulty identifying their own feelings and distinguishing them from bodily sensations, difficulty describing feelings to others, and a preference for external events over inner experience, sometimes called externally oriented thinking. Imagination and fantasy are often reduced. Alexithymia lies on a continuum in the general population, and high levels are reported more often in several clinical groups.

## Assessment
The most widely used measure is the 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20), a self-report questionnaire with three factors: difficulty identifying feelings, difficulty describing feelings, and externally oriented thinking. Observer-rated and performance-based measures exist because people with the trait may have limited insight into it.

## Associations and mechanisms
Higher alexithymia is associated with autism, depression, anxiety, eating disorders and some chronic pain and psychosomatic conditions, and it is thought to be a transdiagnostic risk factor, not a feature of one disorder. The alexithymia hypothesis proposes that some of the emotion-recognition difficulties reported in autism are linked to co-occurring alexithymia rather than to autism itself; the evidence is still debated. Proposed mechanisms include reduced awareness of bodily signals (see [[interoception]]), weak links between emotional experience and language, and altered activity in the insula and anterior cingulate, regions that support interoceptive awareness and emotional awareness. Findings on neural correlates and on interoceptive accuracy are mixed.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is a developing research proposal. Interoceptive inference views emotion as the brain's inference about the causes of internal bodily signals, so that experienced feelings depend on precise predictions about the body (see [[interoception]]). On this view alexithymia could arise from imprecise interoceptive predictions or reduced precision of interoceptive signals, which would leave states poorly differentiated. Seth and others have developed the framework, and some empirical studies support related predictions, but the specific link to alexithymia remains hypothetical.
