# Load Theory

## Overview
Load theory is a theory of selective attention proposed by Nilli Lavie that addresses the long-running early versus late selection debate. It holds that whether irrelevant stimuli are processed depends on the demands of the current task. The name should not be confused with cognitive load theory in educational psychology, which concerns instructional design and working memory in learning.

## Perceptual load
Lavie and Tsal (1994) and Lavie (1995) proposed that perception has limited capacity but proceeds automatically until that capacity is exhausted. Perceptual load is raised by increasing the number of relevant items or by requiring more demanding discriminations of them. Under low load, spare capacity spills over to irrelevant distractors, which are processed and can interfere with the task. Under high load the capacity is used up by the task, so distractors are not perceived and cause little interference. Selection can therefore look late under low load and early under high load, which reconciles the two earlier positions.

In a typical experiment, participants search a ring of letters for a target while an irrelevant distractor letter appears outside the ring. Interference from an incongruent distractor is smaller when the ring contains many non-target letters than when it contains only one. Detection of unexpected stimuli is also reduced under high load, which has been linked to inattentional blindness (see [[inattentional_blindness]]).

## Cognitive load and control
Lavie and colleagues added that executive control also matters. Working memory or other cognitive-control demands unrelated to perception reduce the ability to keep irrelevant distractors from guiding behaviour, so interference increases under high cognitive load (see [[working_memory]] and [[cognitive_control]]). The two kinds of load therefore have opposite effects on distractor interference. Criticisms concern how load should be defined independently of the effect it is meant to explain, and some results have not replicated across task types.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is loose and interpretive. The theory describes limited capacity, whereas Active Inference describes attention as the allocation of precision among competing sources of prediction error, with no fixed capacity as such. A demanding task could be modelled as assigning high precision to task-relevant channels, leaving distractors weakly weighted, but no model derived from the framework has been fitted to the load effects, and the correspondence remains an analogy.
