# Thought Suppression

## Overview
Thought suppression is the deliberate attempt to stop thinking about a particular thought, image or memory. It is a form of mental control that depends on executive resources and is distinct from suppressing the outward expression of emotion (see [[expressive_suppression]]). The experimental literature asks whether suppression works and what it costs.

## Key findings
- **Rebound.** In a well-known experiment, participants were asked not to think of a white bear while reporting their thoughts, and later were told to think of it freely. Those who had first suppressed tended to mention the bear more often in the later phase than those who had not (Wegner, Schneider, Carter and White, 1987). Subsequent studies and a meta-analysis found that such post-suppression increases occur but vary in size across studies and conditions (Abramowitz, Tolin and Street, 2001).
- **Ironic process theory.** Wegner (1994) proposed two processes: an effortful operating process that searches for distracting content, and a more automatic monitoring process that scans for signs of failure, which requires the target thought to be kept accessible. When cognitive load or stress limits the operating process, the monitoring process can increase the frequency of intrusions.
- **Memory suppression.** In the think/no-think paradigm, participants repeatedly avoid recalling a cue's associate. Later recall of the suppressed items is reduced relative to baseline (Anderson and Green, 2001), and later imaging work suggests that prefrontal regions down-regulate hippocampal activity.

Suppression is also studied in relation to intrusive thoughts. Cognitive models of obsessional symptoms and of post-traumatic stress discuss whether habitual suppression maintains such thoughts. These models are research hypotheses, and the findings do not amount to guidance for any individual.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no standard Active Inference formulation of thought suppression. A possible reading treats the unwanted thought as a high-probability belief that the agent tries to withhold by lowering its precision or by redirecting attention, with the monitoring process keeping a representation of the target active. The ironic effect would then follow from the prior needed to detect the target. This is speculative, and the closest established links are to [[inhibitory_control]] and to attentional control.
