# Primary Auditory Cortex

## Overview
The primary auditory cortex, often called A1, is the first cortical region to receive auditory information. In humans it lies on the superior surface of the temporal lobe, in and around Heschl's gyrus (the transverse temporal gyrus), and corresponds approximately to Brodmann area 41. Its main thalamic input comes from the ventral division of the medial geniculate nucleus. It forms part of the auditory core, which is surrounded by belt and parabelt regions that process more complex features (Kaas and Hackett, 2000). The wider region is described in [[auditory_cortex]].

## Organisation and response properties
A defining feature of A1 is tonotopy: neurons are arranged according to the sound frequency to which they respond best, so that frequency changes systematically along one axis of the cortical surface. This reflects the frequency map of the cochlea that is carried through the brainstem and thalamus. Neurons in A1 are tuned to frequency, and they also show sensitivity to sound level, to the timing of onsets and to modulations in amplitude and frequency. Responses become more selective and more dependent on context in the belt regions, and neurons increasingly respond to complex sounds and, in specialised regions, to vocalisations and speech.

## Function in speech and music
A1 is not specialised for speech as such. Models of speech perception propose that early stages extract acoustic features in the primary and surrounding auditory cortex, followed by phonological and lexical processing along ventral and dorsal streams that extend into the temporal and frontal lobes (Hickok and Poeppel, 2007; see [[speech_perception]] and [[wernickes_area]]). Damage to bilateral auditory cortex can cause cortical deafness or auditory agnosia, while damage to one side generally produces milder deficits, since each ear projects to both hemispheres.

## Relation to Active Inference
The relation is an active research programme at the level of the auditory hierarchy, not a property of A1 alone. Models of auditory perception treat the cortex as a hierarchy that predicts sound sequences over increasing timescales (Kiebel, Daunizeau and Friston, 2008). The mismatch negativity, an evoked response to a deviant sound within a regular series, is interpreted by many authors as a signature of prediction error generated in the auditory cortex and its hierarchy, though adaptation-based explanations are also discussed (Garrido et al., 2009).
