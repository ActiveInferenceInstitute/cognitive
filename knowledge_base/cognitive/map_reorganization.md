# Map Reorganization

## Overview
Sensory cortex contains topographic maps, such as the representation of the body surface in somatosensory cortex. Map reorganization refers to changes in the spatial extent or boundaries of these representations after loss of input, peripheral injury, or intensive use. It was long thought that adult maps are fixed, and demonstrations of change became central evidence for adult cortical plasticity (see [[neural_plasticity]]).

## Classic evidence
In adult owl and squirrel monkeys, transecting the median nerve deprived the corresponding cortical territory of its input, and within months it was occupied by expanded representations of neighbouring skin (Merzenich et al., 1983). Pons et al. (1991) reported much larger changes in macaques after long-term deafferentation of a forelimb, with the deprived hand and arm cortex responding to the face. In humans, Ramachandran and colleagues reported that touching the face of some amputees evoked referred sensations in the missing hand, interpreted as a perceptual correlate of such remapping (Ramachandran et al., 1992). Proposed mechanisms include unmasking of existing but weak horizontal connections, changes in inhibition, and Hebbian strengthening of synapses (see [[hebbian_learning]]), with possible sprouting over longer periods.

## A contested interpretation
A more recent critique argues that the evidence for large-scale remapping in adult humans is weaker than commonly stated. Imaging studies of amputees find that the missing hand's representation can persist in a recognisable form, and lip or face activity in the former hand area may reflect altered use and enhanced reliance on the remaining body parts, not reassigned territory. Makin and Krakauer (2023) argue that the "remapping" account has been over-extended, especially for phantom limb pain, while acknowledging that plasticity of other kinds clearly occurs. Defenders point to the animal findings, in which anatomical and physiological changes are directly measured. The matter is not resolved and depends on the species, the lesion and the method.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is weak and interpretive. Changes in map boundaries could be read as re-estimation of the precision and mapping parameters of a generative model when sensory input changes. No specific Active Inference model of cortical remapping is established.
