# Recognition Memory

## Overview
Recognition memory is the capacity to judge whether a stimulus, such as a word, face or scene, has been encountered before. It is usually tested by showing studied items mixed with new ones and asking for old/new judgements, or by asking which of several alternatives was studied. It is a core part of [[episodic_memory]], although it can also reflect other memory systems.

## Familiarity and recollection
A leading idea is that recognition draws on two kinds of information. [[recollection]] is retrieval of qualitative details about the earlier episode, such as where or when the item appeared. [[familiarity]] is a sense that the item has been encountered, without retrieval of such details (Mandler, 1980). Dual-process theories hold that the two contribute independently, and Yonelinas (2002) reviewed evidence from receiver operating characteristics, from patients and from neuroimaging. Single-process theories, based on [[signal_detection]], hold that a single memory-strength dimension, together with decision criteria, can explain the same data, with confidence reflecting strength (Wixted, 2007). The debate is unresolved, and current models often combine elements of both views.

## Measurement
Performance is analysed with hit and false-alarm rates, which separate sensitivity from response bias through measures such as $d'$. Confidence ratings give a receiver operating characteristic curve whose shape is used to test models. The remember/know procedure asks participants to report whether recognition is accompanied by recollection of details or only by familiarity.

## Neural basis
Evidence from patients, lesion studies in animals and imaging suggests that the hippocampus is especially important for recollection, while regions of the medial temporal lobe such as the perirhinal cortex may support familiarity ([[hippocampus]]). Event-related potentials show an earlier frontal old/new effect often related to familiarity and a later parietal effect often related to recollection. The extent to which these processes are anatomically separate is contested, and some studies favour accounts in which the hippocampus contributes to both.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is a loose analogy. A previously encountered item is expected under the learned generative model, so it produces a smaller prediction error than a novel one, which gives a reading of familiarity as low surprise or high processing fluency. Recollection resembles the retrieval of a specific episode by completion of a stored pattern. These readings are consistent with the framework but they are not predictions that distinguish between the theories above.
