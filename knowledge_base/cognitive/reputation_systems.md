# Reputation Systems

## Overview
A reputation system is any mechanism that records information about an individual's past behaviour and makes it available to others deciding whether to interact or cooperate with that individual. In human societies this ranges from gossip and social memory to formal credit records and online ratings. By linking present conduct to future treatment by third parties, reputation can sustain cooperation among people who may never meet again, a pathway known as indirect reciprocity (see [[indirect_reciprocity]]).

## Evolutionary and behavioural accounts
Nowak and Sigmund (1998) showed in a model that cooperation can evolve when individuals help those with a good image score, a record of previous helping. Later work found that simple image scoring can be exploited, because refusing to help a defector then lowers one's own score, and compared it with "standing" and other norms that treat justified refusal differently; the question of which assessment rules sustain cooperation is a developed area of theory (Leimar & Hammerstein, 2001). Behaviourally, people give more when their choices are observable. Cues of being watched, including images of eyes, increased contributions in a field study (Bateson et al., 2006). The effect of such cues is contested: meta-analyses covering later studies found little evidence that artificial surveillance cues raise generosity overall (Northover et al., 2017), so the original finding is best treated as uncertain in size and generality.

## Online platforms
Marketplaces and review sites use aggregated ratings as reputation. Resnick et al. (2000) discuss design problems of such systems, including the reluctance to leave feedback, the cost of creating new identities, which lets a bad actor shed a poor record, and the bias of ratings toward positive scores. Manipulation through fake reviews and retaliation are continuing problems.

## Relation to Active Inference
No established link exists. Reputation could be described as an agent's beliefs about the policies of others, learned from observation and used in planning, and this fits the framework's treatment of social inference, but it remains a loose analogy.
