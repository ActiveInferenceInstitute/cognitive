# Imagination

## Overview
Imagination is the capacity to represent things that are not present to the senses: past events, possible futures, hypothetical situations, fictions and unreal objects. Philosophers and psychologists use the word for several overlapping abilities, including sensory or visual imagining ([[mental_imagery]]), supposing or imagining that something is the case, pretence, and creative recombination of ideas. Kind (2016) surveys these distinctions and the debates they raise.

## Philosophical treatments
Classical philosophers such as Hume gave imagination a role in connecting ideas, and Kant assigned the productive imagination a role in synthesising experience. Contemporary philosophy asks how imagining differs from perceiving and believing, whether imagination is a distinct kind of mental state, and how it can be a guide to possibility and to knowledge. Currie and Ravenscroft (2002) argue that imagination involves states that recreate other mental states, such as beliefs and desires, in an offline mode. Related puzzles concern why people respond emotionally to fiction and why some imaginings, particularly morally repugnant ones, resist adoption.

## Cognitive neuroscience
Remembering the past and imagining the future draw on a common set of regions that includes the hippocampus, medial prefrontal and posterior cortex, and parietal areas. Patients with hippocampal damage who cannot recall past events also show impoverished imagining of new scenes. Hassabis and Maguire (2007) proposed a process of scene construction, in which the hippocampus helps assemble coherent scenes from stored elements and so supports both episodic recall and imagination.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is close in structure. A generative model is by construction something that can be run without sensory input, so that predicted observations are produced from hypothesised causes. Planning in Active Inference evaluates the expected outcomes of policies before acting, which is a form of counterfactual simulation, and mental imagery can be read as top-down generation with reduced sensory constraint. These are standard readings within the framework, supported by simulations. They do not amount to a worked-out theory of human imagination, and empirical work on how imagined and perceived content differ continues.
