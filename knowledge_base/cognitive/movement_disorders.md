# Movement Disorders

## Overview
Movement disorders are neurological conditions in which the production or control of movement is abnormal, in the absence of weakness or sensory loss as the primary cause. They are commonly divided into hypokinetic disorders, characterised by reduced or slowed movement, and hyperkinetic disorders, characterised by excessive or unwanted movement. This page is descriptive and does not give diagnostic or treatment advice.

## Main forms
- **Parkinsonism.** Slowness of movement ([[bradykinesia]]), rigidity and rest [[tremor]], most often due to degeneration of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra, as in [[parkinsons]].
- **Chorea.** Brief, irregular, flowing involuntary movements, the hallmark of Huntington's disease.
- **Dystonia.** Sustained or intermittent muscle contractions producing twisting movements and abnormal postures.
- **Tremor syndromes.** Rhythmic oscillation of a body part, including essential tremor.
- **Tics.** Sudden, repetitive movements or vocalisations, as in Tourette syndrome.
- **Cerebellar disorders.** Incoordination, dysmetria and intention tremor, as in [[cerebellar_ataxia]] (see also [[dysmetria]]).

## Circuit basis
Many of these disorders are attributed to dysfunction of the [[basal_ganglia]], a set of subcortical nuclei that modulate motor and cognitive circuits through the thalamus. A classical model proposes a direct pathway that facilitates movement and an indirect pathway that suppresses competing movement, both modulated by dopamine. Reduced dopaminergic drive is linked to hypokinesia and its excess or imbalance to hyperkinesia (see [[dopamine_system]]). The classical model is a simplification, and newer work stresses more complex, overlapping circuit interactions. The cerebellum contributes to coordination and error correction (see [[cerebellum]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference treats dopamine as encoding the precision of beliefs about policies, which links dopaminergic loss to difficulty in selecting and initiating action (Friston and colleagues, 2012). Related work proposes that parkinsonian slowness reflects abnormally flat or imprecise beliefs about the consequences of movement. These are computational hypotheses used in simulations, not clinical conclusions, and they complement established pharmacological and surgical understanding.
