# Neuroendocrine Measures

## Overview
The neuroendocrine system links the nervous system to hormonal signalling. Neurosecretory cells in the hypothalamus control the pituitary gland, which regulates peripheral glands, forming axes such as the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis for stress, and the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis for reproduction. In cognitive and behavioural research, neuroendocrine measures are hormone concentrations used as indicators of physiological state, stress, arousal or social condition. They are used alongside behavioural, autonomic (see [[skin_conductance]]) and imaging measures.

## Common measures
- **Cortisol.** The main glucocorticoid in humans, measured in saliva, blood, urine or hair, and used as a marker of HPA activity (see [[cortisol]]). Typical indices are the diurnal profile, the awakening response and reactivity to an acute stressor.
- **Oxytocin and vasopressin.** Peptides studied in relation to social behaviour and bonding (see [[oxytocin]] and [[vasopressin]]), where measurement is more contested.
- **Testosterone and other steroids.** Examined in relation to status, motivation and risk taking (see [[testosterone]]).
- **Catecholamines and related markers.** Adrenaline and noradrenaline, and salivary alpha-amylase as a proxy for sympathetic activity.

## Links to executive function
Stress hormones act on prefrontal circuits, which are rich in receptors for glucocorticoids and catecholamines. Moderate arousal is thought to support working memory and flexible control, whereas strong or prolonged stress is linked to impairment of prefrontal function (Arnsten, 2009). Reviews describe effects of stress and its hormones on human memory and executive tasks that depend on timing, dose and the type of task (Lupien and colleagues, 2007; see [[executive_functions]] and [[stress_response]]).

## Methodological cautions
Hormone levels vary with time of day, sex, age, medication, menstrual phase, food, exercise and sampling method. Peripheral concentrations do not map simply onto brain activity, and assays differ in specificity. Single measurements are rarely informative, and small samples with many hormone comparisons raise the risk of false positives.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference accounts of homeostatic and allostatic regulation treat bodily states as hidden states that are inferred and regulated through autonomic and endocrine action (Pezzulo, Rigoli and Friston, 2015; see [[allostatic_regulation]]). In this view, neuroendocrine signals are part of the effector machinery for control of the internal milieu. The proposal is theoretical and does not specify how particular hormone assays map onto model quantities.
