# Gamma Phase

## Overview
Gamma oscillations are rhythmic fluctuations of neural activity in the local field potential, usually in a band of roughly 30 to 80 Hz, though band definitions vary across studies. Gamma phase is the position within one cycle of this rhythm. Because excitability varies across the cycle, the phase at which a neuron fires, or at which input arrives, can affect its influence and can carry information.

## Mechanism
Gamma rhythms in cortex are generated by interactions between excitatory pyramidal cells and fast-spiking inhibitory interneurons, typically parvalbumin-expressing basket cells, whose inhibition rhythmically gates firing (Buzsaki & Wang, 2012; see [[interneurons]]). Spiking is concentrated on the rising and peak phases of excitation, leaving windows of relative silence in each cycle. Gamma is strongest when stimuli engage a local population and shows stimulus- and attention-dependent changes in frequency and power.

## Functional proposals
- **Communication through coherence.** Fries (2005) proposed that groups of neurons in different areas communicate effectively when their gamma rhythms are phase-aligned, so that spikes from the sender arrive at a time of high excitability in the receiver.
- **Phase coding.** Information may be carried by spike timing relative to the oscillation, as in the theta-gamma code, where several items are represented in successive gamma cycles within one theta cycle (Lisman & Jensen, 2013; see [[phase_coding]]).
- **Cross-frequency coupling.** The amplitude of gamma is often modulated by the phase of slower rhythms.

Whether gamma is a causal carrier of information or an emergent by-product of excitatory-inhibitory interaction remains debated, and measures of phase are affected by the choice of filter and reference.

## Role in Active Inference
Bastos and colleagues (2012) associated gamma rhythms with forward prediction errors from superficial layers and slower beta rhythms with backward predictions from deep layers. Recordings in macaque visual cortex found that feedforward influences are stronger in gamma and feedback influences in alpha and beta (Bastos et al., 2015). This is an active research programme with partial support, and the assignment of frequencies to message types is not accepted universally.
