# Word Formation

## Overview
Word formation is the area of morphology that studies how new lexical items are created from existing material. It is usually distinguished from inflection, which produces the forms of one word, such as "walks" and "walked", whereas word formation produces new words, such as "walker" or "walkway". The basic building blocks are morphemes: roots, which carry core meaning, and affixes, which are bound forms attached to them. The boundary between the two areas is debated, and not every framework recognises it.

## Processes
- **Derivation.** Affixes create new words, often changing category or meaning: "happy" to "unhappy" and "happiness". Suffixes often determine the category of the result, so that "-ness" yields nouns from adjectives, which is the observation behind the right-hand head rule.
- **Compounding.** Two or more roots combine, as in "blackboard" or "toothbrush". Compounds are often headed by their final element, a "toothbrush" being a kind of brush, but not always.
- **Conversion.** A word changes category without any affix, as in the verb "to email" from the noun.
- **Shortening.** Clipping ("info" from "information"), blending ("smog" from "smoke" and "fog") and acronyms ("laser") create new forms from parts of existing ones.
- **Back-formation.** A shorter word is derived from a longer one that looks as if it were complex, as "edit" from "editor".
- **Reduplication.** Repeating part or all of a word, used productively in many languages.

Affixes differ in productivity. "-ness" can be attached to almost any adjective, whereas "-th", as in "warmth", is no longer available for new words. Productivity is related to how many distinct words an affix has created, and it is studied with corpus methods (Plag, 2003).

## Processing and Active Inference
Priming studies suggest that complex words are decomposed into morphemes in visual word recognition, at least when they are transparent, as in the finding that prefixed words are accessed via their stems (Taft and Forster, 1975). Whether recognition proceeds by decomposition or by direct retrieval of whole words is debated, and many models allow both routes. For Active Inference the relevance is general: a compositional generative model for words allows novel combinations to be understood by combining known parts. This is an analogy to productivity, not a worked model of morphology.
