# Experimental Psychology

## Overview
Experimental psychology is the study of mental processes and behaviour through systematic, controlled manipulation and measurement. The term covers a methodological commitment more than a single topic, and it spans perception, attention, memory, learning, language and decision making. Its characteristic move is to vary a factor of interest, hold other factors constant, and observe the effect on a measured response.

## History
Laboratory psychology is conventionally dated to the work of Wilhelm Wundt at Leipzig in the late nineteenth century, building on psychophysics and on Ebbinghaus's quantitative studies of memory (Boring, 1950). Behaviourism later emphasised observable behaviour and operational definitions, a stance closely tied to [[operationalism]] (Stevens, 1935), and the cognitive revolution of the mid twentieth century reintroduced inference about internal representations from behavioural data such as reaction times and error patterns.

## Methods and design
- **Variables.** The experimenter manipulates independent variables and measures dependent variables, such as accuracy, response time, ratings or physiological signals.
- **Control and assignment.** Random assignment, counterbalancing and control conditions protect against confounds.
- **Designs.** Between-participant designs compare groups, while within-participant designs compare conditions in the same individuals and often gain statistical power.
- **Validity.** Internal validity concerns whether the observed effect is due to the manipulation, and external validity concerns generalisation across people, stimuli and settings (Shadish, Cook & Campbell, 2002).
- **Analysis.** Methods range from classical hypothesis tests to Bayesian model comparison and computational modelling.

## Reproducibility
A large-scale effort to replicate published psychological studies found that many effects were weaker or not recovered in replication (Open Science Collaboration, 2015). The results contributed to changes in practice, including preregistration, larger samples, open data and registered reports, though interpretations of the replication rate differ.

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference supplies computational models that are fitted to data from experimental paradigms, such as choice tasks and eye-movement tasks, so that parameters like precision or prior preferences can be estimated and compared across conditions or groups. This is an active research programme, but its value depends on the same standards of design and replication as other models, and it adds no exemption from them.
