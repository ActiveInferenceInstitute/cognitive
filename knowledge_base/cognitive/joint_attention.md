# Joint Attention

## Overview
Joint attention is the coordination of attention between two or more individuals toward a shared object or event, with mutual awareness that the focus is shared. It is distinct from merely looking at the same thing: each partner monitors the other's attention as well as the target. It is regarded as a developmental precursor of language and of [[theory_of_mind]].

## Mechanism
Two roles are usually distinguished. In *responding* to joint attention (RJA), a person follows another's gaze or pointing to the referent. In *initiating* joint attention (IJA), a person directs another's attention, for example by pointing or showing, often to share interest rather than to obtain something. Gaze following appears early in infancy and becomes reliable across the first year; around nine to twelve months infants show triadic interactions involving self, partner, and object, a transition Tomasello associates with the emergence of shared intentionality. Joint attention in infancy has been reported to predict later vocabulary development, though effect sizes and causal direction remain debated.

Neuroimaging with adults implicates the posterior superior temporal sulcus and [[inferior_parietal]] cortex (gaze and attention direction), [[medial_prefrontal]] cortex (communicative intent), and the ventral striatum (a reward response to shared attention in some studies). Emery's review describes gaze perception and following in primates as phylogenetically widespread, while full triadic sharing is argued to be human-specific. Reduced or atypical joint attention is commonly described in [[autism]], and is used in early developmental assessments; it should be read as a behavioural observation, not a causal explanation.

## Role in Active Inference
In Active Inference, joint attention can be described as the alignment of two agents' beliefs about a shared hidden state: each agent selects actions, such as gaze or pointing, that are expected to reduce uncertainty about the other's beliefs and the target. Friston and Frith's treatment of communication argues that agents who share a generative model can predict one another, producing synchrony and mutual disambiguation; joint attention is a basic case where the shared model is about "what we are attending to". Epistemic value drives the pointing and looking behaviour ([[epistemic_foraging]]). This is a theoretical account supported by simulation; it has not been tested against developmental data, and rival accounts based on social learning and shared intentionality cover the same ground.
