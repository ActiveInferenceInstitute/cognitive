# Metacognitive Therapy

## Overview
Metacognitive therapy (MCT) is a psychological treatment developed by Adrian Wells, based on the self-regulatory executive function (S-REF) model of emotional disorder proposed by Wells and Matthews (1994, 1996). The model holds that persistent emotional distress is maintained less by the content of negative thoughts than by the way a person responds to them, guided by metacognitive beliefs about thinking. This page describes the approach neutrally and gives no treatment advice.

## The model
- **Cognitive attentional syndrome (CAS).** A pattern of perseverative thinking such as worry and rumination, focused attention on threat, and coping behaviours that prevent disconfirmation of beliefs.
- **Metacognitive beliefs.** Positive beliefs, such as that worrying helps in preparing for danger, motivate use of the CAS, while negative beliefs, such as that worry is uncontrollable or harmful, create further distress.
- **Disorder-general mechanism.** The same processes are proposed to underlie several conditions, including generalised anxiety, depression and post-traumatic stress, so treatment is not organised mainly by diagnosis.

## Treatment components
Techniques include detached mindfulness, in which thoughts are observed without engaging in them (see [[meditation]]), the attention training technique, which practises flexible control of attention (see [[attention_training]]), and postponing worry and rumination. Therapy aims to modify metacognitive beliefs directly instead of challenging the validity of individual thoughts, which marks a difference from standard [[cognitive_therapy]].

## Evidence
Randomised trials and meta-analyses report benefits in anxiety and depression, and several comparisons with cognitive behavioural therapy have been made. The evidence base is smaller than for cognitive behavioural therapy, a number of trials come from groups associated with the approach, and its relative efficacy remains under evaluation.

## Relation to Active Inference
Computational psychiatry describes metacognition as inference about one's own cognitive states, including estimating the precision of beliefs, and these ideas offer a language for formalising beliefs about worry and control (see [[computational_psychiatry]]). No Active Inference model specific to MCT has been established, so the connection is conceptual.
