# Retinal Implants

## Overview
A retinal implant is a neuroprosthetic device intended to restore some visual function in people whose photoreceptors have degenerated, as in retinitis pigmentosa, while the inner retinal neurons and the optic nerve remain at least partly intact. Electrodes deliver patterned electrical stimulation to those surviving cells, which evokes localised spots of light called phosphenes.

## Design and operation
Devices differ mainly in where they are placed. Epiretinal implants sit on the inner retinal surface and stimulate retinal ganglion cells, usually driven by an external camera and image processor. Subretinal implants replace the lost photoreceptor layer and use the remaining retinal circuitry, some with on-chip light-sensitive elements. Other approaches target the optic nerve, the thalamus or the visual cortex. Clinical systems have had only tens to a few thousand stimulating electrodes, and the resulting vision is crude: users can typically detect light, locate high-contrast objects and sometimes identify large shapes or letters, but not recognise faces in normal conditions.

## Limits of the neural code
Electrical stimulation does not reproduce the natural retinal code. Natural vision depends on many distinct ganglion cell types with specific response properties, whereas an electrode activates cells of different types together, and may also stimulate passing axons, producing elongated or distorted phosphenes. Perception also depends on adaptation, training and on cortical reorganisation, so outcomes vary between users.

## Relation to Active Inference
Implant vision illustrates that percepts are interpretations of sparse, unusual input rather than direct readouts. In predictive-processing terms, a user must learn the mapping between stimulation patterns and likely causes in the world, which requires a generative model fitted to the new sensory channel. This is a conceptual framing; formal Active Inference studies of retinal implant users are not an established programme.
