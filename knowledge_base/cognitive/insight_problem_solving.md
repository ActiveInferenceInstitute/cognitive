# Insight Problem Solving

## Overview
Insight problem solving refers to cases in which a solution appears suddenly after a period of impasse, typically accompanied by an "Aha" experience and by a change in how the problem is understood. Classic examples include the nine-dot problem and the candle problem (see [[duncker_problem]]). It is contrasted with analytic solving, in which a solution is reached by a sequence of deliberate steps with a sense of progress.

## Gestalt origins and accounts
Köhler (1925) described apes achieving sudden solutions that seemed to reflect a reorganisation of the perceived situation, and Gestalt psychologists proposed that insight involves restructuring the problem representation. Modern accounts include:
- **Representational change**: constraints that are wrongly assumed, or a habitual way of chunking the problem, are relaxed or revised during impasse.
- **Progress monitoring**: solvers evaluate their rate of progress and shift approach when it is too slow.
- **Special-process versus ordinary-process views**: whether insight uses distinct mechanisms, or the same processes as analytic solving applied to a differently structured problem, is debated.

## Behavioural and neural findings
Metcalfe and Wiebe (1987) had participants rate how close they felt to a solution as they worked. For insight problems, ratings of "warmth" stayed low and then jumped just before the solution, whereas for analytic problems warmth grew gradually, which supported the claim that insight solutions arrive abruptly and with little forewarning. Kounios and Beeman (2014) reviewed neuroimaging and electrophysiological work reporting that insight solutions are associated with a burst of high-frequency activity over the right anterior temporal cortex shortly before the solution, preceded by changes in alpha activity over visual regions consistent with reduced visual input, and that a pre-problem resting state can bias solvers towards insight. The studies are small and the interpretation of these markers continues to be examined.

## Relation to Active Inference
Friston and colleagues (2017) proposed that insight corresponds to Bayesian model reduction: after sufficient evidence accumulates, redundant model parameters or structure are pruned, giving a simpler model that explains the data better, with a drop in complexity and a gain in evidence. The authors note that this has the hallmarks of an "aha" moment (see [[structure_learning]]). It is a formal proposal supported by simulations of abstract rule learning and has not been established as the mechanism in human insight.
