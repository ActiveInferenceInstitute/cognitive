# Representativeness Heuristic

## Overview
The representativeness heuristic is a strategy in which the probability that an object belongs to a category, or that an event comes from a process, is judged by how well it resembles the typical case. Kahneman and Tversky (1972) described it, and it is one of the three heuristics in their account of judgment under uncertainty. Resemblance is a useful cue, but it ignores other information that bears on probability, so it can produce systematic errors.

## Characteristic errors
- **Base-rate neglect.** Given a personality description of a person and asked whether that person is more likely to be an engineer or a lawyer, participants rely on how well the description fits each stereotype and give little weight to the proportions of the two professions in the population.
- **Conjunction fallacy.** In the well-known Linda problem, a description of a socially engaged woman leads many people to rate "bank teller and feminist" as more probable than "bank teller", although a conjunction cannot be more probable than either of its parts (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983).
- **Insensitivity to sample size.** Small samples are expected to resemble the population as closely as large ones.
- **Misconceptions of chance.** Random sequences are expected to look irregular, which underlies the gambler's fallacy.

## Interpretation
Critics, notably Gigerenzer, argue that some of these errors depend on how problems are worded, and that presenting information as natural frequencies reduces them. Others accept the findings but see the heuristic as an attribute-substitution process, in which an easy question about similarity replaces a harder question about probability.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is a Bayesian reading, not a derivation. Bayes' theorem combines a prior with a likelihood, and base-rate neglect can be described as underweighting the prior relative to the likelihood implied by similarity. In predictive processing terms, this resembles an imbalance in the relative precision assigned to prior beliefs and to incoming evidence. This description is a redescription of the behaviour, and it does not by itself explain why the imbalance occurs.
