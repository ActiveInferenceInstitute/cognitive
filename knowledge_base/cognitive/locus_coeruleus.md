# Locus Coeruleus

## Overview
The locus coeruleus (LC) is a compact cluster of neurons in the dorsal pons, near the floor of the fourth ventricle. Its neurons contain neuromelanin, which gives the nucleus its blue-black appearance, and they release norepinephrine. Despite its small size, the LC sends widely branching axons to almost the whole cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, cerebellum and spinal cord, and it is the main source of norepinephrine in the forebrain (see [[noradrenergic_system]]).

## Firing modes and function
LC neurons fire in two characteristic modes. In a phasic mode, brief bursts follow salient or task-relevant events, whereas in a tonic mode baseline firing is elevated, associated with distractibility and exploratory behaviour. Aston-Jones and Cohen (2005) proposed the adaptive gain theory: phasic activity increases the gain of neurons processing task-relevant information and favours exploitation of the current task, while higher tonic activity favours disengagement and exploration of alternatives. The LC is also active during stress, and its firing is low during slow-wave sleep. Pupil diameter has been used as an indirect, although imperfect, readout of LC activity in humans. Early degeneration of LC neurons is found in Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease, and the relevance of this to cognitive symptoms is an active area of study.

## Uncertainty and learning
Yu and Dayan (2005) proposed a Bayesian interpretation in which norepinephrine signals unexpected uncertainty, meaning that the context has changed in a way not predicted by the current model, in contrast with expected uncertainty, which they associated with acetylcholine. On this account a norepinephrine burst tells cortex to discount top-down expectations and to update the model quickly (see [[attention_mechanisms]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy supported by shared Bayesian ideas, not a derivation. Active Inference assigns neuromodulators the job of encoding precisions, the confidence assigned to beliefs, with acetylcholine tied to the precision of sensory evidence and dopamine to the precision of policies (see [[precision_mechanisms]] and [[dopamine_system]]). The role of norepinephrine is less settled; proposals tie it to volatility and to the balance between prior beliefs and incoming evidence, which is consistent with the adaptive gain and unexpected-uncertainty accounts above.
