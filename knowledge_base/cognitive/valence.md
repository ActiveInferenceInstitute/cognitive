# Valence

## Overview
In psychology, valence is the degree to which an affective state, stimulus or event is experienced as pleasant or unpleasant, attractive or aversive. Together with arousal, it is one of the two most widely used dimensions for describing affect. The term has a related technical use in some emotion and judgement research for the positive or negative tone of words and pictures, and an unrelated use in chemistry, where it refers to combining capacity.

## Dimensional models and debates
Russell's (1980) circumplex model places emotion categories on a circle defined by valence and arousal, so that, for example, excitement and calm are both pleasant but differ in arousal. Standardised stimulus sets for research typically collect ratings of valence and arousal on rating scales. Some authors treat valence as a single bipolar dimension. Others, notably Cacioppo and Berntson (1994), argue that positive and negative evaluative processes are partly separable, so that mixed feelings can occur, which bears on the study of [[ambivalence]]. Constructionist theories hold that valence is a core property of affect from which discrete emotions are built, while basic emotion theories treat discrete categories as primary. These theoretical disputes are unresolved.

Neural correlates are not localised to a single region. Meta-analyses implicate regions such as the orbitofrontal cortex, ventromedial prefrontal cortex, amygdala and insula in processing the value of stimuli, but the mapping to subjective valence is debated, and bodily signals processed through [[interoception]] are thought to contribute.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference has produced proposals that give valence a computational definition. Joffily and Coricelli (2013) suggested that emotional valence tracks the rate of change of variational free energy over time, so that decreasing free energy is felt as positive and increasing free energy as negative. Hesp and colleagues (2021) extended this line to deep temporal models, in which valence arises from the dynamics of hierarchical inference and has implications for mood. The two accounts differ in details. They are active theoretical proposals with simulation support, not settled findings, and the main empirical challenge is to show that self-reported valence follows the quantities they specify.
