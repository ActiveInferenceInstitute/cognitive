# Depersonalization

## Overview
Depersonalization is an experience of unreality or detachment concerning the self. People describe feeling like an outside observer of their own thoughts, actions or body, or as if emotions and sensations were muted or not their own. Reality testing remains intact: the person knows that the experience is a feeling and not a fact. It often occurs together with derealization, in which the surroundings seem unreal.

## Occurrence and classification
Brief episodes are common in the general population, often under severe stress, fatigue, sleep deprivation or intense anxiety, and can accompany drug effects or some neurological conditions. When episodes are persistent or recurrent and cause significant distress or impairment, and are not better explained by another disorder or by a substance, diagnostic manuals such as the DSM-5-TR classify them as depersonalization/derealization disorder, one of the dissociative disorders. Descriptions here are for information and not a guide to diagnosis or treatment.

## Proposed mechanisms
Research has examined altered activity in prefrontal and insular regions, reduced autonomic and emotional responsiveness, and attentional and body-representation changes. Reviews, such as Sierra and Berrios (1998), proposed a prefrontal-limbic account in which hyperactivity of prefrontal inhibitory regions suppresses emotional processing, though the evidence base is modest and heterogeneous.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive-processing accounts connect depersonalization to interoception. Seth, Suzuki and Critchley (2012) proposed that the sense of presence arises from successful prediction of interoceptive signals and that its loss reflects a failure to match them, tied to altered precision or weak interoceptive predictions. This is an active research programme, with some supporting findings on interoceptive accuracy and heartbeat-evoked responses, but the evidence is limited and the hypothesis is not established.
