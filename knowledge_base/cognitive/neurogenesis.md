# Neurogenesis

## Overview
Neurogenesis is the process by which neural stem and progenitor cells divide and differentiate into new neurons. Most neurons in the mammalian brain are produced during embryonic and early postnatal development, when proliferating cells in the ventricular zones generate neurons that migrate to their final positions and form connections. For much of the twentieth century it was held that the adult mammalian brain produced no new neurons. This view was revised after work showing that some regions retain the capacity.

## Adult neurogenesis
Altman and Das (1965) reported evidence of newly born cells in the hippocampal dentate gyrus of postnatal rats. Subsequent studies established that two regions of the adult rodent brain produce neurons throughout life: the subgranular zone of the dentate gyrus, whose new granule cells integrate into hippocampal circuits, and the subventricular zone, whose progeny migrate to the olfactory bulb. In rodents the rate of hippocampal neurogenesis is increased by physical exercise and enriched environments and decreased by chronic stress and ageing, and experimental manipulations have implicated new neurons in discriminating between similar memories, a function called pattern separation.

Whether this occurs in adult humans has been disputed. Eriksson and colleagues (1998) found newly generated neurons in the dentate gyrus of adult patients who had received a marker of cell division, while Sorrells and colleagues (2018) reported that neurogenesis in the human hippocampus declines sharply in childhood and is undetectable in adults. Other studies of human tissue have reached different conclusions, and the differences are attributed to tissue preservation, markers and methods. The question remains unresolved (see [[hippocampus]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct derivation. The closest conceptual link concerns structure learning, where the form of a generative model, such as the number of hidden states, is itself inferred and models may be expanded when existing ones explain data poorly. Adding units to a circuit that encodes new experiences is an attractive biological analogy, as is the reduction of redundant structure through synaptic pruning. This is an analogy that motivates hypotheses, not an established mechanism, and mainstream Active Inference models treat network size as fixed. See [[neural_plasticity]].
