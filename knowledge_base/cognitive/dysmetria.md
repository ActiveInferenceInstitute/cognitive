# Dysmetria

## Overview
Dysmetria is a failure to judge the distance, speed or force needed to reach a target, so movements fall short (hypometria) or go past it (hypermetria). It is a defining feature of cerebellar disease and is one component of cerebellar ataxia. The affected limb moves in a decomposed and corrected way, with visible adjustments near the end of the movement.

## Clinical features
- **Finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin tests.** The patient alternates between touching their nose and the examiner's finger. In dysmetria the finger overshoots or undershoots, often with an intention tremor that grows as the target approaches.
- **Saccadic dysmetria.** Eye movements may also miss the target and need corrective saccades, reflecting involvement of cerebellar oculomotor circuits.
- **Laterality.** The deficit appears on the same side as a unilateral cerebellar hemisphere lesion.
- **Associated signs.** It commonly occurs with dysdiadochokinesia, gait ataxia and dysarthria.

The sign is not specific to one cause. It may follow stroke, tumours, demyelinating disease, toxins or degenerative conditions, and diagnosis depends on the full clinical picture. This description is for information and does not substitute for clinical advice.

## Mechanisms
The cerebellum is thought to learn internal models that predict the consequences of motor commands and use them to scale and time movements. With a damaged cerebellum, predictions of movement dynamics become inaccurate, so commands are mis-scaled and the brake that should end a movement acts too early or too late. Schmahmann proposed an extension, the dysmetria of thought hypothesis, in which the same cerebellar operation applied to cognition and emotion produces corresponding impairments, as in the cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome. This hypothesis remains debated in its scope.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference models movement as the fulfilment of proprioceptive predictions, with the precision of predictions determining accuracy. Errors in predicted dynamics, as proposed for cerebellar damage, would show up as misjudged amplitude. The cerebellum's exact role in this scheme is not fully settled, so the connection is an interpretive framework and not a clinical result.
