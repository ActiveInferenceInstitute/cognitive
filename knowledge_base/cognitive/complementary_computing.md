# Complementary Computing

## Overview
Complementary computing is a principle of brain organisation proposed by Stephen Grossberg. It offers an alternative to the view that the brain is a collection of independent modules, each with its own task, as in a conventional computer. Grossberg (2000) reviews evidence that brains are instead organised into parallel processing streams with **complementary properties**. Each stream computes some useful quantities well but cannot compute others, and the properties that one stream lacks are the ones its partner provides. Hierarchical interactions within each stream, together with parallel interactions between streams, then yield coherent representations that overcome the deficiencies of each stream alone and support unitary conscious experience.

## Examples in Grossberg's account
- **Boundaries and surfaces in vision.** Boundary processing groups edges into contours, and it is oriented, completes contours inward across gaps, and does not depend on the sign of contrast. Surface processing fills in colour and brightness, and it is unoriented, spreads outward within boundaries, and does depend on contrast polarity. Perception of a form combines the two.
- **What and where.** The ventral ("what") stream learns to recognise objects by categories that are invariant to position, while the dorsal ("where" or "how") stream represents spatial location and guides movement. In this account, recognition learning uses excitatory matching of expectations to inputs, as in [[adaptive_resonance_theory]], while spatial and motor processing uses inhibitory matching so that it can keep adapting to a changing body. See [[what_stream]], [[where_stream]], [[ventral_stream]] and [[dorsal_stream]].

## Connection to adaptive resonance theory
Complementary computing is closely tied to adaptive resonance theory (ART), which was introduced in Grossberg (1980) and reviewed in Grossberg (2013) as a theory of how the brain learns to categorise, recognise and predict objects and events in a changing world. In the what and where example above, ART supplies the learning and matching mechanisms assigned to the what stream.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is conceptual. Active Inference organises a hierarchical generative model whose levels exchange predictions and prediction errors, and it also associates distinct neural pathways with distinct computations. The complementary-computing claim is a different organising hypothesis, about why streams differ, and it was not derived from the free energy principle. Whether the two can be combined is a research question and not an established result.
