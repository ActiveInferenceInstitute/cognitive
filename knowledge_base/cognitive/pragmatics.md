# Pragmatics

## Overview
Pragmatics concerns how people use language appropriately in social contexts and how listeners recover what a speaker means rather than only what the words literally say. Saying "Can you pass the salt?" is normally a request, not a question about ability. Semantics studies conventional meaning, whereas pragmatics studies the additional inferences drawn from context, the speaker's evident goals, and what both parties know. Developmentally it emerges alongside [[theory_of_mind]], and difficulties with it are prominent in autism and some right-hemisphere or frontal lesions.

## Core ideas
- **Implicature**: Grice (1975) proposed that conversation is governed by a cooperative principle, with maxims of quantity, quality, relation and manner. Listeners infer that "some students passed" implies "not all", because a cooperative speaker would otherwise have said "all".
- **Relevance**: Sperber and Wilson (1986) replaced the maxims with a single principle, that utterances create an expectation of optimal relevance, balancing cognitive effort against informational gain.
- **Deixis and reference**: words such as "here", "you" or "that" depend on the situation of utterance.
- **Presupposition and politeness**: utterances assume background information, and speakers adjust form to social relationships.
- **Speech acts**: language performs actions, which is treated in [[speech_acts]].

Psycholinguistic work asks how fast these inferences occur and whether they are default or effortful. Evidence suggests that context affects interpretation early, though the specific cost of scalar implicatures is disputed.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an active but young research area. Probabilistic accounts such as the Rational Speech Act framework (Frank & Goodman, 2012) model a listener as using Bayes' rule to infer a speaker's intended meaning, assuming the speaker chose words to be informative. This has the same structure as inference in a shared generative model, where each agent infers hidden states of the other. Active Inference work on communication, for example the idea of two agents aligning their models through exchange of signals, treats utterances as actions chosen to reduce uncertainty, which parallels pragmatic reasoning. These are formal analogies and exploratory models; they do not yet account for most empirical pragmatic phenomena. See [[social_cognition]].
