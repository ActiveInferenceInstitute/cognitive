# Default Mode Network

## Overview
The default mode network (DMN) is a large-scale brain network whose activity is high during wakeful rest and mind wandering and is suppressed when attention is directed to an external, demanding task. It was identified from consistent task-related deactivations in neuroimaging and from correlated spontaneous fluctuations across its nodes. Here "default_mode" refers to this network, not to a behavioural default setting.

## Anatomy and function
Core nodes are the medial prefrontal cortex, the posterior cingulate cortex and adjacent precuneus, and the angular gyrus within the inferior parietal lobule; the hippocampal formation and lateral temporal cortex form commonly cited subsystems. Functional connectivity analyses of resting data recover these regions as a coherent network, which is typically anticorrelated with dorsal attention and frontoparietal control systems.

Tasks that engage the DMN share a theme of internally generated content: autobiographical memory retrieval, imagining the future, spatial and scene construction, thinking about other people's mental states, and self-referential judgement. Its activity is therefore associated with spontaneous thought, and failure of its normal task-related suppression has been linked to attentional lapses. These associations are correlational, and the network is heterogeneous, so a single function for the whole network is not established.

Altered DMN connectivity is reported in many conditions, including depression, schizophrenia and ageing. Effect sizes are modest and findings vary with methods, so such results are best treated as research hypotheses rather than diagnostic markers.

## Role in Active Inference
Carhart-Harris and Friston (2010) proposed that the DMN sits near the top of the cortical hierarchy and encodes high-level, self-related priors that constrain lower-level inference, with its activity reflecting the precision those priors carry. This is an interpretive proposal within the predictive processing framework, motivated partly by studies of psychedelic states, and not a derivation from the free-energy principle. Related ideas treat DMN-dependent episodic simulation as sampling from a generative model to evaluate possible futures, which matches the planning-as-inference view of policy selection. The evidence is suggestive and still debated.
