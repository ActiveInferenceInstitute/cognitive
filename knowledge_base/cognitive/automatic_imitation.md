# Automatic Imitation

## Overview
Automatic imitation is the tendency for observing an action to facilitate the execution of the same action and to interfere with a different one, even when the observer intends to respond otherwise. The term is used for two related phenomena. In the laboratory, it refers to stimulus-response compatibility effects produced by observed movements. In social psychology, it is also applied to unintentional mimicry of the postures, gestures and expressions of others, which is a wider and less tightly controlled behaviour (see [[imitation]]).

## Measurement
The standard experimental task asks participants to perform a prespecified movement, such as lifting a finger or opening or closing the hand, as soon as a cue appears, while they watch a video of a hand making either the same or the opposite movement that is irrelevant to the task. Responses are faster when the observed movement matches the executed one and slower when it does not (Brass et al., 2000; Heyes, 2011). The compatibility effect is larger for biological movements than for comparable non-biological stimuli in many studies, though the findings vary with the stimuli and are debated.

## Origins and modulation
- **Associative learning.** Heyes proposed that matching arises from experience in which seeing and performing the same action occur together, so that visual and motor representations become linked. Training with counter-mirror experience reduces the effect, which supports a role for learning.
- **Mirror system.** Others stress an innate or evolved role for the mirror system, whose neurons respond during both execution and observation (see [[mirror_system]]).
- **Social modulation.** Studies report that the effect can be larger toward in-group members, after being mimicked and when prosocial goals are primed, and smaller when the observer is attending to the difference between self and other. Chartrand and Bargh (1999) reported that unintentional mimicry increases liking, though the size of the effect is uncertain.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is a developed proposal. Kilner, Friston and Frith (2007) argued that the mirror system can be understood within predictive coding. On this account, observed actions are explained by a hierarchical generative model of the actions the observer would perform, and prediction errors at each level are reduced by inferring the intention, goal, kinematics and muscle commands that explain the movement. Automatic imitation would then reflect shared representation of observed and executed action, with competing predictions at the motor level. The account is compatible with the data but is not the only one (see [[predictive_processing]] and [[action_recognition]]).
