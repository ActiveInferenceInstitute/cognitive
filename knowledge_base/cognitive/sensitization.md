# Sensitization

## Overview
Sensitization is a simple form of non-associative learning in which a strong or noxious stimulus produces an enhanced response to later stimuli, including ones that are different from the original. It is the counterpart of [[habituation]], in which repeated harmless stimulation reduces the response. Unlike classical conditioning, it does not require pairing between two stimuli. A person startled by a loud noise who then jumps at a minor sound a moment later shows an everyday form of it.

## Cellular basis
The best-studied model is the gill-and-siphon withdrawal reflex of the sea slug Aplysia californica. A strong shock to the tail increases the withdrawal response to a light touch of the siphon. Kandel and colleagues showed that the shock activates modulatory interneurons that release serotonin onto sensory neurons, raising cyclic AMP and increasing transmitter release from the sensory neuron onto motor neurons. This is heterosynaptic facilitation, because the modulating input is separate from the synapse that is strengthened. A single shock produces short-term changes lasting minutes; repeated, spaced shocks produce long-term changes that depend on gene expression and the growth of new synaptic connections (Kandel, 2001).

## Dual-process view
Groves and Thompson (1970) proposed that behaviour after repeated stimulation reflects two independent processes, habituation and sensitization, which sum to determine the response. Sensitization is related to arousal state and is typically transient unless the stimulus is intense or repeated.

## Other uses
In pain research, central sensitization denotes increased responsiveness of central neurons to input, contributing to heightened pain sensitivity. In the same vein, repeated drug exposure can produce behavioural sensitization. These senses share the idea of progressive amplification but have different mechanisms.

## Relation to Active Inference
Sensitization can be interpreted as an increase in the precision afforded to sensory input after a signal of threat, so that subsequent inputs drive stronger updating. This reading is an analogy, and the Aplysia mechanism itself is described in terms of molecular signalling rather than inference.
