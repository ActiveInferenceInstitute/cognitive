# Sensory Gating

## Overview
Sensory gating is the reduction of neural and behavioural responses to sensory input that is redundant, predictable or irrelevant, so that higher processing is not overloaded. Crick (1984) suggested that the thalamic reticular nucleus could act as a searchlight that gates thalamic transmission to cortex, an idea that helped frame gating as a mechanism of attention. Gating occurs at several levels, from spinal and brainstem reflexes to cortical responses.

## Measures
- **Prepulse inhibition (PPI).** A weak stimulus shortly before a startling stimulus reduces the startle reflex. It is usually described as sensorimotor gating, can be measured in humans and rodents, and is often used in translational research.
- **P50 suppression.** In a paired-click paradigm, the electroencephalographic response to the second of two identical clicks, about half a second after the first, is smaller than the response to the first. The ratio indexes how well the response to a repeated stimulus is suppressed.
- **Mismatch and N1 effects.** Reduced responses to repeated sounds are related, but more broadly considered adaptation and prediction effects.

These measures do not necessarily reflect a single mechanism, and correlations among them in individuals are often weak.

## Clinical relevance
Reduced PPI (Braff & Geyer, 1990) and reduced P50 suppression have been reported in groups of people with schizophrenia, and have been proposed as markers of impaired filtering. The findings are variable across studies and are not diagnostic, and reduced gating has been reported in other conditions too. Pharmacological and developmental factors influence PPI in animal models.

## Relation to Active Inference
In predictive-processing terms, gating can be read as modulation of precision: sensory prediction errors that are expected to be uninformative are given low weight, while those that are likely to matter are amplified. Accounts of psychosis in this framework propose that abnormal precision at one or more levels of a hierarchy could explain atypical gating (Adams et al., 2013). This is an active research programme with computational models, not an established mechanism.
