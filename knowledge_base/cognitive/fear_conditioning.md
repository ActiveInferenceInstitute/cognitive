# Fear Conditioning

## Overview
Fear conditioning is a form of Pavlovian learning in which a neutral conditioned stimulus, such as a tone, is paired with an aversive unconditioned stimulus, such as a mild electric shock. After pairing, the tone alone elicits conditioned fear responses: freezing, heightened startle, changes in heart rate and blood pressure, and stress hormone release in rodents, with comparable autonomic and subjective responses in humans. It is a standard laboratory model of how threat associations form and how they may go wrong in anxiety disorders.

## Circuitry
Work by LeDoux and others identified the amygdala as central (LeDoux, 2000). Sensory information about the tone and shock converges in the lateral amygdala, where synaptic strengthening of tone inputs is thought to store the association. Output passes through the basal and central nuclei to the periaqueductal gray, hypothalamus and brainstem, which organise defensive behaviour and autonomic responses. The hippocampus contributes to learning about context, and the medial prefrontal cortex regulates expression of fear (Maren and Quirk, 2004).

## Extinction and reconsolidation
Repeated presentation of the conditioned stimulus without the shock reduces the fear response, called extinction. Behavioural evidence indicates that extinction is new learning that competes with the original memory rather than erasure of it: fear can return with the passage of time, in a new context, or after unsignalled shocks (Bouton, 2004). Retrieval may also make a consolidated memory temporarily labile, so that it must be stored again (Nader, Schafe and LeDoux, 2000), which has motivated studies of interventions at retrieval.

## Clinical relevance
Conditioning, extinction and their failures are used as models for phobias, panic and post-traumatic stress disorder, and exposure therapy resembles extinction training. Models are simplified: human anxiety disorders involve cognition and context beyond simple conditioning.

## Relation to Active Inference
Fear learning is commonly described with prediction-error rules in the tradition of Rescorla and Wagner. In Active Inference the same learning appears as updating beliefs about the outcome model linking cues to aversive events, with avoidance chosen because aversive outcomes are strongly dispreferred in the prior preferences. This reframes the data in a shared formalism, but it does not by itself make distinctive empirical predictions, and standard associative-learning models give similar accounts.
