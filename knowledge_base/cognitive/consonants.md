# Consonants

## Overview
Consonants are speech sounds made with a narrowing or closure somewhere in the vocal tract that obstructs or redirects the airstream. They contrast with vowels, which are produced with a relatively open tract and usually form the peak of a syllable. Languages differ greatly in the size of their consonant inventories, and the International Phonetic Alphabet provides symbols for describing consonants across languages.

## Articulatory classification
A consonant is described by three main properties.
- **Place of articulation**: where the constriction occurs, for example bilabial (both lips, as in /p/), alveolar (tongue tip at the ridge behind the teeth, as in /t/), velar (back of the tongue at the soft palate, as in /k/) or glottal.
- **Manner of articulation**: how the airstream is modified. Stops block it completely, fricatives create turbulent flow through a narrow gap, affricates combine the two, nasals send air through the nose, and approximants and laterals involve a narrowing too slight to cause turbulence.
- **Voicing**: whether the vocal folds vibrate during the sound, as in /b/ versus /p/.

## Acoustic and perceptual aspects
Consonants are cued by brief, rapidly changing acoustic events, including bursts, frication noise and formant transitions into neighbouring vowels. For stop consonants, voice onset time, the interval between the release of closure and the start of voicing, is a major cue to the voicing contrast. Listeners tend to perceive voice onset time categorically, with a sharp boundary between categories, and the boundary location differs across languages. Consonants combine with vowels to form syllables, and languages impose constraints on which consonant sequences are allowed in onsets and codas.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no specific Active Inference theory of consonants. Speech perception more generally has been modelled as hierarchical inference, in which top-down predictions from lexical and contextual levels constrain interpretation of ambiguous acoustic input such as a consonant of uncertain voicing. This is an application of predictive processing to the topic, not a finding about consonants themselves.
