# Angular Gyrus

## Overview
The angular gyrus is a region of the inferior parietal lobule, corresponding approximately to Brodmann area 39, lying at the posterior end of the superior temporal sulcus where the temporal, parietal and occipital lobes meet. It is anatomically positioned to receive visual, auditory and somatosensory information and is connected with temporal, frontal and medial parietal cortex. Rather than a single function, it is associated with a cluster of tasks that share a need to bind information from different sources.

## Functions and evidence
- **Semantic processing.** Meta-analyses of functional imaging find consistent activation of the left angular gyrus during tasks that require retrieval or integration of conceptual knowledge, alongside the anterior temporal lobe (see [[temporal_pole]]) and prefrontal regions (Binder et al., 2009).
- **Reading.** Damage to the left angular gyrus has been associated with alexia accompanied by agraphia, and the region is engaged when written words are mapped to meaning. Atypical function in this area is discussed in connection with [[dyslexia]].
- **Number and calculation.** Left-sided lesions are classically linked with Gerstmann syndrome (acalculia, agraphia, finger agnosia and left-right confusion), although the anatomical specificity of this syndrome is debated.
- **Memory retrieval.** Parietal activity increases when items are recognised or recollected, and the region is a component of the default mode network, which is active during autobiographical recall and internally directed thought (see [[episodic_memory]]).

Seghier (2013) reviews evidence that the region contains several subdivisions with different connectivity, which helps explain why accounts that assign it one role, such as a semantic hub or an attentional reorienting site, each cover only part of the data.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no dedicated Active Inference model of the angular gyrus. The link is interpretive: in hierarchical generative models, higher levels combine information across modalities to form amodal representations that constrain lower-level predictions (see [[multisensory_integration]]). A convergence zone such as the angular gyrus is a plausible anatomical candidate for part of such a level, in particular for context-dependent word meaning. This should be read as an analogy that guides hypotheses, not as an established mapping.
