# Secondary Emotions

## Overview
Some theories of emotion divide emotions into primary and secondary categories. Primary emotions are a small set, such as fear, anger, joy, sadness, disgust and surprise, held to be innate, early developing and shared across cultures. Secondary emotions are those that are derived from or built on the primary ones, usually through learning, social experience and more elaborate cognitive evaluation. Examples commonly given are guilt, shame, pride, jealousy and embarrassment. The term has several senses, and it is distinct from "secondary appraisal" in appraisal theory, which concerns evaluation of coping resources (see [[primary_appraisal]]).

## Major usages
Plutchik (1980) proposed that primary emotions are arranged on a wheel of opposed pairs, and that other emotions arise as blends of neighbouring primaries, so that, for example, joy together with trust yields love. Damasio (1994) separated primary emotions, mediated by limbic circuits including the amygdala (see [[emotion_circuits]]), from secondary emotions, which require the additional evaluation of situations and recall of past experience through prefrontal and somatosensory cortices. In developmental and social psychology, a related category is the self-conscious emotions, including guilt, shame and pride, which require a self-concept and evaluation against standards (Tracy & Robins, 2004). These emerge later in childhood than the basic expressions, which is one of the stronger arguments for the primary-secondary distinction.

## Contested status
The distinction depends on basic emotion theory, which holds that a limited set of emotions are discrete natural kinds with distinct expressions and neural circuits (Ekman, 1992). Critics argue that evidence for discrete circuits and universal expressions is weaker than claimed and that emotions are constructed from more general core affect and conceptual knowledge, in which case no emotions are primary and others secondary (Barrett, 2006). Different lists of primaries also disagree. The terms are therefore best treated as descriptive conventions within particular theories.

## Relation to Active Inference
Some Active Inference and predictive processing accounts treat emotion as inference about the causes of interoceptive signals, consistent with constructionist views and with a hierarchy in which complex emotions rely on higher-level models (Seth, 2013). This is an active but contested programme, and it does not endorse a primary-secondary split.
