# Artificial Consciousness

## Overview
Artificial consciousness (machine consciousness) is the question of whether an artificial system could have subjective experience, and the research programme of building or assessing systems with properties associated with consciousness. It must be separated from intelligence: a system can be highly capable without anyone having shown that it is conscious, and the converse is not excluded.

## Mechanism
The debate turns on prior commitments about what consciousness is. Under computational functionalism, what matters is the functional organisation of a system, so suitable software on any substrate could be conscious. Searle's Chinese room argument is the best-known objection that running a program, being syntactic, is not sufficient for understanding or experience. Biological views hold that consciousness depends on life-related processes such as metabolism or self-maintenance, which present AI lacks. Integrated information theory holds that experience depends on physical causal structure, so a conventional digital computer would score very low whatever it runs ([[integrated_information]]). Chalmers's distinction between easy problems (functions) and the hard problem (why there is experience at all) frames the disagreement over whether functional accounts can ever suffice.

A practical, theory-informed approach is to derive indicator properties from scientific theories and check systems against them. Butlin, Long, and colleagues (2023) did this for theories including [[global_workspace]], recurrent processing, higher-order theories, predictive processing, attention schema, and agency and embodiment; they concluded that no existing AI system was a strong candidate, while noting no obvious technical barrier to building systems that satisfy more indicators. Behavioural fluency, including reports of experience by language models, is weak evidence, because such systems are trained on human descriptions of experience.

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference is sometimes cited as supplying ingredients: deep temporal generative models, a self-model, counterfactual planning, and interoceptive or homeostatic grounding, which some theorists consider necessary and others (Seth) tie to being a living organism. The agents in this framework are statistical models implemented in code and are not claimed to be conscious; implementing Active Inference does not by itself establish experience. The relation is a set of candidate architectural criteria, not a demonstration, and which of them if any are sufficient is exactly what the underlying theories dispute. See [[artificial_agents]] and [[active_inference_agent]] for the agent models themselves.
