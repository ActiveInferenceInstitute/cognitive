# Binocular Rivalry

## Overview
Binocular rivalry occurs when the two eyes are presented with dissimilar images, such as a vertical grating to one eye and a horizontal grating to the other. Instead of fusing the images or seeing a stable superposition, the observer experiences perception that alternates over seconds between the two, with brief transitions in which parts of both are visible. The stimulus is constant, but the percept changes, which makes the phenomenon a standard tool for studying visual awareness.

## Characteristics
Dominance durations vary randomly from one period to the next, and their distribution is approximately gamma-shaped. Increasing the contrast of one image mainly shortens the dominance periods of the other image, a regularity often called Levelt's second proposition. Stimulus strength, attention and eye of origin all influence dominance. Conventional accounts emphasised mutual inhibition between neurons or populations representing each eye's input, combined with slow adaptation of the dominant population, so that dominance eventually fades and the other image takes over. Blake and Logothetis (2002) review the evidence that competition occurs at several stages of the visual system, including competition between stimulus representations and not only between eyes.

## Neural findings
Recordings in monkeys show that activity in early visual cortex follows the perceptual alternation only modestly, while neurons in higher visual areas follow the reported percept more closely. In humans, imaging studies find that activity in visual areas correlates with the dominant percept, and that parietal and frontal regions are involved in switches. The extent to which these regions cause, and do not merely accompany, perceptual change is debated.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an explicit and well-known proposal. Hohwy, Roepstorff and Friston (2008) argued that rivalry follows from hierarchical predictive coding. Each monocular image is a plausible hypothesis, but neither explains all the sensory input, so the system alternates between hypotheses as prediction error accumulates for the currently favoured one. This account relies on the brain's priors against seeing two different objects in the same place. It reproduces key features qualitatively, but alternative models based on inhibition and adaptation also fit the data, so the evidence is not decisive.
