# Self-Regulation

## Overview
Self-regulation is the set of processes by which a system monitors and adjusts its own states and actions in pursuit of goals or standards. In psychology it covers control over attention, emotion, impulses, and behaviour; in physiology the term is used for the maintenance of bodily variables, which overlaps with [[homeostatic_regulation]].

## Mechanism
The dominant formal idea is the negative feedback loop. Miller, Galanter, and Pribram's TOTE unit (Test-Operate-Test-Exit) and Carver and Scheier's control-theory model describe behaviour as comparison of a current state to a reference value, with action taken to reduce the discrepancy. Goals are arranged hierarchically: higher-level goals set reference values for lower-level loops. Related work distinguishes goal setting, monitoring, and strategy selection, and the temporal discounting of rewards in favour of long-term aims.

Ability to inhibit impulses and override habits ([[inhibitory_control]]), hold goals in mind ([[working_memory]]), and shift strategies ([[cognitive_flexibility]]) are the cognitive components. The resource model, in which self-control draws on a limited, depletable energy supply (ego depletion), was influential, but a large preregistered multi-lab replication reported an effect close to zero, and the account is now widely regarded as unsupported in its original form; alternative accounts emphasise shifting motivation and opportunity cost. Self-regulation also develops over childhood and varies between individuals ([[individual_differences]]), and the term is used clinically in several fields without a single shared definition.

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference is a control-theoretic framework in which prior preferences over outcomes act as reference values and action reduces the discrepancy between predicted and sensed states; Pezzulo, Rigoli, and Friston described homeostatic and allostatic regulation as active inference over a hierarchy in which higher levels set the set-points for lower ones. This maps naturally onto hierarchical feedback models of self-regulation ([[allostatic_control]], [[hierarchical_inference]]). The framework adds that the set-points are themselves beliefs that can be updated and that [[precision_weighting]] decides how strongly a discrepancy drives action. The mapping is formal and conceptually close, but empirical tests that separate the Active Inference reading from standard control theory are limited, and complex human self-control (long-range goals, social standards) is only partly captured by existing models.
