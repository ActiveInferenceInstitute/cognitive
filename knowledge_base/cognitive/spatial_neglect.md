# Spatial Neglect

## Overview
Spatial neglect is a neuropsychological syndrome in which a patient fails to notice, respond to or orient toward stimuli on the side of space opposite the brain lesion, in a way that cannot be attributed to a primary sensory or motor deficit. It is most common and severe after right-hemisphere damage, so the neglected side is usually the left. Patients may leave food on one side of a plate, ignore one side of their body, shave or dress half of the face or body, and omit one side when copying or drawing (Parton, Malhotra and Husain, 2004).

## Features and assessment
Standard bedside tests include line bisection, in which patients mark the midpoint of a line toward the unaffected side, cancellation tasks, in which targets on one side are missed, and drawing from memory. Neglect is not a single deficit. It can be egocentric or relative to an object, can affect personal, near or far space, and can occur in imagined space: Bisiach and Luzzatti (1978) found that patients describing a familiar square from memory omitted landmarks on the left, with the omissions reversing when they imagined viewing it from the opposite side. Many patients also lack awareness of their deficit. Recovery is often substantial in the first months, and rehabilitation methods include prism adaptation and visual scanning training.

## Anatomy and theory
Lesions associated with neglect involve the inferior parietal lobule and temporoparietal junction, the superior temporal cortex and frontal regions, and the white-matter tracts that connect them (Karnath and Rorden, 2012). The exact critical region is debated. A network account proposes that damage to the right ventral attention network and its interaction with dorsal attention areas produces a rightward bias, with an imbalance between the hemispheres (Corbetta and Shulman, 2011). Competition between hemispheres also underlies explanations of [[extinction]].

## Relation to Active Inference
Parr and Friston (2018) built an Active Inference model, based on a Markov decision process, that reproduces visual search on a line cancellation task, and showed in simulation that lesioning the model in three distinct ways produced search deficits resembling visual neglect. This computational study demonstrates plausibility, not an empirical test, and it complements established network accounts. It is an example of an active research programme in computational neurology.
