# Sensory Substitution

## Overview
Sensory substitution is the delivery of information normally gathered by one sense through another, using a device that converts the signals of the lost or absent modality into patterns that the user's remaining senses can receive. Typical applications convert visual scenes into touch or sound for blind users, or sound into touch for deaf users. After training, users can interpret the new signals, localise objects and sometimes report experiences that resemble the original modality.

## History and findings
- **Tactile vision.** Bach-y-Rita and colleagues (1969) described a device that converted camera images into patterns of vibration or stimulation on the skin of the back. Blind participants learned to discriminate shapes and, notably, to attribute the sensations to objects in space rather than to the skin.
- **Auditory and other devices.** Later systems convert images to soundscapes, as in the vOICe developed by Meijer (1992), or deliver pixelated images to the tongue. Newer devices map distance or orientation to touch or sound.
- **Role of action.** Learning is faster and more reliable when users control the sensor, for example by moving a camera, than when images are passively delivered. This observation supports sensorimotor accounts of perception (O'Regan & Noe, 2001).
- **Brain changes.** Imaging has reported activation of visual cortical areas in blind users of such devices, which is taken as evidence of cross-modal plasticity, though the strength and functional role of that activation are debated. Bach-y-Rita and Kercel (2003) review the approach and its implications.

Substitution devices are limited by the bandwidth of the receiving channel and by the cognitive load of learning an artificial code, and they have not displaced other assistive technologies.

## Relation to Active Inference
Perception in Active Inference is inference about hidden causes from whatever sensory data arrive, so the identity of the transducing channel is not what matters; what matters is that a stable mapping from causes to sensations can be learned. A substitution device supplies a new likelihood mapping, and active sampling lets the user test hypotheses about the scene. This is a conceptual fit with the framework and with sensorimotor contingency theories, and no specific model has been established for these devices.
