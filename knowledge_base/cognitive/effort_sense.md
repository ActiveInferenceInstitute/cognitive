# Effort Sense

## Overview
The sense of effort is the conscious impression of how hard one is working, whether to lift a weight, sustain a posture, run, or hold attention on a demanding task. It is distinct from the sensation of muscle force, pain or breathlessness, although these often accompany it. Psychologists measure it with ratings of perceived exertion, such as the Borg scale (Borg, 1982), and with matching tasks in which a person reproduces a force felt in one limb with the other.

## Proposed origins
Two broad accounts have been contrasted.
- **Central (corollary discharge) accounts.** The sense of effort derives from the motor command itself, or from a copy of it sent to sensory areas, and not from signals returning from the periphery (see [[efference_copy]]). Support comes from findings that when muscles are weakened by fatigue or partial paralysis, a stronger command is needed for the same force and the same force feels heavier. Marcora (2009) argued that perceived effort during exercise is independent of afferent feedback from muscles, heart and lungs and reflects central motor command.
- **Peripheral accounts.** Signals from muscle, joint, skin, heart and lungs contribute to the impression of exertion. Reviews conclude that the sense of force relies on both central and peripheral signals, with their weighting depending on the task (Proske & Gandevia, 2012).

## Mental effort
Cognitive tasks also feel effortful. Shenhav and colleagues (2017) proposed that mental effort reflects the computed cost of exerting control weighed against its expected benefit, and that this is related to decisions about whether to engage in demanding activity.

## Role in Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. In Active Inference, descending signals are seen as predictions, and the precision of proprioceptive prediction error determines how readily they are fulfilled (see [[motor_control]]). Effort could be read as an inference about the cost or difficulty of enacting a policy, in which an unusually large prediction error accompanies a given movement. This is a hypothesis in a developing literature, and it has not replaced the corollary discharge account.
