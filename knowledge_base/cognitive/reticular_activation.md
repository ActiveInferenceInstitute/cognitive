# Reticular Activation

## Overview
Reticular activation refers to the ascending reticular activating system (ARAS), the network of brainstem and diencephalic neurons whose ascending projections keep the cerebral cortex in an awake, responsive state. It originates in the reticular formation, a diffuse core of neurons in the midbrain, pons and medulla, and acts through the thalamus, hypothalamus and basal forebrain. Its integrity is a precondition for the level of consciousness, or wakefulness (see [[wakefulness]]).

## Discovery
Moruzzi and Magoun (1949) found that electrical stimulation of the brainstem reticular formation in cats replaced the slow, synchronised cortical rhythm of sleep with the fast, desynchronised activity of alertness. Later lesion experiments showed that interrupting this region of the upper brainstem, while sparing the classical sensory pathways, produced a sleep-like state in animals. These findings overturned the idea that wakefulness was a passive result of sensory input, and showed that it depends on an active arousal system.

## Components
Modern work shows that the system is not a single pathway but several parallel arousal systems that converge on thalamus and cortex. They include cholinergic neurons in the pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei, noradrenergic neurons of the locus coeruleus (see [[noradrenergic_system]]), serotonergic neurons of the raphe nuclei, dopaminergic neurons of the midbrain, and histaminergic and orexin-containing neurons in the hypothalamus. Small lesions in the upper pons or midbrain can cause coma, whereas extensive cortical damage may leave arousal intact, which is why the distinction between level and content of consciousness is useful (Parvizi & Damasio, 2001; see [[coma]] and [[consciousness]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is interpretive. Active Inference accounts treat arousal-related neuromodulators as setting the precision or gain of neural populations, so that the ascending systems determine how strongly prediction errors influence cortical beliefs. Some theories of consciousness built on this framework propose that a sufficient level of arousal is needed for the hierarchical inference that supports conscious experience. These ideas link established anatomy to theory in a plausible but unproven way, and the ARAS is not itself a component of the formal models. See [[vigilance]].
