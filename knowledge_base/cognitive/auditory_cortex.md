# Auditory Cortex

## Overview
The auditory cortex is the part of the cerebral cortex that receives and analyses acoustic information relayed through the medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus. In humans it lies on the superior surface of the temporal lobe, including Heschl's gyrus and the adjacent planum temporale. It transforms the spectrotemporal pattern coded by the cochlea and brainstem into representations of pitch, timbre, location and sound identity that support speech and music perception (see [[auditory_processing]]).

## Organisation
Work in primates describes a hierarchical arrangement of a primary core, a surrounding belt and a more lateral parabelt. Core fields are tonotopically organised, meaning that neighbouring locations respond to neighbouring sound frequencies, and respond well to simple tones. Belt and parabelt fields respond preferentially to more complex sounds and are less strictly tonotopic.

Beyond these regions, processing is commonly described as splitting into two streams. A ventral stream projecting toward anterior temporal and inferior frontal cortex is associated with identifying sounds, while a dorsal stream projecting toward parietal and premotor cortex is associated with sound location and with sensorimotor mapping of speech (Rauschecker & Scott, 2009; Hickok & Poeppel, 2007). The exact functional division, especially for speech, is still debated. Left and right auditory regions also differ in emphasis, with left-hemisphere areas often linked to fast temporal changes and right-hemisphere areas to spectral detail and pitch contour.

## Relation to Active Inference
Auditory cortex is one of the better-studied cases for predictive accounts of perception. The mismatch negativity, an evoked response to a deviant sound within a regular sequence, has been modelled as a prediction error signal arising from changes in synaptic efficacy and top-down expectations within a hierarchy of temporal and frontal sources (Garrido et al., 2009; see [[predictive_coding]] and [[prediction_error]]). Such models are fitted to electrophysiological data and are an active research programme, but the data are also compatible with adaptation-based explanations, so the evidence does not by itself force a predictive interpretation. For speech, hierarchical generative models of sound sequences offer a formal account of how context sharpens recognition (see [[speech_perception]]).
