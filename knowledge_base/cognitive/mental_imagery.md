# Mental Imagery

## Overview
Mental imagery is the experience of sensory qualities without the corresponding external stimulus. It occurs in all modalities, though visual imagery has been studied most. It is used in remembering, planning, spatial reasoning and skill rehearsal, and it appears in dreams and in intrusive memories. Imagery is distinguished from [[imagination]] in general because it concerns sensory-like content, whereas imagination can be propositional.

## The imagery debate
Behavioural work such as the mental rotation experiments of Shepard and Metzler (1971), in which response time rose with the angle through which an object had to be mentally turned, suggested that imagery involves analogue transformations. Kosslyn and colleagues (2006) argue that imagery uses depictive representations that engage the same visual areas as perception. Pylyshyn (2002) countered that behaviour attributed to images can arise from propositional representations and tacit knowledge. Neuroimaging has shown activity in visual cortex, including early areas, during imagery, and decoding methods can predict imagined content from activity patterns that resemble those during perception, which supports the depictive view, though the strength of early-area involvement varies across studies.

## Individual differences
Vividness varies greatly. Some people report little or no visual imagery, a condition called aphantasia (Zeman et al., 2015), while others report unusually vivid imagery. Such differences do not map simply onto performance in all tasks, which suggests that imagery as experienced and imagery as used cognitively can diverge. Pearson (2019) reviews the cognitive neuroscience of imagery, including its role in working memory and in emotional disorders.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive accounts interpret imagery as top-down generation by the [[generative_model]] when bottom-up sensory evidence is weak or absent, so perception and imagery differ chiefly in the relative weight of sensory input. Dynamic causal modelling of fMRI data found increased bottom-up coupling during perception, and increased top-down coupling during both perception and imagery, with a much stronger top-down increase during imagery (Dijkstra et al., 2017). This is an active research programme with encouraging but limited evidence, and rival theories of imagery, such as those emphasising working memory, make overlapping predictions.
