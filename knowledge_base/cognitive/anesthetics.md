# Anesthetics

## Overview
Anesthetics are drugs that reduce or abolish sensation. Local anesthetics block conduction in nerves in one region of the body. General anesthetics produce a reversible loss of responsiveness and, for most agents, of consciousness, together with amnesia and loss of reaction to surgical stimulation. This page concerns general anesthetics, which are of interest to cognitive science because they allow consciousness to be switched off and on in a controlled way.

## Agents and targets
- **Intravenous agents.** Propofol and etomidate enhance the action of GABA at GABA-A receptors. Ketamine instead blocks [[nmda_receptors]] and at sub-anesthetic doses produces dissociative effects.
- **Inhaled agents.** Volatile drugs such as sevoflurane and isoflurane act on several targets, including GABA-A receptors and certain potassium channels. No single molecular target accounts for all agents.
- **Systems level.** Anesthesia affects arousal circuits in the brainstem, thalamus and cortex. Clinical anesthesia combines hypnosis, analgesia and often muscle relaxation, and these components are managed separately by clinicians.

## Brain dynamics
Under propofol, EEG shows large slow oscillations and changes in frontal alpha activity. Studies report reduced long-range cortical communication, especially feedback from frontal to posterior regions, and diminished complexity of evoked responses (see [[frontoparietal]] and [[oscillations]]). Brown, Lydic and Schiff (2010) review the relation of anesthesia to sleep and coma. Ketamine anesthesia differs, and can preserve cortical activity while behaviour is unresponsive, which complicates simple accounts that link unconsciousness to reduced activity.

## Relation to theories of consciousness
Anesthesia is used as an experimental perturbation to test theories of consciousness. [[global_workspace]] theories predict a loss of widespread ignition and broadcast. [[integrated_information]] theory predicts reduced integration and differentiation of cortical activity. Both are consistent with parts of the evidence, and the data do not decide between them.

## Relation to Active Inference
Predictive-processing accounts of consciousness often treat anesthesia as a state in which hierarchical message passing, particularly top-down prediction, is disrupted, and studies of auditory deviance responses under anesthesia have been used to test this idea. The link is empirical and interpretive. It is not a derivation from the free energy principle, and competing interpretations exist (see [[predictive_processing]] and [[consciousness_theory]]).
