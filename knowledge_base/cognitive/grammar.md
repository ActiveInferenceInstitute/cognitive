# Grammar

## Overview
Grammar is the system of principles that governs how words and smaller meaningful units are combined into phrases and sentences. In the sense used for development, it also covers morphology, such as plural and tense endings, and syntax, such as word order and the hierarchical structure of clauses. Learning grammar means acquiring productive knowledge, since children use rules to produce and understand sentences they have never heard.

## Developmental course
Children typically begin with single words, move to two-word combinations around the second year, and then add inflections, function words and more complex constructions over the following years. A familiar pattern is that children first produce irregular forms such as "went" correctly, then overregularise to "goed", and later recover the correct form. This is often cited as evidence that learners extract general rules and do not merely memorise.

## Theoretical accounts
- **Nativist accounts.** Following Chomsky, some researchers argue that a specialised, innate language faculty constrains the grammars that learners consider, partly because of the apparent mismatch between the input available and the knowledge acquired.
- **Usage-based accounts.** Researchers such as Tomasello argue that grammar emerges from general cognitive abilities, including pattern-finding and intention-reading, applied to experience with specific constructions that become gradually more abstract.
- **Statistical learning.** Infants detect regularities in sequences. Saffran, Aslin and Newport (1996) showed that eight-month-old infants can use transitional probabilities to segment syllable streams, which suggests that distributional learning contributes to early language.

These positions are not mutually exclusive, and the question of how much structure is built in remains unsettled.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference treats language comprehension and production as inference with hierarchical generative models, and Friston and colleagues (2020) have simulated dialogue between synthetic agents using deep generative models whose factorial structure is suited to the combinatorics of language. That work concerns communication and sequential language processing, not the acquisition of grammar. This is an active research direction. Whether the framework adds explanatory power for how grammar is learned, beyond other probabilistic models, has not been established.
