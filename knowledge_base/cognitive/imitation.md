# Imitation

## Overview
Imitation is the reproduction of another individual's observed action, including its form or goal. It is a major route of [[social_learning]] and cultural transmission. Researchers separate true imitation (copying the action and often its means) from emulation (reproducing an outcome by one's own means), and from mimicry (automatic, often unintended matching of posture, expression, or speech).

## Mechanism
Imitation poses the correspondence problem: how a visual observation of someone else's body is converted into one's own motor commands. Proposed solutions include a direct matching mechanism, in which observed actions activate corresponding motor representations through a mirror-neuron system; associative sequence learning, in which matching arises from experience of seeing and doing the same action together (Heyes); and goal-directed or ideomotor accounts. Mirror neurons were discovered in macaque premotor and inferior parietal areas ([[inferior_parietal]]); whether human imitation depends on them, and how, is debated.

Developmental findings are contested. Meltzoff and Moore reported neonatal imitation of facial gestures in 1977, but a later longitudinal study (Oostenbroek and colleagues, 2016) found no evidence for it, so its existence is unsettled. Deferred imitation and selective imitation of intentional actions are better documented in older infants. Automatic mimicry, as in Chartrand and Bargh's "chameleon effect", is associated with rapport, but its strength and generality have been questioned by replication efforts of related social-priming work. Imitation relates to [[joint_attention]], [[perspective_taking]], and the learning of [[motor_control]] skills.

## Role in Active Inference
Kilner, Friston, and Frith proposed that the mirror-neuron system is a product of hierarchical predictive coding: observing an action means inferring its goal and intention as the cause of the sensory input, and the same generative model that produces one's own actions, when inverted, explains others' actions. Imitation then follows from inference over a shared model and from action that fulfils the predicted sensory consequences (see [[active_inference]], [[generative_model]]). The account explains why action observation and execution share representations, and it makes testable predictions about prediction-error signals. It is a theoretical proposal; empirical tests of its specific claims remain limited and competing accounts (associative learning, direct matching) account for much of the same data.
