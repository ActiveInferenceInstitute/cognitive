# Deictic Gesture

## Overview
A deictic gesture is a movement, most often pointing with the index finger or hand, that directs another person's attention to a thing, place, direction or person in the immediate or imagined surroundings. The word comes from deixis, the property of expressions such as "this", "there" and "you" whose reference depends on the context of utterance. Deictic gestures are contrasted with iconic gestures, which depict a shape or action, and with beat gestures that mark rhythm (McNeill, 1992; see [[iconic_gesture]]).

## Development
Infants begin to produce communicative pointing at about nine to fourteen months of age, around the time of other joint attention behaviours such as following another's gaze and showing objects (Carpenter, Nagell & Tomasello, 1998). Pointing typically precedes and predicts early vocabulary growth, and children combine pointing with words before they produce two-word utterances. Tomasello (2008) argues that pointing reflects a cooperative motive to share attention and information, and that it is a foundation for human linguistic communication. Whether pointing is uniquely human is debated, since great apes point in some settings, mainly to request.

## Use in communication
In adult speech, deictic gestures resolve ambiguity in expressions such as "put it there", coordinate reference to a visible object, and can also point to locations in abstract space, for example to indicate earlier and later events along a line. Pointing conventions vary across cultures, including the use of lips or the head in some communities (Kita, 2003). Comprehension of pointing depends on understanding the pointer's communicative intent, and it is affected in some neurodevelopmental conditions, where findings across individuals vary.

## Relation to Active Inference
Friston and Frith (2015) used communication between two agents to develop an Active Inference account of how shared generative models can arise through mutual prediction, with joint attention as an example of shared expectations. Pointing can be described as an action chosen to reduce another agent's uncertainty about a referent. This is a theoretical proposal, not an empirically established model of pointing.
