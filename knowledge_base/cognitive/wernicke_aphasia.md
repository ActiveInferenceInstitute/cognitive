# Wernicke Aphasia

## Overview
Wernicke aphasia, also called receptive or sensory aphasia, is a language disorder in which comprehension of spoken and written language is markedly impaired while speech remains fluent in rate, rhythm and intonation. It is named after Carl Wernicke, who described a cluster of symptoms associated with damage to the posterior part of the left superior temporal gyrus (Wernicke, 1874). It is classed as a fluent aphasia, in contrast to the non-fluent pattern of [[broca_aphasia]]. The description here is educational and not clinical guidance.

## Clinical features
- **Fluent but disordered output.** Speech is effortless and of normal length and prosody, but it contains paraphasias, which are substitutions of sounds (phonemic paraphasias) or of words (semantic paraphasias), and sometimes neologisms. In severe cases output becomes jargon with little recognisable content. Syntax may appear preserved on the surface while content is empty or incoherent.
- **Impaired comprehension.** Understanding of single words and sentences is reduced, and the degree varies with severity.
- **Poor repetition and naming.** Repetition is typically impaired, and word-finding difficulty is common.
- **Reduced awareness.** Some patients appear unaware of their errors or seem to monitor their speech poorly, which separates the disorder from the frustration often seen in non-fluent aphasia.

## Anatomy and course
The classic lesion involves the posterior superior and middle temporal gyri of the left hemisphere (see [[wernickes_area]]) and often extends into the inferior parietal region. A typical cause is stroke affecting the territory of the middle cerebral artery. Contemporary accounts hold that language comprehension relies on a distributed network and that the syndrome's severity and persistence depend on the extent of damage to temporal, parietal and white-matter connections (Benson and Ardila, 1996). Recovery varies widely and depends on the cause, extent of damage and rehabilitation.

## Relation to Active Inference
Friston and colleagues (2020) formulated linguistic communication as inference with hierarchical generative models, in which comprehension infers the intended message from speech. In such a picture, a comprehension deficit could correspond to degraded lexical or semantic levels, with fluent output arising from preserved lower-level sequence generation. This is a conceptual reading, and formal models have not been fitted to patients with this syndrome.
