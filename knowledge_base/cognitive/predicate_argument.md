# Predicate-Argument Structure

## Overview
Predicate-argument structure describes how a clause is organised around a predicate, usually a verb, which denotes an event or state, and its arguments, the participants required to complete that meaning. In "The dog chased the cat", chased is the predicate and the dog and the cat are its arguments; the difference between that sentence and "The cat chased the dog" is a difference in which argument fills which role. The idea derives from logic, where a predicate such as chase(x, y) takes ordered arguments (see [[predicate_logic]]), and is a common core of otherwise different linguistic theories.

## Argument number and realisation
Verbs differ in how many arguments they require: sleep takes one, chase takes two, give takes three. This valence constrains the syntactic frames the verb can occur in. Languages mark which argument is which by word order, case or agreement, and many verbs allow alternations, such as active and passive, or "load the wagon with hay" and "load hay onto the wagon", in which the same participants are realised differently (Levin & Rappaport Hovav, 2005). A distinction is drawn between arguments, which are required by the predicate, and adjuncts, such as times and places, which are optional, although the boundary is not always sharp.

## Roles of arguments
Theories differ in how to characterise the relation between predicate and argument. Fillmore (1968) proposed a set of case roles such as agent and instrument. Dowty (1991) argued that discrete role labels are unsatisfactory and that arguments are better characterised by clusters of entailments, forming prototypical Agent and Patient properties, which a verb's arguments possess to differing degrees. The appropriate inventory is still debated, and more detail appears under [[semantic_roles]] and [[thematic_roles]].

## Role in comprehension
Listeners appear to compute who did what to whom incrementally, and verbs make available expectations about upcoming arguments. Failure to assign roles correctly can produce misinterpretations in difficult sentences. Detailed neural claims are debated (see [[pars_triangularis]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
No established link exists. Event structure could be represented as a factorised generative model whose factors correspond to participants, but this is an analogy and not a developed theory.
