# Participatory Sense-Making

## Overview
Participatory sense-making is a concept proposed by De Jaegher and Di Paolo (2007) within the enactive approach to cognition. In enactivism, an organism generates meaning for itself by regulating its coupling with the environment, a process called sense-making. Participatory sense-making extends this to cases where two or more agents coordinate their activity, so that meaning emerges in the interaction and cannot be attributed to either participant alone.

## Main claims
The account begins from the observation that interaction has its own dynamics: the coordination of movement, speech and gesture between partners can be maintained, lost and repaired, and can shape what each partner does. Coordination varies in degree. Individual sense-making is modulated by the interaction, and in some cases the interaction itself takes on a degree of autonomy, in that it is sustained by the participants' mutual regulation. The authors present this as a challenge to the traditional focus on individual mind-reading. De Jaegher, Di Paolo and Gallagher (2010) argue that social interaction should be treated as a constitutive part of social cognition, not merely a context in which individual cognitive processes operate.

## Criticisms
The approach is contested. Critics argue that interaction dynamics do not replace the need for individual representation of others' mental states, and that the claim of a constitutive role is stronger than the evidence, which mostly shows that interaction influences cognition (Gallotti & Frith, 2013, offer a "we-mode" alternative that stresses a group-level perspective). Others question whether the notion of an autonomous interaction process can be given a precise empirical test. Defenders point to experimental paradigms, such as minimal perceptual crossing, in which pairs of participants coordinate without explicit representation of the other. The empirical status therefore remains open.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is a developing programme that connects enactivism with Active Inference, for example through the shared emphasis on autonomy, Markov blankets and the coupling of agent and environment (Kirchhoff et al., 2018). For interaction, Friston and Frith (2015) model two agents whose generative models of one another produce generalised synchrony. Whether this amounts to participatory sense-making, or merely analogises it, is itself debated.
