# Nutrient Cycles

## Overview
Nutrient cycles, also called biogeochemical cycles, describe how chemical elements essential to life move between organisms, soil, water and atmosphere. Unlike energy, which flows through an ecosystem and is eventually lost as heat, elements are conserved and recycled. The main cycles concern carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur and water, though every essential element has its own pathways.

## Processes
Within an ecosystem, plants and microbes take up nutrients in inorganic form and incorporate them into organic matter. Herbivores and predators pass them along the food web. Decomposers, mainly bacteria and fungi, break down dead material and release inorganic forms in a process of mineralisation, which makes nutrients available again. Several steps depend on specialised microbes. In the nitrogen cycle, nitrogen-fixing organisms convert atmospheric N2 to forms that plants can use, nitrifying microbes oxidise ammonium to nitrate, and denitrifying microbes return nitrogen to the atmosphere. Phosphorus has no significant gas phase and cycles mainly through rock weathering, soils, water and sediments, so its supply is often limiting. The growth of producers is often limited by whichever nutrient is scarcest relative to need.

## Feedbacks and human influence
Nutrient cycles contain feedbacks that can stabilise or amplify change, such as the effects of plant litter quality on decomposition and nutrient supply. Human activities have altered the cycles substantially. Burning fossil fuels and clearing land changes the carbon cycle (Falkowski et al., 2000), and industrial nitrogen fixation and fossil fuel combustion have greatly increased reactive nitrogen in the biosphere (Vitousek et al., 1997). Excess nutrients entering water bodies cause eutrophication. Restoration and management therefore often involve nutrient budgets.
