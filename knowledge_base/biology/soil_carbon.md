# Soil Carbon

## Overview
Soil carbon is the carbon stored in soils, mostly as soil organic matter, which comprises plant and microbial residues in various stages of decomposition, with a smaller inorganic fraction as carbonates in some regions. Soils are among the largest terrestrial carbon pools, holding more carbon than vegetation and the atmosphere combined, and small proportional changes in the pool are therefore relevant to atmospheric carbon dioxide (Lal, 2004).

## Dynamics
Carbon enters soil through plant litter, roots and root exudates, and through microbial biomass. It leaves mainly as carbon dioxide from decomposer respiration, and through erosion and leaching. The balance depends on climate, vegetation, soil texture and mineralogy, moisture and management.

Older accounts described persistence as a property of "recalcitrant" molecules. Schmidt et al. (2011) argued instead that persistence of organic matter is largely a property of the ecosystem: carbon survives when physical protection within aggregates, association with mineral surfaces, unfavourable conditions for decomposers, or spatial separation from them limit decomposition. This view shifts attention from the chemistry of the material to the conditions around it. The turnover time of soil carbon spans a very wide range, from months for fresh litter to centuries or longer for mineral-associated forms.

## Management
Practices that influence soil carbon include tillage, residue return, cover crops, grazing management, restoration of vegetation and drainage of wetlands. Claims for large-scale climate mitigation through soil carbon sequestration are debated, since gains can saturate, can be reversed by later changes in management or climate, and are hard to measure at field scale.
