# Serotonin

## Overview
Serotonin, or 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), is a monoamine that acts as a neurotransmitter and neuromodulator in the brain and as a signalling molecule in the gut, blood and other tissues. The large majority of the body's serotonin is found in the gut, whereas the brain's supply is made by a small population of neurons. This page concerns its central nervous system role.

## Mechanism
Serotonin is synthesized from the amino acid tryptophan, which is converted by tryptophan hydroxylase to 5-hydroxytryptophan and then to 5-HT. Neurons that release it are concentrated in the raphe nuclei of the brainstem, and their axons project to nearly every forebrain region, including cortex, [[amygdala]], hippocampus and striatum. Release is terminated mainly by reuptake through the serotonin transporter. Mammals have at least fourteen receptor subtypes in seven families (5-HT1 to 5-HT7); all but 5-HT3, which is a ligand-gated ion channel, are G-protein-coupled. Different subtypes can have opposite cellular effects, so the net action of serotonin depends on the receptors present in each circuit.

Proposed functions are varied and are not captured by a single label such as "mood chemical." Candidates include modulation of aversive processing and behavioral inhibition, regulation of patience and waiting for delayed reward (optogenetic activation of dorsal raphe serotonin neurons increased waiting for reward in mice, Miyazaki and colleagues 2014), sleep and arousal regulation, and plasticity during development. Dayan and Huys proposed that serotonin supports control over the impact of aversive predictions. The long-standing claim that depression results from low serotonin is disputed, and the relationship between serotonin and mood disorders remains an area of active research; this page makes no clinical claims.

## Role in Active Inference
Two hypotheses connect serotonin to Active Inference. One relates serotonin to the precision or weighting of beliefs about the future and to [[temporal_discounting]]. The other, from Carhart-Harris and colleagues, links the 5-HT2A receptor, a target of classic psychedelics, to relaxation of high-level priors, giving more weight to sensory evidence ([[precision_weighting]], [[prior_beliefs]]). Both are proposals that guide experiments and are not established findings; the evidence is mixed, particularly for the role of different receptor subtypes.
