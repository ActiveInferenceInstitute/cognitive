# Ecosystem Engineering

## Overview
Ecosystem engineers are organisms that directly or indirectly control the availability of resources to other species by causing physical state changes in biotic or abiotic materials. The concept was formalised by Jones, Lawton and Shachak in 1994. It differs from ordinary trophic interactions such as predation in that the effect is mediated by changes to the physical environment rather than by consumption.

## Types
Jones and colleagues distinguished two kinds. Autogenic engineers change the environment through their own physical structures, as trees do by creating shade and microclimate, or corals do by building reefs. Allogenic engineers transform materials from one state into another through their activity, as beavers do when they cut wood and build dams that flood land and create wetlands, as earthworms do when they mix and aerate soil, and as burrowing animals do when they create shelters used by other species. Many organisms are both. Humans are sometimes treated as ecosystem engineers on a very large scale.

## Effects
Engineering can raise or lower species richness, depending on the habitat. Jones and colleagues (1997) argued that engineers can add habitat heterogeneity, so that their effect on species richness across a landscape is likely to be positive in many cases, while effects within a modified patch can be positive or negative and depend on the kind, abundance and persistence of the engineers and their structures. Effects persist after the organism has gone, so engineered structures can remain as records of earlier activity in a landscape. Engineering also changes selection pressures on the engineers and on other species, which connects the concept to evolutionary theory of [[niche_construction]].

## Relation to Active Inference
Ecosystem engineering is a direct ecological example of organisms acting on their environment so that future sensory states are changed. Active Inference formulations of niche construction treat the environment as shaped by the actions of agents whose generative models are shaped in turn by the environment, which gives a formal language for this loop. The engineering literature itself is empirical ecology; the Active Inference reading is a theoretical extension.
