# Alarm Pheromones

## Overview
Alarm pheromones are chemicals released by an animal in response to danger that change the behaviour of nearby members of its species. They occur in many insects, fish and some mammals, and are especially prominent in social insects, where a threat to the nest requires rapid collective response. Wilson and Bossert (1963) classified alarm substances among the main kinds of chemical signal and noted that they should be volatile enough to spread quickly and to fade quickly.

## Examples and properties
- **Honeybees.** The sting apparatus releases a mixture whose best-known component is isopentyl acetate, which attracts guards and provokes stinging. Mandibular gland secretions also contribute to alarm responses.
- **Ants.** Alarm secretions from the mandibular, Dufour's or poison glands may include ketones such as 4-methyl-3-heptanone and, in formicine ants, formic acid, depending on species. Responses range from attraction and aggression to dispersal and nest evacuation (Hölldobler & Wilson, 1990).
- **Aphids.** (E)-beta-farnesene released from the cornicles when aphids are attacked causes nearby aphids to stop feeding and drop or move away.
- **Signal design.** Compounds are small and volatile, effects are short-lived, and the active space expands quickly and then collapses. This contrasts with persistent chemical marks such as [[trail_pheromones]].
- **Context.** The same compound can cause attraction at low concentration and aggression or flight at high concentration, and responses depend on colony state and caste.
- **Exploitation.** Predators, parasites and other species may eavesdrop on alarm signals (see [[chemical_ecology]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is analogical. A colony can be modelled as a collective inferring its environment through signals that change members' sensory evidence, with alarm pheromone acting as an evidence channel about threat and a trigger for coordinated action (see [[active_inference_social_insects]]). This is a modelling perspective and not an empirical finding of the free energy principle. The chemistry and behaviour above are established independently.
