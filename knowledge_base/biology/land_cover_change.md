# Land Cover Change

## Overview
Land cover is the physical material at the surface of the Earth, such as forest, grassland, cropland, water, bare ground or built surfaces. Land use is the human purpose to which land is put, such as grazing, logging or housing. The two are related but distinct: one land cover can serve several uses, and one use can occur on several covers. Land cover change is the conversion of one cover type to another (for example, forest to cropland) or the modification of a cover type within a class (for example, forest degradation).

## Drivers
Research in land change science has emphasised that proximate causes, such as agricultural expansion, wood extraction and infrastructure, are driven by underlying factors including demography, economics, technology, policy and culture. Lambin and colleagues argued that simple single-cause explanations such as population growth alone are not supported, and that the pathways differ among regions. Natural processes, including fire, storms, drought and succession, also change cover.

## Detection
Satellite remote sensing allows repeated, consistent mapping of land cover over large areas. Methods classify pixels by spectral signature, compare images over time to detect change, and combine optical, radar and other data. Global products have quantified forest loss and gain at fine spatial resolution (Hansen et al., 2013). Field data and high-resolution imagery are needed to check accuracy, and the definitions used, such as what counts as forest, affect the results.

## Consequences
Land cover change alters habitat and often fragments it, changes the carbon stored in vegetation and soil, modifies local and regional water cycles and surface reflectance, and affects the ecosystem services on which people depend (Foley et al., 2005). Conversion can bring benefits such as food production alongside these costs, so assessments consider trade-offs.
