# Sprouting

## Overview
In neuroscience, sprouting is the formation of new branches from an existing axon or dendrite. The word covers growth during development, when arbors are elaborated, and growth in the mature nervous system, where it is a form of structural plasticity. It differs from regeneration, in which a severed axon regrows along its original course, although the two share cellular machinery. In plants the word refers to germination, which is a separate topic.

## Kinds of sprouting
- **Collateral sprouting.** Intact axons send out new branches into territory vacated by a damaged neighbour. Raisman (1969) described the formation of new synapses by surviving fibres in the septal nuclei of the adult rat after partial deafferentation, a classic demonstration that adult central circuits can reorganise their connections.
- **Regenerative sprouting.** Cut axons form new growth cones at the lesion. Success in the peripheral nervous system contrasts with the limited regrowth in the adult central nervous system, where inhibitory molecules, glial scarring and reduced intrinsic growth programmes all contribute.
- **Experience-dependent sprouting.** Dendritic spines and branches can be gained and lost with experience, and imaging studies in living animals show turnover of some structures even in adulthood (Kandel et al., 2013).
- **Pathological sprouting.** Branching may be adaptive or maladaptive. Mossy fibre sprouting in the hippocampus after seizures is one example whose functional role is still discussed.

## Mechanisms
Sprouting depends on cytoskeletal remodelling, growth-associated gene expression, neurotrophic factors and cues of the kind studied in [[axon_guidance]]. Neural activity, calcium signalling and the stability of existing synapses influence whether new branches persist (see [[calcium_signaling]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is by analogy at the level of model structure. Active Inference treats learning as change in parameters, and some accounts extend this to structure learning, in which model complexity is adjusted to evidence; growth and pruning of connections are a natural biological counterpart. This correspondence is a suggestion, not an established derivation.
