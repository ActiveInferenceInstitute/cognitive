# Monoamines

## Overview
Monoamines are small signalling molecules that each contain a single amine group joined to an aromatic ring by a two-carbon chain. They act as neurotransmitters and neuromodulators in the brain and as hormones and local signals elsewhere in the body. The main brain monoamines are the catecholamines dopamine, noradrenaline (norepinephrine) and adrenaline (epinephrine), and the indoleamine serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine). Histamine, an imidazole amine, is often included in the group. Unlike fast transmitters such as glutamate, they are released by comparatively small neuron populations whose axons spread across wide areas, and they mostly change the responsiveness of target circuits instead of directly driving them.

## Synthesis, storage and clearance
- **Catecholamines** are made from the amino acid tyrosine. Tyrosine hydroxylase, the rate-limiting enzyme, produces L-DOPA, which is converted to dopamine. Dopamine beta-hydroxylase converts dopamine to noradrenaline, and in some cells an additional enzyme converts noradrenaline to adrenaline.
- **Serotonin** is made from the amino acid tryptophan by tryptophan hydroxylase followed by an aromatic amino acid decarboxylase.
- **Storage.** A vesicular monoamine transporter packs the amines into synaptic vesicles for release.
- **Clearance.** After release, plasma membrane transporters for dopamine, noradrenaline and serotonin take the amines back up. Intracellular monoamine oxidase breaks them down, and catechol-O-methyltransferase acts on catecholamines (Eisenhofer, Kopin and Goldstein, 2004).

## Receptors
Most monoamine receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors, so they act through intracellular second-messenger cascades and are slower in onset than ligand-gated channels. Examples are the dopamine receptor families D1 to D5, the adrenergic receptors, and most of the serotonin receptor families. An exception is the serotonin 5-HT3 receptor, which is a ligand-gated ion channel. The diversity of receptors means that one amine can excite some targets and inhibit others.

## Anatomy
Each system originates in a small number of nuclei and projects widely. Dopamine neurons lie mainly in the substantia nigra and the ventral tegmental area, noradrenaline neurons in the locus coeruleus (see [[locus_coeruleus]]), and serotonin neurons in the raphe nuclei of the brainstem. Histamine neurons lie in the hypothalamus. Because the systems innervate cortex, striatum, thalamus and limbic structures together, they are positioned to regulate the state of large parts of the brain. Their involvement is discussed in terms of arousal, reward, mood and attention, and many drugs act by changing monoamine release, reuptake or breakdown.

## Relation to Active Inference
In Active Inference, some monoamines have been proposed to encode the precision of particular beliefs. A prominent proposal links dopamine to the precision of beliefs about policies (Friston et al., 2014), and other work relates cholinergic modulation to the precision of sensory prediction errors (Moran et al., 2013). Similar suggestions exist for noradrenaline and serotonin (see [[precision_weighting]] and [[neuromodulation]]). These are hypotheses that organise findings and generate predictions. They are not settled accounts of what the amines do, and the real systems have many more functions and receptor-specific effects than a single precision role would suggest.
