# Symbiosis

## Overview
Symbiosis, a term introduced by Anton de Bary in 1879, means the living together of unlike organisms in a close and prolonged association. The broad definition includes interactions that benefit both partners, interactions that benefit one without affecting the other, and interactions that harm one partner. Some authors restrict the word to mutually beneficial partnerships. The partners are often called host and symbiont.

## Kinds and examples
- **Mutualism.** Both partners gain. Lichens combine a fungus with an alga or cyanobacterium, mycorrhizal fungi exchange soil nutrients for plant sugars, and gut microbes help animals digest food and shape immune function. Leaf-cutting ants cultivate a fungus as food, a case of farming mutualism between insects and microbes.
- **Commensalism.** One partner benefits and the other is unaffected, although in practice small costs and benefits are hard to rule out.
- **Parasitism.** One partner benefits at the cost of the other, and some classifications treat it as a symbiosis and some do not.
- **Obligate and facultative.** Partners may be unable to live independently or may do so under some conditions. Aphids and their bacterial endosymbiont Buchnera are an obligate association.

## Endosymbiosis and evolution
Mitochondria and chloroplasts are widely accepted to derive from bacteria taken up by ancestral host cells. Sagan (1967) set out this serial endosymbiosis theory, which is supported by the organelles' own genomes and bacterial-type ribosomes. Symbioses of this kind show that new levels of biological individuality can arise from cooperation, and they are part of the debate over how host and symbiont evolve together (Douglas, 2010). Interactions are often context-dependent and may shift between mutualism and parasitism.
