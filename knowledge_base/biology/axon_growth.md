# Axon Growth

## Overview
Axon growth is the extension of a neuron's long output process from the cell body toward its targets. It begins after a neuron has become postmitotic and polarised, so that one neurite is specified as the axon. Growth is carried out by the growth cone, a motile, fan-shaped structure at the axon tip that was first described by Ramón y Cajal.

## Cellular mechanism
The growth cone contains finger-like filopodia and sheet-like lamellipodia built from actin filaments. Actin polymerises at the leading edge and is moved rearward by myosin-driven flow. Adhesion to the substrate through cell-surface receptors couples this activity to forward movement. Behind the leading edge, microtubules advance into the growth cone and are consolidated into the axon shaft, allowing the tip to move forward while the axon lengthens. Extracellular signals such as neurotrophins, laminin and other matrix components influence the rate and persistence of growth through intracellular signalling that regulates the cytoskeleton. Branching, including collateral branches formed along the shaft, lets one neuron reach several targets.

## Development and regeneration
In the developing brain, axons grow along defined routes, form synapses and are then refined by activity-dependent competition and pruning. Axon growth therefore underlies the long-range connectivity on which later cognitive function depends. In the adult mammalian central nervous system, regenerative growth after injury is limited, in part because of inhibitory molecules in the environment and a reduced intrinsic growth programme, whereas peripheral nerves regenerate more readily.

## Relation to Active Inference
Axon growth is a developmental process and has no direct treatment in Active Inference. Free-energy accounts of development and morphogenesis exist, and activity-dependent refinement can be read as learning of connection strengths that make the network better at predicting its inputs (see [[hebbian_learning]]). These are interpretive links; the guidance and growth machinery itself is described by molecular and cell biology.
