# Amygdala

## Overview
The amygdala is a bilateral group of nuclei in the anterior medial temporal lobe. It is best known for linking sensory events to emotional significance, especially threat, but it is not a dedicated "fear centre": it also responds to reward-predicting, novel, ambiguous and socially relevant stimuli. Here the term is used in its neuroscience sense, as a brain structure rather than the almond-shaped object its name denotes.

## Structure and function
Three subdivisions are usually distinguished. The basolateral complex (lateral, basal and accessory basal nuclei) receives convergent cortical and thalamic sensory input and is where associations between a neutral cue and an aversive or appetitive outcome are formed. The central nucleus is the main output stage, projecting to hypothalamus and brainstem to drive freezing, autonomic arousal and activation of the stress axis (see [[cortisol]]). The medial and cortical nuclei handle olfactory and social-chemical signals.

In Pavlovian fear conditioning, plasticity of cue inputs in the lateral nucleus, of the kind described under [[ltp]], is a leading mechanistic account of how a tone comes to predict a shock. The basolateral amygdala also modulates consolidation in the hippocampus and elsewhere: arousing events are remembered better, partly through noradrenergic and glucocorticoid signalling acting on this structure. Reciprocal connections with [[medial_prefrontal]] and [[anterior_cingulate]] cortex allow top-down regulation and extinction learning, a topic treated in [[emotion_regulation]].

Human lesion and imaging studies support a broader role in assigning salience and in evaluating faces and social cues, though effect sizes and the specificity of activation to fear versus general arousal remain debated.

## Role in Active Inference
The link is interpretive rather than derived. In affective formulations of Active Inference, emotional states are inferences about the causes of [[interoception]] signals and about expected changes in the body's regulated variables. On that reading the amygdala is a candidate substrate for estimating the salience of a stimulus and thereby adjusting the precision given to prediction errors in downstream autonomic and attentional circuits ([[precision_weighting]]). This is a hypothesis under active research, and the amygdala is one node among many in that proposal.
