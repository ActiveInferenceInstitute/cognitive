# Ecosystem Processes

## Overview
Ecosystem processes are the fluxes and transformations of energy and materials that connect organisms and their physical environment. They are studied by ecosystem ecology, which treats a system as compartments (producers, consumers, decomposers, soil, water) linked by flows, and which complements the species-centred view of [[community_ecology]].

## Principal processes
Primary production is the capture of energy, mostly by photosynthesis. Gross primary production (GPP) is total carbon fixed; net primary production (NPP) is GPP minus plant respiration and is the energy available to the rest of the food web. Energy then moves through trophic levels with large losses at each step to respiration and heat. A transfer efficiency of about 10% per level is a common rule of thumb, although measured values vary widely among ecosystems and between plant-based and detritus-based chains. Energy is not recycled; it enters as light and leaves as heat.

Matter, by contrast, cycles. Carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus move between living biomass, soil organic matter, water and atmosphere. Decomposition by bacteria, fungi and soil fauna mineralizes dead tissue, returning nutrients in forms plants can use, and its rate depends on temperature, moisture and litter quality. Nitrogen fixation, nitrification and denitrification regulate nitrogen availability; phosphorus has a mainly geological cycle with no major gaseous phase. Disturbances such as fire, drought and herbivore outbreaks change process rates.

Organisms modify these rates. Ants and termites turn over and aerate soil, redistribute nutrients to nest sites and alter decomposition, an example of ecosystem engineering reviewed in [[ecological_impacts_social_insects]]. Biodiversity affects process rates and stability ([[biodiversity]]), and the downstream benefits to people are catalogued as [[ecosystem_services]].

## Role in Active Inference
The relation is mostly an analogy at present. A living ecosystem is an open system maintained far from thermodynamic equilibrium by continuous flux, a property that the free-energy principle formalizes as a non-equilibrium steady state ([[non_equilibrium_steady_state]]). Applying Active Inference to whole ecosystems, rather than to individual organisms, is an active research direction and remains contested; see [[ecological_active_inference]]. The standard ecosystem models above do not themselves depend on it.
