# Seed Dispersal

## Overview
Seed dispersal is the transport of seeds away from the parent plant. It matters because seedlings that germinate beneath the parent face competition and enemies such as pathogens and seed predators, and because dispersal lets populations colonise new sites. Howe and Smallwood (1982) reviewed the main hypotheses for its benefits, including escape from density-dependent mortality, colonisation of favourable sites and directed dispersal to safe microhabitats.

## Modes of dispersal
Seeds move by wind (anemochory), water (hydrochory), ballistic ejection, gravity, and animals. Animal dispersal includes ingestion of fleshy fruits and later defecation, attachment of burrs to fur, and scatter-hoarding by rodents and birds, which sometimes fails to retrieve all caches.

## Dispersal by ants
Myrmecochory is dispersal by ants. Seeds of many plants bear an elaiosome, a lipid-rich appendage that ants collect. Workers carry the seed to the nest, feed the elaiosome to larvae and discard the intact seed, often in a nutrient-enriched refuse area or underground (Beattie, 1985). Proposed advantages for the plant include dispersal over short distances, protection from seed predators and fire, and enriched germination sites. The interaction is usually diffuse rather than a tight partnership: many plants rely on several ant species, and some ant species are more effective than others. Because the ants' behaviour is governed by simple rules of foraging and recruitment (see [[collective_behavior_ants]]), the quality of dispersal depends on colony traits as well as on seed traits.

## Relation to Active Inference
The vault mentions seed dispersal as an ecological outcome of ant collective behaviour. There is no Active Inference derivation; ant foraging and recruitment have been modelled with Bayesian and free-energy ideas, but those models do not treat dispersal as a target. The topic is better viewed as a case of how local decision rules in a colony produce consequences at the scale of plant populations and communities (see [[ecological_networks]]).
