# Mutual Aid

## Overview
Mutual aid is the practice of partners helping each other, so that each gains from the others' help. As a biological concept it is associated with the Russian naturalist and anarchist thinker Peter Kropotkin, who in Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution (1902) argued that cooperation within species is as important in evolution as competition. He wrote partly in reply to T. H. Huxley's essay on the struggle for existence, which stressed competition. Kropotkin drew examples from insects, birds, mammals and human societies. His argument was also a political one, and this page records his biological thesis and does not take a position on the politics.

## Modern explanations
Evolutionary biology has since developed formal accounts of when helping behaviour can evolve. Hamilton's theory of inclusive fitness (1964) explains help directed to relatives, because relatives share genes. Trivers (1971) described reciprocal altruism, in which helping can evolve between unrelated individuals if help is later returned. Axelrod and Hamilton (1981) showed with repeated Prisoner's Dilemma games that reciprocating strategies can be successful. Mutualism, in which partners of different species exchange benefits, is a third route, and group-level selection is another mechanism, whose importance is debated. Nowak (2006) summarised five mechanisms for the evolution of cooperation: kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity and group selection. Cooperative breeding and the eusociality of ants, bees and termites are among the best-known cases.

## Interpretation
Biologists commonly distinguish helping that gives a net benefit to the actor from altruism that carries a cost, and they note that cooperation is vulnerable to free riders unless some mechanism stabilises it. Whether Kropotkin's emphasis or the competitive emphasis better describes nature depends on the system, and most modern accounts treat both as present.

## Relation to Active Inference
Active Inference models of cooperation treat agents as inferring one another's goals and sharing generative models, so that mutual aid can be expressed as joint inference. That is a framework for modelling and does not bear on the historical argument above.
