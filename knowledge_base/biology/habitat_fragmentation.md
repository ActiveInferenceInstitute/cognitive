# Habitat Fragmentation

## Overview
Habitat fragmentation is the process by which a continuous area of habitat is divided into smaller, more isolated patches, usually by land conversion for agriculture, roads or development. It is both a landscape pattern and a process, and it is one of the drivers of [[biodiversity]] decline considered in [[conservation_biology]].

## Components and consequences
Fragmentation involves several linked changes: a reduction in total habitat area, smaller patch size, greater distance between patches, and a larger proportion of habitat near edges. Edge effects alter microclimate, predation and invasion pressure within a band along patch boundaries. Small, isolated populations face higher extinction risk from demographic and environmental fluctuation and from loss of genetic diversity through drift and inbreeding ([[population_genetics]]).

Theory supplies two main tools. Island biogeography (MacArthur and Wilson) treats a patch as an island whose species number balances immigration and extinction, and predicts a species-area relationship of the form $S = cA^z$, with $z$ commonly reported in roughly the 0.2 to 0.35 range, so that a 90% loss of area is expected to eliminate on the order of 40% of species at $z = 0.25$ in the long run. Metapopulation theory (Levins) treats a set of patches as a system of local populations that go extinct and are recolonized, so persistence depends on patch number and connectivity. Corridors and stepping-stone patches are management responses.

A methodological point is contested: Fahrig argued that the effect of fragmentation per se (the spatial arrangement of a fixed amount of habitat) is generally weaker than, and should be separated from, the effect of habitat loss, and this has been disputed by other authors. For social insects, fragmentation affects colony founding and dispersal by winged queens, and ant-plant and ant-ant interactions in remnants ([[conservation_biology_social_insects]]).
