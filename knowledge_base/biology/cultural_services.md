# Cultural Services

## Overview
Cultural services are the non-material benefits people derive from ecosystems. The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005) listed them as one of four categories of ecosystem services, alongside provisioning, regulating and supporting services. They include recreation and ecotourism, aesthetic enjoyment, spiritual and religious significance, cultural heritage, sense of place, inspiration, and educational and scientific value.

## Characteristics
Cultural services differ from other categories in several ways. They depend on human perception, values and practice, so the same landscape may be valued differently by different groups. They are often intangible and bound up with identity, and they resist both measurement and monetary valuation. Many are relational: they arise from interaction between people and places, not from the ecosystem alone. Some of the services, such as access to nature, depend on the condition of the ecosystem, which links them to biodiversity and habitat quality, while others, such as heritage, depend mostly on continuity of use.

## Assessment
Approaches include surveys and interviews, participatory mapping of valued places, analysis of visitation data and social media, and deliberative methods that avoid reducing values to money. Critics have argued that treating culture as a service risks omitting social and relational values, and the literature debates how to include them (Chan et al., 2012; Daniel et al., 2012). Cultural services are also central to the practices of Indigenous and local communities, whose knowledge of land is discussed under [[traditional_ecological_knowledge]]. The page presents these views as positions in a continuing debate and does not favour one.
