# Ecosystem Services

## Overview
Ecosystem services are the benefits that people obtain from ecosystems. The concept frames biodiversity and ecological processes in terms of their contribution to human well-being, which makes them comparable with other inputs in planning, policy, and accounting.

## Mechanism
The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005) grouped services into four categories. Provisioning services are products such as food, fresh water, timber, and fiber. Regulating services are benefits from regulation of ecosystem processes, including climate regulation through carbon storage, flood and erosion control, water purification, disease regulation, and crop pollination. Cultural services are non-material benefits such as recreation, aesthetic value, and spiritual significance. Supporting services, including nutrient cycling, soil formation, and primary production, underlie all the others. Later frameworks refine this: the Common International Classification of Ecosystem Services avoids double counting by separating final services from supporting processes, and the IPBES framework (Diaz and colleagues, 2018) speaks of nature's contributions to people, which includes cultural and relational values that are not captured by market valuation.

Valuation methods include market prices, avoided-cost and replacement-cost estimates, and stated-preference surveys. Costanza and colleagues (1997) produced an influential global valuation, and also drew sustained criticism that aggregate monetary values hide dependence on threshold effects and cannot be summed meaningfully for services that are non-substitutable. Services depend on ecosystem structure and function: pollination, for example, depends on the community of pollinators and on landscape configuration, and so declines when habitat fragments. Because ecosystems can have alternative stable states, services may degrade abruptly past a threshold rather than gradually.

## Role in Active Inference
There is no direct derivation of ecosystem services from the free-energy principle. The relevant connection is to socio-ecological governance: a management body is an agent that maintains a model of an ecosystem, monitors indicators, and acts to keep service flows within a desired range. Describing monitoring and adaptive management as belief updating and preference-driven action is a useful vocabulary, particularly in the adaptive co-management setting, but it adds an interpretive layer rather than new ecological content.
