# Chemical Communication

## Overview
Chemical communication is the transfer of information between organisms by means of chemical compounds that a sender emits and a receiver detects. It is probably the oldest communication channel, used by bacteria, plants, fungi and animals. In animals, chemicals that carry signals between members of the same species were named pheromones by Karlson and Lüscher (1959). Compounds that benefit only the receiver or only the sender across species are called kairomones and allomones.

## Signal types and transmission
Pheromones are often classified by effect. Releaser pheromones trigger a rapid behavioural response, such as alarm, attraction of mates or trail following. Primer pheromones act more slowly and alter physiology or development, for example the effect of queen pheromones on worker reproduction. Signals may be airborne and volatile, or they may be deposited on surfaces and be detected on contact or at close range (see [[cuticular_hydrocarbons]]).

Physical properties shape their use. Volatile molecules travel in turbulent plumes that give intermittent and noisy concentration cues, and their range is set by diffusion, airflow and degradation. Chemical signals persist after the sender leaves and can be left as marks, but they are slow to switch on and off, so they carry less detailed information per unit time than visual or acoustic signals. The first pheromone chemically identified was bombykol, the silk moth sex attractant, characterised by Butenandt and colleagues in 1959.

## Reception
Receptors on chemosensory sensilla, concentrated on the antennae in insects, bind odorant molecules and convert them into neural activity (see [[antennation]]). A given blend is usually decoded by combinations of receptors, and the response depends on concentration, context and the state of the receiver.

## Relation to Active Inference
In social insects, chemical signals set up shared external states such as trails and colony odours that alter the sensory input of other agents (see [[trail_pheromones]], [[stigmergic_coordination]]). Describing these systems as agents updating beliefs from noisy chemical evidence is a natural modelling choice, but the connection is an analogy and not a developed theory.
