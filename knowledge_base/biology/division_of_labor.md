# Division of Labor

## Overview
Division of labor is the partitioning of colony work among individuals, so that different workers specialize on different tasks at a given time. It operates at two levels: reproductive division, where one or a few queens lay eggs and workers are largely sterile, and worker division, where non-reproductive individuals divide brood care, nest maintenance, defense and foraging. It is considered a defining feature of eusociality ([[evolution_of_eusociality]]).

## Forms and mechanisms
Three organizing patterns are described. Morphological castes involve physical differences between workers: leafcutter ants (*Atta*) and *Pheidole* have large-headed soldiers and small workers with different jobs. Age or temporal polyethism assigns tasks by age: honeybee workers typically progress from cell cleaning and brood care to food handling and finally foraging, although the schedule is flexible and responds to colony needs ([[bee_colony_organization]]). Task specialization without fixed castes arises among nominally similar workers because of individual differences in sensitivity and experience.

For the third pattern, the leading explanation is the fixed [[response_thresholds]] model: each worker has a threshold for each task stimulus, and a worker with a lower threshold engages sooner. When some workers are removed, stimulus rises and others with higher thresholds join, giving flexible task reallocation without central control. Extensions add self-reinforcement (successful performance lowers a worker's threshold), social inhibition, spatial fidelity within the nest, and learning. Whether threshold models fully account for observed specialization is still tested empirically, and alternatives emphasize network position and information flow.

Adaptive benefits proposed include reduced task-switching costs, learning-curve gains from repetition, and spatial efficiency. These effects are not universal, and some colonies show weak or inconsistent specialization.

## Role in Active Inference
Colony-level task allocation can be modelled as distributed inference, where each worker acts on local cues to reduce uncertainty about colony needs; [[active_inference_social_insects]] explores this framing. The mapping is a modelling hypothesis rather than a result established by experiment, and standard threshold models make no use of free-energy minimization.
