# Morphogenetic Fields

## Overview
In developmental biology a morphogenetic field is a region of an embryo whose cells are specified to form a particular structure, such as a limb or an eye, and whose development is coordinated as a unit. The idea arose in early twentieth-century experimental embryology, where grafting and excision experiments on amphibian embryos showed that a field could regulate: after part of it was removed, the remainder often still produced a complete structure. Gilbert, Opitz and Raff (1996) argued that the concept remains useful for linking development to evolution.

## Mechanisms
Modern work gives the term a mechanistic reading. Spatial patterns arise from diffusible signals called morphogens that form concentration gradients, which cells interpret to adopt positional identities (Wolpert, 1969). Reaction-diffusion systems, mechanical forces, and cell-cell signalling networks also contribute (see [[pattern_formation]] and [[morphogenesis]]). Some researchers, notably Levin, also emphasise bioelectric patterns across tissues as an additional layer of spatial control in regeneration and development. This claim is an active research area, and the extent to which bioelectric states instruct large-scale form is still being worked out.

## Contested usage
The phrase is also used for morphic fields, a hypothesis of formative causation proposed by Rupert Sheldrake (1981) in which organisms are shaped by a non-material, inherited memory acting across space and time through morphic resonance. This proposal is distinct from the embryological concept. Sheldrake maintains that it is testable and has reported experiments that he takes to support it, but the hypothesis is not part of accepted developmental biology and its claimed supporting results have not been accepted by the wider scientific community. Critics rejected it from the start: an editorial in Nature (Maddox, 1981), titled "A book for burning?", argued that Sheldrake's book was unscientific. Readers should treat the two senses as separate.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an active research programme rather than an established result. Friston and colleagues (2015) modelled pattern regulation as cells minimising variational free energy while inferring their position within a target morphology. In their simulations a collective of cells that carries a generative model of the whole form can regenerate after damage, giving a Bayesian reading of the regulative behaviour seen in classical field experiments. The approach is a theoretical model, not a demonstration that real embryos implement such inference.
