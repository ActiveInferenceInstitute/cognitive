# Climate Change Ecology

## Overview
Climate change ecology studies how changes in temperature, precipitation, atmospheric carbon dioxide, and extreme events alter the physiology, distribution, timing, and interactions of organisms. It combines physiological ecology, biogeography, and community ecology with climate data.

## Mechanism
Organisms respond to warming in three broad ways: they shift their ranges, change their timing, or adapt through plasticity and evolution; those that do none of these decline. Parmesan and Yohe (2003) found consistent poleward and upslope range shifts and advancing spring events across a wide set of taxa. Range shifts are limited by dispersal ability, habitat fragmentation, and the availability of suitable microclimates. Phenological shifts (earlier flowering, emergence, and migration) occur at different rates in different species, so interactions can be decoupled, as when an insect emerges before or after the plants or birds it depends on; this is called phenological mismatch.

Physiological limits matter because ectotherms, including insects, have performance curves with an optimum and upper critical limits. Temperature also changes colony-level traits in social insects, such as foraging time windows in ants, brood development, and nest microclimate regulation. Ocean warming and acidification affect calcifying organisms and coral symbiosis. Species distribution models correlate occurrence with climate variables to project future ranges, but they do not by themselves represent dispersal, biotic interactions, or evolution, so projections need to be treated as scenarios.

## Role in Active Inference
In the free-energy view an organism persists by keeping its states inside a viable range, and climate change shifts the statistics of the environment so that previously well-adapted models become poor predictors. This gives a natural vocabulary for thinking about plasticity as fast belief updating and evolutionary adaptation as slow change of the prior structure. It is an interpretive frame for already-known phenomena; the page does not claim that Active Inference makes novel ecological predictions here. In practice the repository's closest connection is through simulations in which the environment parameters (food availability, temperature-dependent activity) are varied to test the robustness of agent behavior.
