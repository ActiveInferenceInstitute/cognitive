# Cell Migration

## Overview
Cell migration is the directed movement of cells from one location to another. It is essential in embryonic development, immune surveillance and wound healing, and it is hijacked in cancer metastasis. In the developing brain, newly formed neurons must travel from their birthplace to their final positions before they can form working circuits.

## Mechanism
Migrating cells commonly cycle through four coupled steps. The cell extends a protrusion at its front, driven by actin polymerisation. The protrusion forms adhesions to the surrounding substrate, often through integrin receptors. Contractile forces generated by myosin II move the cell body forward, and adhesions at the rear are released. Small GTPases of the Rho family regulate these steps, with Rac and Cdc42 promoting protrusion and Rho promoting contractility. Cells migrate by different modes, from adhesive mesenchymal movement to faster, less adhesive amoeboid movement, and they follow guidance signals such as soluble chemical gradients (chemotaxis), substrate-bound gradients and tissue stiffness.

## Neuronal migration
In the developing cerebral cortex, many excitatory neurons arise in the ventricular zone and migrate radially along radial glial fibres to the cortical plate. Later-born neurons pass earlier ones, producing an inside-out layering in which deeper layers form first. Inhibitory interneurons in many species arise elsewhere in the forebrain and reach the cortex by tangential migration. Disrupted migration is associated with malformations of cortical development, such as lissencephaly, in which the folds of the cortex are reduced.

## Relation to Active Inference
Cell migration is a developmental process whose standard explanation is molecular and mechanical. Chemotaxis can be framed as gradient following, and the Free Energy Principle has been used to describe how cells and tissues self-organise, but the link to migration is an analogy and a modelling choice, not a derived result. The relevance to cognition is indirect: correct positioning is a precondition for the circuits that later implement perception and learning.
