# Quantum Biology

## Overview
Quantum biology asks whether non-trivial quantum effects contribute to the function of living systems, beyond the quantum mechanics that already underlies all of chemistry. Lambert et al. (2013) survey the field and identify candidate processes in photosynthesis, enzyme catalysis and magnetoreception. The central difficulty is that biological matter is warm, wet and noisy, conditions in which coherence between quantum states is expected to be lost quickly (see [[decoherence_theory]]). The scientific question is therefore whether any coherence that survives is used by the organism, and not merely whether it exists.

## Candidate phenomena
- **Energy transfer in photosynthesis.** Engel et al. (2007) measured oscillating signals in two-dimensional electronic spectroscopy of a light-harvesting complex and interpreted them as wavelike energy transfer through quantum coherence. Whether such coherence persists under natural conditions, and how much it improves transfer efficiency, has since been debated.
- **Magnetoreception.** The radical-pair hypothesis proposes that magnetic fields change the outcome of reactions in which pairs of molecules carry correlated electron spins, which could let birds sense the direction of the geomagnetic field (see [[magnetic_sense]]). It is a leading hypothesis and is not settled.
- **Enzyme catalysis.** Quantum tunnelling of hydrogen has been proposed to contribute to the rates of some enzymatic reactions (see [[enzyme_kinetics]]).
- **Other proposals,** such as a role for molecular vibrations in olfaction, are more contested.

## Assessment
Marais et al. (2018) outline the current state of the field and directions for its future, and the field is best described as having suggestive cases and open arguments. Where an effect is claimed, the useful test is a functional one: does removing or disrupting the quantum feature change the biological performance, and does a classical model with the same parameters fail to account for the data?

## Relation to Active Inference
No established connection exists. Active Inference describes organisms with classical probabilistic models of states and observations, and the free energy principle does not require quantum effects in biological tissue. The quantum formulation of the principle by Fields, Friston, Glazebrook and Levin (2022) concerns the formal generality of the framework and not a claim that cells exploit coherence. Quantum biology is also separate from quantum cognition, which uses quantum probability as a modelling language for judgement without positing quantum states in the brain (see [[quantum_cognition]]).
