# Restoration Ecology

## Overview
Restoration ecology is the scientific basis for assisting the recovery of ecosystems that have been degraded, damaged or destroyed. Restoration practice is the corresponding activity; the discipline supplies tests of ecological theory, since restoring a system requires knowing how it assembles and what limits recovery. It overlaps with [[conservation_biology]] but concentrates on repairing what has been lost rather than protecting what remains.

## Concepts and methods
Restoration targets are usually defined against a reference ecosystem: a similar intact site or a historical description used to set composition, structure and function goals. Because climate and species pools change, many practitioners treat the reference as a range of acceptable trajectories rather than a fixed state. Interventions range from passive restoration (removing the cause of degradation and letting succession proceed) to active restoration (reseeding, replanting, soil amendment, hydrological repair, removing [[invasive_species]], reintroducing species).

Theory emphasizes thresholds and alternative stable states. A degraded system may be held in a persistent state by positive feedbacks, so that removing the original stressor does not restore the original community. Suding, Gross and Houseman argued that such feedbacks and hysteresis should shape restoration planning, with intervention needed to push a system across a threshold. Community assembly theory ([[community_ecology]]) adds that arrival order and dispersal limitation affect outcomes, a reason that restoration trajectories can diverge on similar sites.

Monitoring uses indicators of vegetation, soils and fauna. Ant assemblages have been used as bioindicators in mine-site rehabilitation in Australia, because ants are abundant, taxonomically tractable and respond to vegetation structure and soil change ([[conservation_biology_social_insects]], [[ecological_impacts_social_insects]]). Success depends on scale: restoring fragmented landscapes needs connectivity as well as patch quality ([[habitat_fragmentation]]), and benefits are often assessed in terms of [[biodiversity]] and [[ecosystem_services]] recovery.

## Role in Active Inference
Restoration is naturally described as adaptive management: a loop in which managers act, observe the system and update their model. Active Inference offers a formalization of such act-observe-update cycles under uncertainty, and has been applied conceptually to stewardship in [[bioregional_stewardship_theory]] and [[adaptive_comanagement]]. This is an application of the framework as a decision model, not a claim that ecosystems themselves perform inference.
