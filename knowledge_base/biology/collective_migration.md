# Collective Migration

## Overview
In collective migration, cells move together as a cohesive group, a sheet, strand or cluster, while maintaining cell-cell junctions. This differs from the independent movement of single cells. Examples include the sheet of epithelial cells closing a skin wound, the neural crest cells that disperse through the embryo, the lateral line primordium in zebrafish, border cells in the fruit fly ovary, and strands of invading tumour cells.

## Principles
Several features recur across systems:
- **Leaders and followers**: cells at the front often have distinct protrusive behaviour and sense external cues, while followers are guided by contact with neighbours.
- **Mechanical coupling**: junctions transmit force, so cells coordinate their direction of movement and a group can span distances far larger than the range any single cell senses.
- **Chemical guidance**: cells follow gradients of chemoattractants, and a group can generate its own gradient by consuming or degrading a signal, which lets it carry on migrating through tissue.
- **Contact inhibition of locomotion**: collisions between cells cause them to retract protrusions and repolarise, which can drive dispersal or, at group boundaries, polarise a cluster.

The result is a form of self-organisation in which group-level behaviour depends on local interactions. Physical models of active matter and flocking, such as the Vicsek model, are used to describe the emergence of collective motion in both cell sheets and animal groups.

## Relation to Active Inference
Swarm-intelligence pages in the vault list collective cell migration as one example of group coordination without central control. The Free Energy Principle has been extended to describe nested systems, in which groups can be treated as having their own Markov blanket, and cell collectives are a natural example. The formal statement of this idea is a proposal in the theoretical literature; empirical work on collective migration relies on cell biology and physics, not on Active Inference models.
