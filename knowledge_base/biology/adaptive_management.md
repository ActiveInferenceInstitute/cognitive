# Adaptive Management

## Overview
Adaptive management is a framework for deciding and acting when the behaviour of an ecological system is poorly understood. Rather than waiting for certainty, managers treat each intervention as a test of a hypothesis about how the system responds, monitor the result, and change the next decision accordingly. The approach was developed in the 1970s and 1980s by C. S. Holling, Carl Walters and colleagues, and it is now widely used in fisheries, forestry, wildlife and water management.

## The adaptive cycle
The usual cycle has several linked steps:
- **Problem framing**: stakeholders state objectives and bound the system.
- **Models of the system**: competing hypotheses about how it responds are written down explicitly, often as quantitative models.
- **Action**: a management policy is chosen, sometimes deliberately to discriminate between hypotheses.
- **Monitoring**: data are collected that bear on the hypotheses and on the objectives.
- **Learning and revision**: confidence in each hypothesis is updated, and the policy is revised.

Walters distinguished passive adaptive management, which acts on the currently best model and learns as a by-product, from active adaptive management, which accepts some short-term cost to run informative experiments. Because the trade-off between immediate performance and information is central, the problem is commonly framed as sequential decision making under partial observability, and Bayesian updating of model weights is a standard tool.

## Limitations
Adaptive management requires institutions able to monitor for long periods and to change course. Critics have noted that many projects labelled adaptive never complete the learning step, that experiments on large ecosystems are hard to replicate, and that power, cost and social acceptability can limit what can be tried.

## Relation to Active Inference
The structure of the problem matches the one that Active Inference addresses: an agent with several candidate models chooses actions that balance expected reward against expected information gain. The expected free energy decomposition into pragmatic and epistemic value mirrors the passive versus active distinction. This is a structural correspondence rather than an established research programme, since the standard adaptive management literature uses decision-theoretic methods, not free energy minimisation.
