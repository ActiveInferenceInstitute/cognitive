# Regulating Services

## Overview
Regulating services are the benefits people obtain from the regulation of ecosystem processes. The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005) grouped ecosystem services into provisioning, regulating, cultural and supporting categories, and placed within the regulating group the control of climate, air quality, water flow and quality, erosion, pollination, pests and disease, and natural hazards. Unlike food or timber, these services are seldom traded in markets, so their value tends to be noticed only when they decline.

## Examples
- **Climate regulation.** Vegetation and soils store carbon and exchange water and energy with the atmosphere (see [[soil_carbon]]).
- **Water regulation and purification.** Wetlands and forest cover moderate runoff, reduce flood peaks and filter pollutants.
- **Erosion control.** Root systems and ground cover hold soil in place.
- **Biological control and pollination.** Predators, parasitoids and pollinators supply services that agriculture depends on.
- **Hazard moderation.** Coastal vegetation and reefs reduce wave energy.

Costanza et al. (1997) and Daily (1997) are standard references for the case that such functions carry large economic importance, although attempts to put a single monetary total on global services have been criticised on methodological grounds.

## Measurement and trade-offs
Regulating services depend on underlying [[ecosystem_processes]] and on the diversity of organisms that perform them. A recurring finding in the assessment literature is that managing an ecosystem to maximise one service, typically provisioning, can reduce regulating services. Their supply also depends on spatial scale: a wetland regulates floods for the downstream catchment rather than for the site itself.

## Relation to Active Inference
The vault uses regulating services as state variables in [[bioregional_state_space]], where they describe conditions that a stewardship agent tries to keep within viable bounds. That use is a modelling choice made within the BioFirm framework. It is an application of state-space description to environmental management rather than a result of Active Inference. The notion of regulation is also close to homeostasis (see [[homeostatic_regulation]]), but the analogy is loose.
