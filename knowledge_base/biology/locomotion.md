# Locomotion

## Overview
Locomotion is self-propelled movement of an organism from one place to another, by walking, running, swimming, flying or crawling. It requires coordinated rhythmic muscle activity, continual adjustment to terrain and body state, and a link between movement and the sensory consequences it produces, which is why it is a recurring example in work on [[motor_control]] and [[embodied_cognition]].

## Mechanism
Rhythmic limb movement is generated largely by central pattern generators (CPGs), neural circuits in the spinal cord of vertebrates and in thoracic ganglia of insects that produce alternating flexor and extensor activity without patterned input. T. Graham Brown's half-center proposal, in which two mutually inhibiting populations alternate, remains the conceptual template. CPGs are not autonomous in the intact animal: descending signals from brainstem and motor cortex start, stop and steer gait, and sensory feedback from muscle spindles, load receptors and the skin ([[proprioception]]) adjusts the timing of phase transitions and corrects perturbations.

Gait is the pattern of limb coordination. Quadrupeds shift between walk, trot and gallop as speed increases. Insects with six legs commonly use a tripod gait at higher speeds, with two sets of three legs alternating, while ants and other species switch to slower, more statically stable patterns when carrying loads or on uneven ground. Flight, covered in [[insect_flight_dynamics]], depends on a different class of rhythm generator and on fast mechanosensory control.

Developmentally, locomotion is a milestone in humans, with independent crawling and walking emerging during the first one to two years. A line of developmental research argues that self-produced locomotion changes spatial cognition, social interaction and responses to heights, although the size and generality of these effects are debated ([[cognitive_development]]).

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference gives a specific account of locomotion: descending motor signals are treated not as commands but as predictions of proprioceptive input, which classical reflex arcs then fulfil by moving the limbs (Adams, Shipp and Friston 2013). Under this scheme, movement is the minimization of proprioceptive prediction error through action, with attenuation of sensory precision during movement explaining why self-generated sensations are suppressed ([[precision_weighting]]). The scheme is a computational hypothesis consistent with motor-system anatomy; many details await empirical test.
