# Functional Diversity

## Overview
Functional diversity describes the range and distribution of functional traits among the organisms in a community, where a trait is any measurable characteristic that influences performance or the organism's effect on its environment, such as body size, leaf nitrogen content, diet or dispersal mode. It complements taxonomic measures such as [[species_richness]], because species can be many and yet similar in what they do, or few and very different.

## Measurement
Many indices exist, and they capture different aspects of a community's trait distribution. Early approaches summed branch lengths in a dendrogram built from trait differences (Petchey and Gaston, 2002). Villéger and colleagues defined three complementary indices in a multidimensional trait space: functional richness, the volume of trait space filled; functional evenness, the regularity of abundance distribution across that space; and functional divergence, the degree to which abundance is concentrated toward the edges of the space. Rao's quadratic entropy and distance-based indices (Laliberté and Legendre, 2010) can combine several traits and incorporate abundance. The results depend on which traits are selected and how they are weighted, so comparisons require a stated trait set.

## Ecological significance
Experiments and field studies indicate that ecosystem processes such as productivity and nutrient cycling often depend more on the functional traits present and their abundance than on species number as such (Tilman et al., 1997; Díaz and Cabido, 2001). Functional redundancy, in which several species perform similar roles, may buffer a function against loss of species, while response diversity, differences in how species respond to disturbance, supports stability. Loss of functionally unique species can have disproportionate effects.
