# Genomics

## Overview
Genomics studies the complete set of DNA of an organism, its genome, and its organisation, function and evolution. It differs from classical genetics in considering many or all genes together rather than single genes, and it relies on large-scale sequencing and computation. Sub-fields include structural genomics, functional genomics, comparative genomics, population genomics and metagenomics.

## Methods
- **Sequencing**: the chain-termination method of Sanger and colleagues (1977) was the basis of the first genome projects, and the first complete genome of a free-living organism, the bacterium Haemophilus influenzae, was published in 1995 (Fleischmann et al.). High-throughput sequencing technologies now allow many genomes to be sequenced cheaply, with long-read methods improving the completeness of assemblies.
- **Assembly and annotation**: reads are assembled into contiguous sequences, and genes, regulatory elements and repeats are annotated using computational prediction and experimental data such as RNA sequencing.
- **Comparative genomics**: alignment across species identifies conserved elements, gene families and rearrangements, and estimates evolutionary relationships.
- **Population genomics**: genome-wide variation in many individuals is used to study demography, selection and structure of populations (see [[insect_population_genetics]]).

The draft sequences of the human genome were reported by the public consortium (Lander et al., 2001) and by a private effort (Venter et al., 2001).

## Findings and limits
Genomes revealed that gene number does not track organismal complexity in a simple way and that much of the genome outside protein-coding exons has regulatory or other roles, and the functional interpretation of non-coding sequence is an active area. A sequence alone does not specify phenotype, because gene expression, epigenetic state and environment contribute.

## Relation to Active Inference
Genomics is not part of the Active Inference framework. A theoretical proposal treats natural selection as Bayesian model selection, and, in a related reading, the genome as encoding priors that were tuned over evolutionary time. These are interpretive and are not tested by sequence analysis.
