# Termite Mounds

## Overview
Termite mounds are above-ground nest structures built by workers of many termite species from soil, saliva and faeces. Those of some African and Australian species stand several metres high, far taller than the body of a single builder. No individual holds a plan of the structure; the mound is the visible product of thousands of workers responding to local cues in the material they encounter.

## Architecture and function
A mound shelters the colony, the brood and, in fungus-growing species of the subfamily Macrotermitinae, the fungus gardens on which the colony feeds. Mounds contain galleries, chambers and networks of channels that exchange air with the surroundings. Gas exchange is passive and depends on the porous walls, temperature differences within the structure and wind. The nest therefore buffers the colony against fluctuations in temperature and humidity, and is often described as an external organ of the colony, an instance of an animal-built structure with physiological functions (Turner, 2000). Structure and shape vary strongly among species, and some termites build little or nothing above ground, so the mound is a trait of particular lineages rather than of termites in general.

## Stigmergic construction
The pioneering account is Grassé (1959), who studied nest reconstruction in *Bellicositermes natalensis* and introduced the term stigmergy. A worker deposits a soil pellet impregnated with a pheromone. The deposit makes that location more attractive to other workers, who add further pellets, and pillars and arches emerge when growing columns come close enough to join. The structure acts as a shared memory: the state of the work stimulates the next action, so coordination needs no direct communication between builders. Models of this process rely on positive feedback from pellet deposition, a decay of the pheromone signal, and the randomness of worker movement (see [[stigmergic_coordination]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is an analogy and a modelling opportunity rather than a derivation. Building behaviour can be framed as agents whose actions change the shared environment that supplies their sensory input, which is a form of [[niche_construction]]. Whether termites are best modelled as individual inference agents or as parts of a colony-level system remains open (see [[active_inference_social_insects]]).
