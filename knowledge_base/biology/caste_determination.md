# Caste Determination

## Overview
In eusocial insects such as ants, termites, social bees and wasps, a colony contains individuals that differ in morphology, physiology and behaviour, and reproduction is concentrated in a few of them. These classes are called castes. The basic division is between reproductive females (queens, or gynes before they found a colony) and workers. In many ant species the workers are further divided into subcastes, for example minor and major workers, which differ in size and form and in the tasks they perform. Caste determination is the question of what makes a given larva develop into one caste and not another. Hölldobler and Wilson (1990) describe the variety of caste systems in ants.

## The classical view: a developmental switch
For a long time caste was explained mainly as a developmental switch. Females of a species share a broadly similar genome, and environmental cues received during larval development push them down one pathway or another, an example of **polyphenism**. Cues include the quality and quantity of larval food, hormone levels (notably juvenile hormone), temperature and season, and the presence of the queen or other colony members. Wheeler (1986) reviewed the developmental and physiological determinants of caste in social Hymenoptera and the evolutionary implications of such mechanisms.

## Genetic contributions
Evidence from several taxa has shown that genotype also influences caste. Schwander and colleagues (2010) concluded that the view of caste as solely an environmentally controlled switch no longer holds, and that the relative importance of genes and environment varies among species, from largely environmental to almost purely genetic.
- **Genetic caste determination in harvester ants.** In hybridizing lineages of *Pogonomyrmex* harvester ants, diploid offspring produced from gametes of the same lineage develop only into queens, whereas offspring from gametes of distinct lineages develop into workers (Julian et al., 2002; Cahan et al., 2006).
- **Mixed control in the Florida harvester ant.** In *Pogonomyrmex badius*, which has three female castes, larval diet differed between castes, colony size and resources affected the size of minor workers only, and about 19 percent of patrilines were biased in which caste they produced. Smith and colleagues (2008) concluded that environmental, social and genetic variation interact, and that the importance of each depends on the caste.

## Latent developmental potential
Rajakumar and colleagues (2012) found that ants of the genus *Pheidole* retain an ancestral developmental potential to produce a "supersoldier" subcaste that can be induced by environmental and hormonal manipulation, and that this potential has been realised at least twice independently. Their interpretation is that the retained potential helped novel subcastes evolve repeatedly.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is an interpretive analogy. A developing larva effectively takes cues from its environment and develops accordingly, and one could describe this as a form of inference about the colony's needs, which is the sort of reading explored in [[active_inference_social_insects]]. The mechanisms above are, however, developmental and genetic processes, and no study cited here uses the Active Inference framework to explain them.
