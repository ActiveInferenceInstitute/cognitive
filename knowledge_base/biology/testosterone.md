# Testosterone

## Overview
Testosterone is a steroid hormone of the androgen class. It is made mainly by Leydig cells of the testes, with smaller amounts from the ovaries and adrenal glands, and its secretion is controlled by the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis: gonadotropin-releasing hormone stimulates pituitary release of luteinising hormone, which drives testicular production, and testosterone feeds back to restrain the axis. Levels are higher in males than females, vary over the day and with age, and respond to context.

## Mechanism
Testosterone acts directly on the androgen receptor, a nuclear receptor that regulates transcription. It can also be converted to dihydrotestosterone, a more potent androgen, or by aromatase to estradiol (see [[estrogen]]), so some of its effects in the brain are mediated by estrogen receptors. Developmental exposure organises neural circuits, and circulating hormone later modulates their activity.

## Dominance and status
Across vertebrates, testosterone is associated with reproductive competition. The challenge hypothesis proposes that in seasonally breeding birds testosterone rises above a baseline level mainly when males compete over mates or territory (Wingfield et al., 1990). In humans, the relation to dominance is weaker and depends on context. Mazur and Booth (1998) reviewed evidence that testosterone and dominance behaviour influence one another in men, with levels changing after competitive wins and losses. Experimental administration studies suggest the hormone may promote behaviour aimed at status more than aggression as such, although results differ across studies and tasks (Eisenegger et al., 2011). Popular equations of testosterone with aggression are therefore not supported by the evidence.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is indirect and speculative. If neuromodulatory systems encode the precision of predictions, hormones that modulate them, together with stress hormones (see [[cortisol]]), could influence how an agent weights social evidence or prior expectations about rank. No established formal model of testosterone in Active Inference exists.
