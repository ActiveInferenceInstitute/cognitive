# Biofilm Formation

## Overview
A biofilm is a community of microorganisms attached to a surface or to each other and embedded in a matrix of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) that the cells themselves produce. The matrix contains polysaccharides, proteins and extracellular DNA. Biofilms occur on rocks, plant roots, teeth, medical devices and tissue, and the biofilm mode of life appears to be the usual state of many bacteria in nature.

## Stages
Formation is commonly described as a cycle:
- **Reversible attachment** of free-living (planktonic) cells to a surface.
- **Irreversible attachment**, often involving appendages such as pili and the first matrix production.
- **Microcolony formation** by growth and aggregation.
- **Maturation** into a structured community with channels through which fluids move.
- **Dispersal**, in which cells leave to colonise new sites.

Cells within a biofilm differ in gene expression, partly because gradients of oxygen and nutrients form across the structure. Biofilm cells are typically much more tolerant of antibiotics and host defences than planktonic cells, which is why biofilms matter in chronic infection. The reasons include matrix barriers and physiologically slow-growing subpopulations.

## Coordination
Many species regulate biofilm development by cell-to-cell signalling, including [[quorum_sensing]], in which diffusible molecules let cells respond to local population density. The result is a structure built without a central plan: each cell responds to local chemical and physical conditions, and community form arises from those responses. The collective-construction analogy to nest building by social insects is therefore common in the swarm literature.

## Relation to Active Inference
The vault's [[swarm_intelligence]] page cites biofilms as an example of decentralised construction. A multi-agent Active Inference reading would treat each cell as an agent with a generative model of local chemistry, and the biofilm as a shared niche that agents modify. That is a modelling proposal consistent with the Free Energy Principle's treatment of cells, not an established account of biofilm biology.
