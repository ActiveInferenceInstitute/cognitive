# Speciation

## Overview
Speciation is the evolutionary process by which populations diverge until they form distinct species. What counts as a species depends on the concept used. Under the biological species concept of Mayr (1942), species are groups of interbreeding natural populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups. Other concepts use phylogenetic history, ecological niche or morphology, and the choice matters for organisms that reproduce asexually or hybridise often.

## Modes of speciation
- **Allopatric.** Populations separated by a geographic barrier diverge through [[natural_selection]], [[genetic_drift]] and mutation, and later cannot interbreed. It is regarded as the most common mode.
- **Peripatric.** A small population at the edge of a range diverges rapidly, with drift and founder effects playing a larger role.
- **Parapatric.** Populations in adjacent habitats diverge despite limited gene flow across a boundary.
- **Sympatric.** Divergence occurs without geographic separation, for example through ecological specialisation or sexual selection. It is harder to demonstrate and is generally accepted for some cases, including host races of insects and polyploidy in plants.

## Reproductive isolation
Barriers are classed as prezygotic, such as differences in habitat, timing, mating signals or gamete compatibility, and postzygotic, such as hybrid inviability or sterility. The Dobzhansky-Muller model explains how genetic incompatibilities arise without any intermediate unfit population: two isolated lineages each accumulate different substitutions that work in their own backgrounds but fail together in hybrids (Dobzhansky, 1937; Coyne & Orr, 2004). Where hybrids are less fit, selection may strengthen prezygotic isolation, a process called reinforcement. Speciation is usually gradual and can be incomplete, as shown by ring species and ongoing hybridisation.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct link. Speciation is treated within population genetics and evolutionary biology (see [[population_genetics]]). The only contact is the general programme of describing evolutionary change in terms compatible with the free energy principle, which is discussed under [[natural_selection]] and does not offer specific predictions about species formation.
