# Succession Theory

## Overview
Ecological succession is the sequence of changes in species composition and community structure that follows a disturbance or the creation of new habitat. Primary succession begins on substrates without soil, such as new volcanic rock or exposed glacial till, and secondary succession begins on sites where soil and some biota remain, such as abandoned fields or burned forest. Succession theory seeks to explain the pattern and the causes of this change.

## Classical positions
Clements (1916) treated the plant community as a quasi-organism that develops through predictable seral stages toward a regional climax determined by climate. Gleason (1926) disputed this, arguing that communities are the outcome of individual species' responses to the environment and chance, so that no integrated unit develops. Debate between the two views shaped twentieth-century ecology, and most present accounts retain parts of each: some trajectories are predictable, but many are contingent on history, dispersal and local conditions.

## Mechanisms and development
Connell and Slatyer (1977) distinguished three models of how early species affect later ones: facilitation, in which early species make the environment more suitable for later ones; tolerance, in which later species succeed because they can persist at lower resource levels; and inhibition, in which residents suppress newcomers until they are damaged or die. Empirical studies have found all three, depending on system and stage. Odum (1969) proposed trends expected over succession, such as growing biomass and more complex organic structure, though the generality of these trends has been questioned. Contemporary work stresses alternative stable states, priority effects and the role of repeated disturbance, so that the notion of a single stable climax is regarded as an idealisation (see [[ecosystem_stability]]).
