# Plant Intelligence

## Overview
Plant intelligence is the proposal that plants should be described as cognitive or intelligent organisms because they sense their surroundings, integrate many cues and adjust growth and physiology in ways that serve the individual over its lifetime. The term is contested. Supporters treat intelligence as adaptively variable behaviour in the face of environmental challenge, a definition that does not require a nervous system. Critics hold that the label stretches the word beyond its useful meaning and that plant responses are better described by established physiology.

## Established facts
Plants detect light quality and direction, gravity, touch, water and nutrient gradients, temperature and chemical signals from herbivores, pathogens and neighbours (Taiz et al., 2015). Signalling relies on hormones such as auxin, on calcium waves, on electrical signals including action potentials in some species, and on volatile compounds. Roots grow towards resources and away from hazards, and shoots adjust to shading by neighbours. None of this is disputed. The dispute concerns how to interpret it.

## Positions
- **Pro.** Trewavas (2005) argued that coordinated, plastic, goal-sensitive growth amounts to intelligent behaviour, and Calvo et al. (2020) extended the case to memory, anticipation and learning. The most cited learning reports are habituation of leaf closing in the sensitive plant *Mimosa pudica* (Gagliano et al., 2014) and associative learning in pea seedlings, in which an air flow from a fan was paired with a light source and seedlings later grew towards the side the air flow came from (Gagliano et al., 2016). Defenders also reply that "plant neurobiology" is a useful metaphor for signalling, not a claim that plants have brains (Trewavas, 2007).
- **Contra.** Alpi et al. (2007) objected to "plant neurobiology" because plants lack neurons, synapses and a brain, and Taiz et al. (2019) argued that plants neither possess nor require consciousness. A common objection is that adaptive responses of this kind are explained by feedback in signalling networks without a cognitive vocabulary. The learning evidence has also been challenged: Markel (2020) repeated the pea-seedling experiment under several conditions and found no evidence of associative learning, and Gagliano and colleagues published a comment disputing that conclusion in the same journal. The sensitive-plant and pea reports therefore remain unsettled, and the two camps disagree about both the data and the definition of intelligence.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is a conceptual proposal rather than a derivation. Calvo and Friston (2017) suggested that plant behaviour can be read as predictive processing, with sensory signals compared against expectations and growth acting to reduce discrepancies. This extends the free-energy framing to any self-maintaining system, which is consistent with the vault's treatment of [[cellular_intelligence]], but it does not by itself show that plants are cognitive, and the formal models needed to test it remain largely undeveloped.
