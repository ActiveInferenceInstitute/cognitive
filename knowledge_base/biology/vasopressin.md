# Vasopressin

## Overview
Vasopressin, also called arginine vasopressin (AVP) or antidiuretic hormone, is a nine-amino-acid peptide synthesised mainly by neurons in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus. Many of these neurons project to the posterior pituitary, from which the peptide is released into the blood. Other neurons release it within the brain, where it acts as a neuromodulator. It is structurally close to oxytocin: the two peptides differ at only two of nine positions (see [[oxytocin]]).

## Peripheral physiology
In the body, vasopressin is released in response to rising plasma osmolality or falling blood volume and pressure. By acting on V2 receptors in the collecting ducts of the kidney it increases water reabsorption and concentrates urine, and through V1a receptors on vascular smooth muscle it causes vasoconstriction. V1b receptors in the anterior pituitary contribute to the stress axis by enhancing release of adrenocorticotropic hormone. It is thus a component of homeostatic regulation of fluid balance and cardiovascular function (see [[homeostasis]]). Failure of secretion produces diabetes insipidus.

## Central and social effects
In rodents, vasopressin acting through V1a receptors influences scent marking, territorial and aggressive behaviour, anxiety-related behaviour and social recognition, often with sex differences. The strongest case comes from voles. In the monogamous prairie vole, blocking vasopressin receptors reduced pair bonding in males, and differences in V1a receptor distribution between monogamous and non-monogamous species were associated with differences in social behaviour (Winslow et al., 1993; Young & Wang, 2004). Insel (2010) reviews these findings alongside the difficulties of translating them to humans. In humans, intranasal administration and genetic association studies have reported effects on social perception, emotional responses and aggression, but results are variable, sample sizes are often small, and it is uncertain how much administered peptide reaches the brain. The evidence therefore remains preliminary (see [[social_neuroscience]]).

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no established formal link. In an informal reading, peptides such as vasopressin and oxytocin might modulate the precision assigned to social cues or to threat-related predictions, in line with proposals that neuromodulators encode precision. This is speculation that has not been tested in computational models of these hormones, and the role in water balance is the better understood aspect of the system.
