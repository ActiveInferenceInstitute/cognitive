# Invasive Species

## Overview
An invasive species is a non-native organism that establishes, spreads, and causes ecological or economic harm in its new range. Invasion biology studies why some introductions fail while others transform communities, and it is one of the major drivers of biodiversity loss alongside habitat destruction and climate change.

## Mechanism
Blackburn and colleagues (2011) describe invasion as a sequence of stages: transport, introduction, establishment, and spread, with each stage acting as a filter. The probability of success is strongly related to propagule pressure, the number of individuals released and the number of release events. Several hypotheses address what happens after arrival and are not mutually exclusive: the enemy release hypothesis (the invader escapes specialist predators and parasites), the novel weapons hypothesis (the invader produces chemicals to which native species are naive), and biotic resistance (diverse native communities resist invaders). Elton's 1958 book The Ecology of Invasions by Animals and Plants is the usual starting point for the field.

Social insects are prominent invaders. The Argentine ant Linepithema humile and the red imported fire ant Solenopsis invicta have spread widely through human transport. In its introduced range the Argentine ant forms very large unicolonial supercolonies in which workers from distant nests do not fight, removing the intraspecific aggression that limits native colony density; this lets the species displace native ants. Other well-known cases include the cane toad in Australia and the zebra mussel in North American freshwater systems.

## Role in Active Inference
This page has only a loose connection to Active Inference. A native community can be viewed as a set of coupled agents whose joint dynamics reach a quasi-stable regime, and an invader as a perturbation that changes the statistics of the environment each agent models; resilience then corresponds to the capacity of agents to update beliefs and re-establish viable behavior. This is a general analogy, not a quantitative framework. In this repository, the more concrete link is through the Ant_Colony Thing, which treats colony-level foraging and could be used to ask how a competitor with a different recruitment rule alters resource use.
