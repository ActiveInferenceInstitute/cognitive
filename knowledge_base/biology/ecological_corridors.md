# Ecological Corridors

## Overview
An ecological corridor is a linear or networked area of habitat, or of landscape that permits movement, linking habitat patches that would otherwise be isolated. Corridors are used in conservation planning to counteract [[habitat_fragmentation]], by letting animals disperse, plants spread seeds, and populations exchange individuals and genes. Examples include riparian strips, hedgerows, wildlife crossings over roads, and continental-scale connectivity networks.

## Connectivity concepts
Structural connectivity describes the physical arrangement of habitat in a landscape. Functional connectivity describes how readily organisms of a particular species actually move through it, which depends on behaviour and on the surrounding matrix. Taylor and colleagues emphasised that connectivity is a property of landscape structure as perceived by organisms. A corridor that serves one species may be a barrier or a hazard for another. Stepping stones, which are small patches separated by gaps, can function as corridors for mobile species. Metapopulation theory predicts that connectivity can reduce local extinction risk by enabling recolonisation, and that genetic exchange can limit inbreeding in small populations.

## Evidence and debate
Reviews have found that corridors generally increase movement between patches, though evidence for effects on long-term population persistence is harder to obtain. Beier and Noss reviewed the empirical record, concluding that corridors can provide connectivity, while noting that few studies tested demographic outcomes. Concerns raised include the possible spread of invasive species, disease or fire, edge effects in narrow corridors, and the opportunity cost of land. Practitioners therefore recommend that corridor design be species-specific and based on movement data.
