# Fungal Intelligence

## Overview
Fungal intelligence is a contested label for adaptive, problem-solving behaviour in fungi. Most fungi grow as mycelium, a branching network of filaments (hyphae) without a brain or nervous system. The network explores its surroundings, allocates resources, and responds to food, damage and competitors. Whether this deserves the term intelligence depends on the definition used, and researchers disagree.

## Observations
- **Foraging and network form**: mycelia extend toward resources, thicken productive paths and prune others, and transport nutrients and water through the network.
- **Plant interactions**: mycorrhizal fungi form associations with plant roots, and Simard and colleagues (1997) reported, from isotope-labelling experiments in the field, a net transfer of carbon between ectomycorrhizal tree species (paper birch and Douglas-fir), and interpreted it as possibly passing through shared fungal links. The extent and ecological significance of such common mycorrhizal networks remain under active debate, and Karst and colleagues (2023) argued that positive citation bias and over-interpretation have led to claims that outrun the evidence, for example about resource sharing among trees.
- **Electrical activity**: some fungi show spontaneous electrical spiking. Its function is unknown, and reading it as signalling or information processing is speculative.
- **Comparison with slime moulds**: the maze-solving and network-design experiments with the slime mould Physarum are often cited together with fungi, but slime moulds are not fungi. Experiments by Nakagaki and colleagues, and later by Tero and colleagues, found that Physarum networks reach efficient, fault-tolerant layouts through local adaptation of tube thickness.

## Interpretation
One view, called basal cognition, holds that adaptive information processing is widespread among living things and need not involve neurons. On it, foraging, resource allocation and integration of signals across a network are candidate cognitive-like capacities. The opposing view reserves terms such as intelligence or cognition for systems with specific capacities, such as representation, learning and memory, and regards the fungal case as sophisticated regulation without cognition. Money (2021), for example, argued against the concept of a "fungal mind" or mycelial consciousness. The evidence is therefore mostly of adaptive behaviour and network-level regulation, which both camps accept, while the dispute is over whether those results warrant the label intelligence. This page presents both positions without endorsing either.

## Relation to Active Inference
The Free Energy Principle applies in principle to any self-organising system with a boundary and is the basis of work on cognition in simple and non-neural organisms. No fungus-specific Active Inference model is established, and any such account would be a proposal about a system that has not been shown to maintain explicit generative models.
