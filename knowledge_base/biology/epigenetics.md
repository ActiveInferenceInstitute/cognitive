# Epigenetics

## Overview
Epigenetics studies stable changes in gene activity that do not alter the DNA sequence itself. Chemical marks and chromatin structure determine which genes are accessible for transcription, so cells with identical genomes can maintain distinct identities such as neuron or liver cell. Some marks are copied through cell division, which allows a cell lineage to remember its differentiated state.

## Mechanisms
- **DNA methylation.** In vertebrates, methyl groups are added mainly to cytosines in CG dinucleotides. Methylation at gene promoters is generally associated with reduced transcription, and maintenance enzymes copy the pattern to daughter strands after replication.
- **Histone modification.** Chemical groups such as acetyl and methyl groups on histone tails change how tightly DNA is packaged, and different combinations are associated with active or silenced regions.
- **Non-coding RNA.** Some RNAs guide chromatin-modifying complexes, as in X-chromosome inactivation, where one X chromosome in female mammals is silenced.
- **Genomic imprinting.** A small set of genes is expressed according to parental origin through differential marking.

## Environment and inheritance
Environmental conditions can influence epigenetic states, especially during development. In rats, variation in maternal care was associated with differences in methylation of a glucocorticoid receptor gene in offspring and with stress responses (Weaver et al., 2004). Whether acquired marks are inherited across generations in mammals is debated; reprogramming in germ cells erases most marks, and clear transgenerational cases are better established in plants and some invertebrates than in humans. Claims in the popular literature often go beyond the evidence, and Bird (2007) cautions against loose definitions of the term.

## Relation to Active Inference
The link is conceptual and weak. Epigenetic regulation is one mechanism by which an organism's development and physiology are tuned to its environment (see [[phenotypic_plasticity]]), which invites describing developmental programming as slow adaptation of priors over longer timescales. This is an analogy used in theoretical discussions of development, and it is not a worked-out result of the free energy principle. Early-life experience affecting later cognition is discussed under [[cognitive_development]].
