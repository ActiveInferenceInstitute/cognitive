# Social Immunity

## Overview
Social immunity refers to the cooperative defences by which members of a group reduce the arrival, establishment and spread of parasites and pathogens within the group. The term was developed for social insects by Cremer, Armitage and Schmid-Hempel (2007). It is needed as a concept because crowded, genetically related and closely interacting colonies offer favourable conditions for the spread of disease, yet many such colonies persist for years.

## Forms of defence
- **Prevention.** Nest sites and materials are chosen or treated to inhibit microbes. Honey bees collect plant resins, which they use in the nest as propolis, and some ants apply secretions with antimicrobial activity to themselves, to nestmates and to the nest.
- **Hygienic behaviour.** Workers groom contaminated nestmates, which removes spores before they germinate, and they remove dead nestmates or diseased brood from the nest.
- **Spatial organisation.** Foragers and nurses can show limited mixing, so that infection entering from outside reaches the brood and queen less readily.
- **Behavioural change of the infected.** Individuals that are sick sometimes leave the nest or reduce their contact with nestmates, an act that benefits the colony.

These measures work alongside the physiological immune responses of individuals. Cooperative defence has costs, because grooming and nest care take time and workers that handle pathogens risk infection themselves. Low-level exposure transmitted between nestmates may in some cases also strengthen resistance, an area where results differ among species.

## Relevance to Active Inference
The relationship is an analogy and an open modelling question. Social immunity is a case of collective inference about a threat, where individual detection of danger, recognition of nestmates and transmission of cues (see [[cuticular_hydrocarbons]]) combine to produce a colony-level response. No established Active Inference model of social immunity exists. Related proposals treat the immune system of a single organism as an inferential system (see [[adaptive_immunity]]).
