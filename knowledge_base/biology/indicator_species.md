# Indicator Species

## Overview
An indicator species is an organism whose status, such as presence, abundance or condition, is used to infer something that is difficult to measure directly. This may be an environmental condition, such as pollution or soil acidity, or the condition of other species and the wider ecosystem. Familiar examples are lichens that disappear where air pollution is high, and aquatic invertebrates whose composition reflects water quality. The term is distinct from keystone species, which have disproportionate effects on a community, and from umbrella or flagship species, chosen for conservation planning or public attention.

## Kinds of indicator
Indicators are commonly grouped by what they signal. Environmental indicators reflect abiotic conditions or change. Ecological indicators reflect the effects of a stressor on biota. Biodiversity indicators reflect the diversity of a wider set of taxa. McGeoch described a framework for selecting and testing bioindicators, including terrestrial insects, in which candidate indicators are evaluated against explicit objectives and statistical tests.

## Statistical identification
In community ecology, indicator species of a habitat or group of sites can be identified by an index that combines a species' specificity to the group and its fidelity within it. The indicator value method of Dufrêne and Legendre (1997) is one standard approach, and De Cáceres and Legendre (2009) compared several association indices and their significance tests.

## Limits
Landres, Verner and Thomas (1988) criticised the use of vertebrate indicators, noting that population trends of an indicator may not track those of other species and that the assumptions behind the relationship are often untested. A good indicator should have a demonstrated, preferably quantitative, link to the target variable, a practical way of being sampled, and known sensitivity. Responses can also vary from place to place.
