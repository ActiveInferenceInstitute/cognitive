# Second Messengers

## Overview
Second messengers are small, rapidly produced or released molecules and ions that carry a signal from a receptor at the cell surface to targets inside the cell. The extracellular ligand is the first messenger. Receptors such as G protein-coupled receptors and receptor tyrosine kinases, once activated, change the concentration of a second messenger, which then regulates enzymes, channels and transcription factors (Alberts et al., 2015).

## Principal messengers
- **Cyclic AMP.** Made from ATP by adenylyl cyclase and broken down by phosphodiesterases; it chiefly activates protein kinase A (see [[camp_pathway]]).
- **Calcium.** Cytosolic concentration is kept low at rest and rises through entry across the plasma membrane or release from the endoplasmic reticulum, then acts through binding proteins such as calmodulin (see [[calcium_signaling]], [[voltage_channels]]). Berridge and colleagues described its versatility in encoding signals through the timing and location of increases (Berridge et al., 2000).
- **Inositol trisphosphate and diacylglycerol.** Both are produced when phospholipase C cleaves a membrane lipid; the first releases calcium from internal stores and the second helps activate protein kinase C.
- **Cyclic GMP and nitric oxide.** Cyclic GMP is produced by guanylyl cyclases, some of which are activated by nitric oxide, a gas that diffuses between cells.

## Features of the mechanism
Second messengers allow amplification, since one active receptor can lead to many messenger molecules, and integration, since pathways converge on shared kinases and phosphatases. Their effects are terminated by degradation, sequestration or pumping out, which keeps responses brief and spatially restricted.

## Role in neurons and relation to Active Inference
In neurons, second messengers modulate excitability, [[transmitter_release]] and long-term changes in synaptic strength (see [[synaptic_plasticity]], [[neuromodulation]]). The Active Inference connection is indirect: neuromodulatory signals that act through these cascades are proposed in the free-energy literature as a possible substrate for encoding precision, but that mapping is a hypothesis and the cascade details are not part of the formal theory.
