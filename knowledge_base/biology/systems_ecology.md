# Systems Ecology

## Overview
Systems ecology studies ecosystems as organised wholes whose behaviour arises from interactions among biotic and abiotic components and from flows of energy, carbon and nutrients between them. It applies concepts from general systems theory, cybernetics and thermodynamics, and typically uses compartment models, feedback analysis and simulation. Its roots lie in Tansley's (1935) introduction of the ecosystem concept and Lindeman's (1942) trophic-dynamic account, in which energy transfer between trophic levels became the central currency.

## Core ideas
- **Compartments and fluxes.** An ecosystem is divided into pools such as producers, consumers, detritus and soil nutrients, joined by flows that can be represented by coupled differential equations (see [[differential_equations]]).
- **Energy and matter.** Energy flows through an ecosystem and is dissipated, while elements such as nitrogen and phosphorus cycle. Odum (1983) developed energy-based accounting and systems diagrams for these flows.
- **Feedback.** Positive and negative feedbacks regulate stocks, and delays and nonlinearity can produce oscillations, thresholds and alternative states (see [[critical_transitions]]).
- **Hierarchy and scale.** Processes operate at different rates and spatial extents, so analysis depends on choosing the appropriate level of description (see [[levels_of_organization]]).

## Criticisms and development
Early holistic claims, such as that ecosystems behave as self-regulating superorganisms or optimise a single quantity like power, have been contested, and many ecologists favour explanations built from the evolutionary interests of individual populations. Modern work blends systems-level accounting with population and community mechanisms, and with network analysis of food webs and nutrient flows. Large simulation models of ecosystems proved difficult to validate, which led to more targeted models.

## Relation to Active Inference
The connection is thematic. Both frameworks describe self-maintaining systems that exchange energy and information with their environment, and both use feedback and hierarchical organisation. Applications of the free-energy principle to ecosystems exist as theoretical proposals, but systems ecology is an independent empirical tradition and does not depend on them.
