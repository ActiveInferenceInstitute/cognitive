# Phenotypic Plasticity

## Overview
Phenotypic plasticity is the capacity of a single genotype to produce different phenotypes depending on the environment. The relationship between environment and phenotype for a genotype is called its reaction norm. Plasticity covers changes in morphology, physiology, behaviour and life history, and it occurs over development or within an individual's lifetime.

## Forms and examples
- **Developmental and reversible plasticity.** Some changes become fixed once development passes a stage, such as polyphenism in seasonal butterfly forms, while others are reversible, such as acclimation to temperature or altitude.
- **Induced defences.** Water fleas of the genus Daphnia develop protective helmets or spines when chemical cues from predators are present, a standard case of adaptive plasticity.
- **Caste determination.** In social insects, diet and other cues in larvae shift development into queen or worker forms from the same genome.
- **Non-adaptive plasticity.** Environmental effects such as stress-related stunting need not be beneficial, and distinguishing adaptive from incidental responses requires evidence about fitness in each environment.
- **Mechanisms.** Responses are mediated by signalling and by changes in [[gene_expression]], sometimes including [[epigenetics]] marks.

## Evolutionary significance
Plasticity can buffer populations against environmental change, can allow colonisation of new habitats, and may precede genetic change. West-Eberhard (2003) argued that environmentally induced variants can be a starting point for adaptive evolution through later genetic accommodation, a view that is influential and still debated. Plasticity is itself a trait that [[natural_selection]] can shape, as the form of the reaction norm is heritable and varies among genotypes, and it carries costs and limits (Pigliucci, 2001).

## Relation to Active Inference
The free energy principle treats organisms as systems that keep themselves within viable states by adapting to the environment, and plasticity at different timescales, from learning in neural systems (see [[neural_plasticity]]) to developmental tuning, is naturally described as updating the organism's implicit model.
