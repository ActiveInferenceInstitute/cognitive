# Community Ecology

## Overview
Community ecology studies assemblages of species that live together and interact in one place, asking what determines which species are present, how abundant they are, and how the assemblage changes through time. It sits between population ecology, which follows single species, and ecosystem ecology, which follows flows of energy and matter (see [[ecosystem_processes]]).

## Core claims and frameworks
Vellend's synthesis reduces community dynamics to four high-level processes: selection among species with differing fitness, drift in the abundances of ecologically equivalent species, dispersal into and out of the community, and speciation. Classical niche theory emphasizes selection: species coexist when they differ in resource use enough that each limits itself more than others, formalized in Lotka-Volterra competition models. Hubbell's neutral theory instead assumes functional equivalence and explains abundance patterns through drift and dispersal. Empirical communities usually show a mix, and the two views are better treated as ends of a continuum than as rivals.

Species interactions include competition, predation, parasitism, mutualism and facilitation. Their pattern of links defines food webs and interaction networks, analyzed in [[ecological_networks]]. Ecologists distinguish direct effects from indirect ones, such as trophic cascades, in which predators alter plant biomass by suppressing herbivores. Succession describes directional community change after disturbance, and disturbance regimes help explain why diversity peaks at intermediate levels of disturbance in some systems. Spatial structure matters through metacommunity theory, which couples local interactions with dispersal among patches ([[spatial_ecology]]).

Ant communities are a standard model: dominant species, thermal tolerance and competition structure assemblages, and ants are used as indicators of habitat condition ([[ecological_impacts_social_insects]]).

## Role in Active Inference
The link is a modelling opportunity, not an established derivation. Ecological communities can be represented as networks of coupled agents and environments, and the multi-agent and Markov-blanket formalisms of Active Inference have been proposed for such systems ([[ecological_active_inference]], [[multi_agent_active_inference]]). Community ecology itself rests on its own empirical and mathematical tradition.
