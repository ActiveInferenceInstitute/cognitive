# Myelination

## Overview
Myelination is the enclosure of axons in a lipid-rich insulating sheath. In the central nervous system the sheath is formed by oligodendrocytes, in the peripheral nervous system by Schwann cells. Myelin raises the speed of action-potential conduction and lowers its metabolic cost, and the progressive myelination of the brain tracks maturation of function.

## Mechanism and development
Myelin consists of many tightly wrapped layers of cell membrane, with proteins such as myelin basic protein and proteolipid protein ([[structural_proteins]]). It is interrupted at regular gaps, the nodes of Ranvier, where voltage-gated sodium channels are concentrated. The action potential regenerates only at the nodes and jumps between them, a process called saltatory conduction. Conduction velocity in unmyelinated axons is on the order of 1 m/s, whereas large myelinated fibres conduct at up to roughly 100 m/s, so a wrapped axon is both faster and requires less ion pumping per spike than an unwrapped axon of the same diameter.

Myelination begins before birth in sensory and motor pathways and continues for years in association cortex. Imaging and histological studies indicate that prefrontal and parietal connections continue to myelinate through adolescence and into the third decade, which is often cited alongside the extended maturation of executive functions ([[cognitive_development]], [[executive_functions]]). The timing is correlational evidence; causal contribution to specific cognitive changes is hard to isolate.

Myelination is also plastic. Studies in rodents show that neuronal activity and motor learning can promote oligodendrocyte differentiation and new myelin, and that blocking it can impair learning, so myelin counts as a component of [[neural_plasticity]] alongside synaptic change. Because conduction delays shape which inputs arrive together, myelin thickness can tune the timing relationships that underlie [[neural_synchrony]]. Demyelinating diseases, such as multiple sclerosis, illustrate the functional importance of the sheath by the slowed or blocked conduction that results.

## Role in Active Inference
The connection is indirect. Active Inference models of cortical message passing assume that prediction and prediction-error signals travel between hierarchical levels, and conduction delays are a physical constraint on those loops. Whether activity-dependent adjustment of myelination could implement part of precision or timing optimization is a speculative proposal; no established Active Inference derivation depends on it.
