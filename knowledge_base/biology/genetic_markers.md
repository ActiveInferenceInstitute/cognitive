# Genetic Markers

## Overview
A genetic marker is a variable DNA sequence at a known location in the genome that can be detected and inherited. Markers do not need to affect traits themselves. They serve as landmarks for mapping genes, as fingerprints for identifying individuals, and as measures of relatedness and population structure.

## Marker types
- **Restriction fragment length polymorphisms (RFLPs)**: differences in fragment length after digestion with restriction enzymes. Botstein and colleagues proposed their use to build linkage maps.
- **Minisatellites and DNA fingerprints**: highly variable repeat regions used by Jeffreys and colleagues to produce individual-specific patterns.
- **Microsatellites**: short tandem repeats, such as two to six base units, that are highly polymorphic and codominant, which made them a workhorse of population and kinship studies.
- **Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)**: single-base differences, abundant across the genome and scored at large scale with arrays or sequencing.
- **Mitochondrial DNA**: inherited maternally in most animals, with a high mutation rate, widely used in phylogeography and species identification.

Markers differ in polymorphism, mutation process, cost and the assumptions needed to analyse them. Schlötterer discussed how the choice of marker type has changed with technology.

## Applications
In ecology and evolution, markers are used to estimate paternity and kinship, gene flow among populations, effective population size, and hybridisation. In social insects, markers have been used to determine the number of queens in a colony, the number of males mating with a queen, and relatedness among workers, which tests predictions of kin selection. Microsatellites and SNPs have also been used to identify the colony of origin of individuals, for example in studies of ant populations. In medicine, markers are used in association studies and diagnostic testing.

## Relation to Active Inference
There is no direct relation to Active Inference. Genetic variation matters to the vault's themes in the evolutionary background: evolution is sometimes described as a process that shapes the priors of organisms over long timescales, and markers are tools for measuring the variation on which that process acts. The relation is a general conceptual one and not a specific model.
