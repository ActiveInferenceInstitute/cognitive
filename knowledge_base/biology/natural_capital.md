# Natural Capital

## Overview
Natural capital is the stock of natural assets, including soil, air, water, minerals and living things, from which flows of goods and services useful to people are derived. The term borrows from economics the idea of capital as a stock that yields a flow. Costanza and Daly (1992) discussed it in this sense and argued that sustainable development requires maintaining natural capital as well as manufactured capital. The term is used in ecological economics, conservation policy and corporate and national accounting.

## Main ideas
- **Stocks and flows**: forests, fisheries, soils and aquifers are stocks. Timber, fish, soil fertility and clean water are flows, and ecosystem services such as pollination and climate regulation are flows that depend on the condition of the stock (see [[ecosystem_services]]).
- **Renewable and non-renewable**: renewable capital can regenerate if not over-used, while non-renewable capital, such as fossil fuels and minerals, is depleted by use.
- **Substitutability**: a central disagreement is whether manufactured capital can substitute for natural capital. Weak sustainability assumes that it largely can, and strong sustainability holds that some natural capital is critical and cannot be replaced.
- **Valuation**: Costanza and colleagues (1997) estimated a monetary value of global ecosystem services, which drew both wide attention and criticism about methods and about whether monetary valuation is appropriate for nature. Others regard valuation as a useful way to make hidden costs visible, and still others favour non-monetary approaches.
- **Accounting**: the Dasgupta Review (2021) argued that economic analysis should treat nature as an asset that provides services, and that the economy is embedded in the biosphere.
