# Structural Plasticity

## Overview
Structural plasticity is change in the physical architecture of the nervous system in response to experience, as opposed to a change only in the strength of existing synapses. It includes formation and elimination of synapses, growth and retraction of dendritic spines and axonal branches, and, in limited regions, the birth of new neurons. It sits beside functional change in [[synaptic_plasticity]] under the umbrella of [[neural_plasticity]].

## Forms and evidence
Dendritic spines, the small protrusions that carry most excitatory synapses, are dynamic. Two-photon imaging in living mouse cortex shows that some spines persist for months while others appear and disappear within days, with turnover rates depending on region and age (Trachtenberg and colleagues, 2002). Learning a motor skill increases formation of new spines on specific branches of motor cortex neurons, and sensory experience and deprivation alter turnover (Yang, Pan and Gan, 2009). At the single-synapse level, Matsuzaki and colleagues showed with glutamate uncaging that stimulation protocols for potentiation enlarge individual spines, a structural counterpart of [[ltp]]. The cytoskeletal changes underlying this involve actin remodelling and scaffold proteins ([[structural_proteins]]).

Other forms include axonal sprouting after injury, remapping of cortical representations after limb loss or training, white-matter changes ([[myelination]]), and adult neurogenesis in the hippocampal dentate gyrus. Neurogenesis is well documented in rodents; whether it persists meaningfully in adult humans is disputed and findings differ between studies.

Structural change is slower than functional change, often hours to days, and it is metabolically costly, so it is thought to stabilize longer-lasting memory while functional changes act as the rapid first stage. Stability constraints come from [[homeostatic_plasticity]] and from consolidation ([[synaptic_consolidation]], [[stability_plasticity]]). Plasticity is highest in [[critical_periods]] and reduced, though not eliminated, afterward.

## Role in Active Inference
Active Inference distinguishes learning of parameters within a fixed model from structure learning, which adds or removes components of the model. Structural plasticity is the natural candidate biological correlate of the second kind. The formal tool is Bayesian model comparison and Bayesian model reduction, which has been proposed as an analogue of synaptic pruning during sleep ([[structure_learning]], [[friston_2017_curiosity]]). The mapping is a theoretical proposal; direct evidence that spine turnover implements model comparison is lacking.
